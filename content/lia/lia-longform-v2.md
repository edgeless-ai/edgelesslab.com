## Prologue

<a id="chapter-p"></a>

### P. The record we have already made

In April 1986 Don R. Swanson, of the Graduate Library School at the University of Chicago, published a paper in *The Library Quarterly* called "Undiscovered Public Knowledge." Most of it is an argument about libraries and search. In the middle of it he gave what he called a live example from the current scientific literature. [^1]

The example had two halves. The first was a set of clinical reports on dietary fish oil. Those reports said that fish oil, and in particular the eicosapentaenoic acid in it, changes the blood: platelets clump less readily, and the blood becomes less viscous. The authors of those reports were interested in heart and artery disease. [^1]

The second half was a set of reports on Raynaud's syndrome, a disorder of the peripheral circulation in which the fingertips and toes blanch in episodes. Its cause was unknown and it resisted treatment. Several research groups had measured the blood of patients with Raynaud's and found it abnormally viscous, with platelets that aggregated too readily. Other groups had found that injections of prostacyclin, a strong inhibitor of platelet aggregation, helped these patients for a short time. [^1]

Swanson put the two halves side by side. Fish oil lowers blood viscosity and platelet aggregability. Lower viscosity and lower aggregability could help a patient with Raynaud's. It follows that dietary fish oil might, at least in some cases, benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome.

He looked for that sentence in print and did not find it. None of the fish-oil articles he examined mentioned or cited the work on Raynaud's, and none of the Raynaud articles mentioned or cited the work on fish oil. The two groups of authors did not overlap and did not cite each other. [^1] Writing about the case three years later, he put it in one clause: "these two literatures had no articles in common." [^2]

He was exact about what he was and was not claiming. His search for a link between the two literatures had been, in his own account, reasonably diligent and far from exhaustive, and he invited readers to tell him of any connection he had missed. He did not claim that no physician had ever had the thought. He could not know that.

What he could establish was that no one had yet said it in print, and that the pieces needed to say it were already published, separately, by people who were not looking at each other's work. A fuller account of the argument went to a biomedical journal later the same year, where readers in a position to criticize it would see it. [^1]

A trial followed. In 1989 DiGiacomo, Kremer, and Shah published a double-blind, controlled, prospective study of fish-oil supplementation in patients with Raynaud's. Thirty-two patients with the primary or the secondary form of the condition were randomly assigned to fish-oil capsules or to olive-oil capsules as a placebo. The investigators put the patients' hands in water baths of decreasing temperature and timed, with a stopwatch, how long it took for blood flow in a finger to stop. [^3]

In the fish-oil group the median time before onset rose from 31.3 minutes at baseline to 46.5 minutes at six weeks. Among patients with the primary form who took fish oil, five of 11 could no longer be brought to an episode at the six-week or twelve-week visit. Among patients with the primary form who took olive oil, the figure was one of nine. The authors concluded that fish oil improves tolerance to cold and delays the onset of vasospasm in patients with primary Raynaud's, and that it does not do so in patients with the secondary form. [^3]

That is the evidence, and it should be read at its size. The trial tested one regimen in 32 people and found a benefit in one of two subgroups. It is a limited clinical test of the hypothesis. It is not a general recommendation, and it does not confirm each step of the mechanism Swanson proposed.

Three years separate the two publications: the hypothesis appeared in 1986 and the first reported trial in 1989. That interval measures how long validation took once the hypothesis was stated. It does not measure how long the two literatures had been available to be joined, which the record of this case does not date. [^2] [^3]

#### What Swanson did and did not do

Swanson did not examine a patient. He did not run an assay or collect a sample. Every observation in his argument had been made by someone else, written up, reviewed, and printed before he began.

The fish-oil researchers had measured what fish oil does to blood. The Raynaud researchers had measured what is wrong with the blood of their patients. Each group had done its work properly and published it where its colleagues would read it. [^1]

What Swanson added was the conclusion. In his terms, one literature said that A causes B, the other said that B causes C, and the statement that A causes C existed only as an implication that nobody had drawn.

He was exact about the status of that implication. It was a hypothesis. Whether it was true depended on how well the two premises had themselves been tested, and it would need a direct test of its own. But it was a real hypothesis with real support, and it had been available to anyone who read both sets of papers. [^1]

He also insisted that this was different from forgetting. The proposition that fish oil might help patients with Raynaud's was not something once known and then lost. On the evidence of the published record it had never been stated. It was new, and it was made entirely from old material. [^1]

The reason it had not been stated earlier was not that anyone had been careless. Swanson's explanation was that the quantity of published information is far larger than one person can read, so that everyone depends on search, and that search can never be shown to have found everything relevant to a problem.

A search retrieves documents by their titles, index terms, and subject headings. Those handles are assigned when a document is written. They cannot encode its relevance to problems that nobody had formulated at the time. A paper on the viscosity of blood after a fish-oil diet is indexed as a paper about fish oil and blood. Nothing in its index entry points toward a circulatory disorder of the fingers. [^1]

So two bodies of careful work could stand in the published record for years, each complete in its own terms, with a connection between them that was visible only to a reader who held both at once. For some period there was no such reader, or none who wrote it down. [^1]

#### The asymmetry

The founding text of Latent Information Archaeology, written by David Murray and published under a CC BY 4.0 license, opens with a general statement of the situation that Swanson's case shows in miniature. Its preamble observes that papers, patents, observations, failures, and archives each enter a particular context and are usually interpreted within the discipline, institution, language, or moment that produced them, and that the relationships between them are not necessarily exhausted when their original purpose is fulfilled. Then it says:

> There is an asymmetry between what humanity has recorded and what humanity has recognized. [LIA Preamble]

The next line names the subject of this piece:

> **Latent Information Archaeology (LIA) is the proposed discipline of investigating that asymmetry.** [LIA Preamble]

The fish-oil reports and the Raynaud reports were recorded. That one bore on the other was not recognized. The asymmetry in that case was one sentence wide, and it took one reader to close it and one trial to test what he found.

The preamble gives three forms the asymmetry can take. A finding can travel between fields, an experiment that failed can prove useful later, and facts established separately can imply a conclusion no one has drawn. [LIA Preamble] Swanson drew just such a conclusion by applying findings from cardiovascular research to a disorder of the hands.

The founding text is precise about how far its premise goes. "Its premise is not that all future discoveries have already been made. It is that some discoveries can be made from information already in existence, and that the systematic exploration of this possibility deserves to be a discipline in its own right." [LIA Preamble]

The word to hold on to is *some*. Swanson's hypothesis could be assembled from the library. Its test could not. Someone had to recruit 32 patients and fill the water baths. [^3] The claim is that there is a class of discoveries, and of candidates for discovery, for which the necessary observations have already been made and recorded, and that finding the members of that class is work that can be done on purpose, with method, and checked.

Swanson saw the same thing from the side of the library. He wrote that the growth of scientific knowledge is usually thought of as wresting new discoveries from the physical world, and that the world of recorded knowledge is a second frontier, open-ended in the same way. He added that making discoveries from the literature was neither unprecedented nor unusual. His point was that the structure of unintended connections, and the process of finding them, deserved to be studied in their own right. [^1] The founding text takes that step, and takes it past the biomedical library to every kind of record.

#### The route

The rest of this piece follows the order of the founding text and is built from cases like the one above. Each chapter begins with something that happened and can be checked, and then gives the part of the founding text that the case bears on.

Part I is about the backlog: the definition of LIA, the central thesis and the reasons the founding text gives for it, the cost of making a connection, and the reason for the word *archaeology*. Part II is about what is latent. It covers the kinds of latency the founding text names, the boundary it draws around the space of possible discoveries, and its distinctions between a candidate and a validated discovery and between novelty and truth. Part III is the excavations, domain by domain, starting from the lineage that begins with Swanson and including connections that were proposed, checked, and rejected. Part IV is the discipline: the fundamental problem of telling a plausible inference from an actual discovery, the principles, the method, the excavation record, and the research agenda by which the whole proposal is to be judged.

In 1986 the evidence for a testable treatment was sitting in the published record in two places, and the record did not contain the sentence that joined them. The chapters that follow ask how much else is in that condition, how it can be found, and how a real finding of this kind is told from a persuasive mistake.

## Part I: The backlog

<a id="chapter-i-01"></a>

### I-01. A definition

On March 15, 2023, Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross, and Brent Seales launched a competition called the Vesuvius Challenge. Its subject was a set of papyrus scrolls from a library at Herculaneum that was buried by a volcanic eruption in 79 AD, about two thousand years ago. The scrolls were found in the 18th century. Hundreds of them are kept in a library in Naples.

They are carbonized lumps, and they cannot be opened without severe damage; earlier attempts to unroll such scrolls by hand left fragments. For the challenge, scrolls belonging to the Institut de France were imaged at the Diamond Light Source, a particle accelerator near Oxford, and in March 2023 the organizers released the resulting high-resolution CT scans to anyone who wanted to work on them. They offered more than one million dollars in prizes. [^4]

The grand prize had a stated bar, set at the launch: four passages of 140 characters each, with at least 85 percent of the characters recoverable. Submissions closed at midnight on January 1, 2024. The organizers spent that January on review. In their words, a team of papyrologists worked to "review 15 columns of text in anonymized submissions," while a technical team audited and reproduced the submitted code and methods.

One entry stood out. Working independently, each papyrologist on the team recovered more text from it than from any other. It met the four-passage bar and included a further 11 columns, more than 2,000 characters in all. The prize of $700,000 went to Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor, and Julian Schilliger. [^4]

The organizers reported that the recovered text amounted to about 5 percent of one scroll, that it was not a duplicate of any existing work, and that its general subject, in the papyrologists' preliminary reading, was pleasure. [^4] The scroll itself was never unrolled. One calendar year separates the release of the scans in 2023 from the announcement in 2024, and that year is the interval that belongs to this case. The age of the writing is not a discovery lag. The result covers passages from a scroll, not the library. [^4]

What matters for a definition is where the text was during that year. It was in the scans. The scans were finished, published, and in many hands.

The ink was recorded in them as a signal that no one could yet separate from the carbonized papyrus around it. The entrants measured no new thing about the scroll. They worked out how to read a record that had already been made.

#### The definition

The founding text gives the canonical definition in one sentence.

> **Latent Information Archaeology is the systematic identification, reconstruction, and validation of previously unrecognized information, relationships, implications, and opportunities latent within existing informational artifacts.** [LIA §1]

The sentence has one adverb, three nouns that name activities, four objects, and a location. Each is doing work.

*Systematic* is the word that separates a discipline from an accident. People have always stumbled on things in old records. Swanson remarked that the discovery of new knowledge within the scientific literature was neither unprecedented nor unusual, and that what deserved study was the logical structure of such connections and the process of finding them. [^1] The definition asks for that: a way of working that can be stated in advance, repeated by someone else, and judged by its results.

*Identification* is finding that something is there. In the scrolls it was the stage at which an entrant could show that a pattern in the scan was ink and not an artifact of the scanner or of the model looking at it. In Swanson's case it was the recognition that two literatures contained complementary halves of one argument. [^4] [^1]

*Reconstruction* is assembling what has been identified into a form that can be read and examined. The scroll's surface had to be traced through the scan and flattened, and the detected ink laid out as columns a papyrologist could read. [^4] Swanson's reconstruction was an argument: fish oil reduces blood viscosity and platelet aggregability, those reductions could help a patient with Raynaud's syndrome, and so fish oil might help that patient. Each premise came with its citations, and the conclusion was written out so that a reader could attack it. [^1]

*Validation* is the test of whether the reconstruction should be believed. For the scrolls it had several parts, all reported by the organizers.

The submissions were anonymized before the papyrologists read them. The papyrologists worked independently of one another. A separate technical team reran the code. The winning entry used three different model architectures whose results supported one another, along with other measures the organizers list against overfitting and hallucination. [^4] For Swanson's hypothesis the validation was a clinical trial, run by other people and published three years after the hypothesis. [^2]

The three activities come in that order, and the definition does not allow the third to be dropped. A pattern that has been identified and reconstructed and never tested is not yet a finding. The founding text's own word for it, introduced in §5, is a candidate.

#### Four things that can be latent

The definition lists four objects: "information, relationships, implications, and opportunities." [LIA §1] They are different things, and the cases so far already separate the first three.

*Information* is the plainest. The columns of Greek in the scroll are information in the ordinary sense: text that someone wrote, that was preserved, and that could not be read. [^4] Nothing had to be inferred to get it. It had to be recovered.

A *relationship* is a connection between two artifacts, or between things recorded in them, that no record states. Before 1986 the fish-oil papers and the Raynaud papers were each in print. The relationship between them was that the effect measured in one set was the deficit measured in the other. That relationship was a fact about the two literatures, and it appeared in neither. [^1]

An *implication* is what follows once a relationship is seen. That fish oil might benefit patients with Raynaud's is not a relationship between papers. It is a claim about patients, and it is the product of the argument. Swanson was careful to call it a hypothesis that existed implicitly and had not been published explicitly. [^1]

An *opportunity* is something that could now be done. The founding text spells this out at the end of the same section: LIA is concerned with what can be recovered, inferred, or reconstructed from the existing record, "including what can be identified as a promising candidate for subsequent empirical investigation." [LIA §1]

The trial that DiGiacomo and colleagues ran was an opportunity that the two literatures had contained since the later of them was published, and that became visible when the implication was written down. [^2] An opportunity found this way is still only a candidate. It tells someone where to look. It does not tell them what they will find.

The phrase "previously unrecognized" governs all four. It does not mean unrecorded. The ink was recorded in the scan and the two premises were recorded in the journals. It means that, as far as the record shows, no one had yet read it, connected it, drawn it, or seen that it could be acted on.

#### Informational artifacts

The location in the definition is "within existing informational artifacts," and the founding text defines the term immediately.

> An informational artifact is any preserved representation of an observation, claim, process, decision, or event: a scientific paper, dataset, patent, photograph, recording, software repository, experimental notebook, model, or other record. [LIA §1]

Two words in that sentence set the scope. *Preserved* means the thing has to exist now, in some form that can be consulted. A conversation nobody wrote down is not an artifact. *Representation* means the artifact stands for something else: an observation that was made, a claim someone advanced, a process that was followed, a decision that was taken, an event that occurred. It is a record of the world and not the world.

The list that follows is deliberately wide. A scientific paper is the artifact in Swanson's case: a claim, the observations offered for it, and a list of the earlier papers it rests on. [^1] A dataset is the artifact in the scroll case. A CT scan is a preserved representation of an observation, namely how strongly each small volume of the scroll absorbed X-rays, and nothing in it is text until someone reads it as text. [^4]

A patent preserves a claim and the date on which it was disclosed. A photograph and a recording preserve an event. A software repository preserves a process, together with the sequence of decisions that changed it. An experimental notebook preserves observations that may never have reached print, including the ones that did not work. A model, in the sense of a trained computational model, preserves structure taken from whatever it was trained on, and the founding text gives that case separate treatment and separate cautions in §3.

The list ends with "or other record," so it is open. What the items share is that each one was made for a purpose, and each one contains more than its purpose required.

The scans were made to show the internal structure of a scroll. They also held its text. The fish-oil trials were run to learn about heart disease. They also held half of an argument about the circulation of the fingers.

#### A new understanding of what is already available

The next sentence of the founding text states what kind of result LIA produces.

> An archaeological discovery is not necessarily new information about the physical world. It may instead be a new understanding of the information already available about that world. [LIA §1]

The two cases sit at the two ends of what that sentence covers. In the scrolls, the discovery is close to plain recovery. The text was there, and after the work it could be read. Nobody's understanding of physics or of papyrus changed. What changed was that a record which had been opaque became legible, and the proof was that independent readers read the same letters. [^4]

In Swanson's case nothing was illegible. Every paper involved could be read by anyone who picked it up, and had been. The discovery was entirely a new understanding: that these papers, taken together, said something none of them said alone. [^1] It was also a discovery that pointed back out at the physical world, because the implication it produced could be tested on patients, and was. [^2]

Between those ends lie the cases the later parts of this piece take up, in which something is partly recovered and partly inferred. The definition covers the whole range, and it holds the range together with one requirement. The evidence has to come from artifacts that already exist. Where a claim needs a new observation to settle it, as Swanson's did, LIA supplies the candidate and the reasons for it, and the new observation belongs to experimental science.

#### A discipline, not a tool

The first section of the founding text closes with a sentence about what LIA is not.

> It is a discipline, not a specific algorithm, architecture, product, or model. Artificial intelligence is an important enabling technology, but it is not a prerequisite for doing LIA. [LIA §1]

The two cases show both halves of that sentence. The scroll text was recovered with machine learning. The strongest images in the winning entry came from a model trained to detect ink, and a large share of the surface was traced by software one of the winners wrote. [^4] Without those tools the scans would have stayed unread. That is what an enabling technology looks like.

Swanson's hypothesis was produced by a person searching indexes and reading articles. He was a professor in a library school, and his paper is as much about the limits of retrieval as about medicine. The method he used on the two literatures was to search, read what the search returned, revise the search, and reason about what he found. [^1] No model was involved, and the result meets every term of the definition: it was systematic enough that he could set out his steps, it identified a relationship, reconstructed an implication, and was validated by a trial. [^2]

A discipline is defined by its object and its standards. The object here is the gap between what existing artifacts contain and what has been recognized in them. The standards are the ones the word *validation* brings with it. Methods will change, and the founding text says so in its ninth principle: methods are replaceable, standards are not. A definition tied to one tool would expire with the tool.

The definition says what LIA does and where it works. Why there should be much for it to find depends on how large the gap is and why it exists, which is the subject of the founding text's second section and of the next chapter.

### I-02. The epistemic backlog

In 2015 Qing Ke and colleagues published a study in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* on a kind of paper that bibliometricians call a Sleeping Beauty: a paper whose citation history shows a long period with little attention followed by a sudden rise. Earlier studies had suggested such papers were scarce. Ke and colleagues pointed out that those studies had found Sleeping Beauties by setting thresholds, so many years asleep and so many citations on waking, and had applied the thresholds to small collections or to single disciplines. They replaced the thresholds with a single coefficient that needs no parameters and that scores any paper by how far its citation curve bends toward late recognition. Then, in the words of their abstract, they went on to "apply our method to 22 million scientific papers," covering all disciplines of the natural and social sciences across more than a century. [^5]

They reported that delayed recognition is not exceptional. There is no line with sleepers on one side and ordinary papers on the other. There is a continuous spectrum, in which both the length of the quiet period and the strength of the awakening vary. They also reported that the effect is much more visible in a multidisciplinary collection than in a single field, because many papers reach late and exceptional importance in a discipline different from the one in which they were published. [^5]

The study has to be read for what it measures. A citation records that one author pointed at another's paper. It is a measure of attention.

A paper that drew few citations for decades may have been read and used without being cited, and a paper that drew many may have been cited for a small part of what it contains. The coefficient shows when attention arrived. It does not show when the paper's usefulness or truth became known, and the study establishes no first-availability and first-recognition dates for any discovery, so no discovery lag is inferred from it here. [^5] What it does establish is narrower and still considerable: across 22 million papers, the interval between a paper entering the record and the record's users turning to it is often long, and the eventual users are often in another field.

#### The thesis

The second section of the founding text begins with the sentence that this piece treats as the central thesis.

> The informational state of civilization exceeds its articulated understanding of that information. [LIA §2]

The preamble puts the same point in terms of what people do: "Humanity has become extraordinarily good at producing information and comparatively poor at exhausting its implications." [LIA Preamble]

The thesis compares two quantities. One is what has been recorded, in all the artifacts that exist. The other is what has been stated about those artifacts: the conclusions drawn, the connections written down, the uses noticed.

The thesis says the first is larger than the second. Swanson made a claim of the same shape about the library. In his account the world of published knowledge contains more than any one person can know, and more than the sum of what all persons know. [^1]

The founding text then gives the reasons in one sentence.

> The gap arises because knowledge production is distributed, specialization is necessary, archives are incomplete, vocabulary changes, incentives favor certain questions, and the possible relationships between artifacts grow faster than our ability to investigate them. [LIA §2]

That sentence names six causes. None of them is a failure of effort or intelligence, and each can be seen in a case.

#### Six causes

*Knowledge production is distributed.* Michael Polanyi's account of how science is organized starts from this fact. Scientists choose their own problems and pursue them by their own judgment, and what coordinates them is that each adjusts to the results the others have published. He compared it to many people assembling one very large jigsaw puzzle in sight of one another, each watching for the place where the last piece fitted by someone else opens a place for the next. No central authority assigns the pieces, and in his view none could do so without bringing the work to a standstill. [^6]

A system built this way produces a great deal. It also means that no participant, and no office, holds the whole picture. Each worker watches the part of the puzzle near their own.

*Specialization is necessary.* Swanson stated the constraint plainly: the quantity of published information is far larger than one person can read in any reasonable span of time. [^1] The response of every field is to divide. A specialist reads the journals of the specialty and can be thorough there.

The cost falls at the borders. The fish-oil authors and the Raynaud authors were each reading their own literature, and the two sets of papers did not cite each other. [^1] The founding text calls specialization necessary and not regrettable. The alternative to reading narrowly is not reading everything. It is reading nothing well.

*Archives are incomplete.* An archive can be incomplete because something was never kept, and it can be incomplete in practice because what was kept cannot be reached by the means at hand. When Olga Kononova and colleagues built a dataset of synthesis procedures from materials science papers in 2019, they used articles from six publishers that had granted permission for large downloads, and they processed only papers published after 2000, because earlier full texts were mostly in PDF, which was hard to parse. [^7]

Everything printed before that date was still in the libraries. For the purposes of their method it was outside the corpus. Any excavation works on the part of the record it can get, and that part is never the whole.

*Vocabulary changes.* Swanson gave an example from engineering. Someone who wants the mathematical analysis of how a child pumps a swing will find very little by searching for words about pumping and swings. A far larger body of relevant work is filed under parametric amplifiers.

The physics is the same, and the words share nothing. He drew the general point that the terms attached to a document when it is written cannot anticipate every problem it will later bear on, least of all problems that had not been formulated at the time. [^1] Words also drift within a field across decades, and differ between languages. A search by words finds what was called by those words.

*Incentives favor certain questions.* The fish-oil researchers were funded and trained to study heart and artery disease, and their papers were written for readers with that interest. [^1] No one was paid to ask what their measurements meant for a disorder of the fingers. Ke and colleagues drew a conclusion about incentives directly from their data: their results are evidence against using short-term citation counts to quantify scientific impact. [^5] Where recent attention is what gets counted, work whose use will arrive late, or in another discipline, is counted low for as long as it sleeps.

*The possible relationships between artifacts grow faster than our ability to investigate them.* This cause is arithmetic. Among *n* artifacts there are *n*(*n* − 1)/2 possible pairs. Ten papers make 45 pairs. The 22 million papers in the Ke study make about 240 trillion pairs, before counting any relationship that involves three or more. [^5]

Almost all of those pairs mean nothing. The difficulty is that the meaningful ones are not marked. The number of papers also keeps rising. Van Raan and Winnink count the papers indexed in the Web of Science in physics, chemistry, engineering, and computer science at 287,117 for 1990 and 545,268 for 2007, a growth of about 4 percent a year. [^8]

When the number of artifacts roughly doubles, the number of possible pairs roughly quadruples. The number of people reading, and the hours each of them has, do not.

The six causes reinforce one another. Distribution and specialization put related work in separate places. Vocabulary hides the relation from search.

Incentives give no one the job of looking. Incomplete archives remove some of the evidence. The growth in possible relationships guarantees that an unsystematic look will cover a shrinking share.

#### The backlog

From these causes the founding text draws its central term.

> These conditions create an **epistemic backlog**: a collection of recoverable observations, potentially meaningful relationships, unresolved contradictions, and unexamined implications. [LIA §2]

A backlog, in ordinary use, is work that has arrived and has not been done. The word fits. The observations have been made and recorded. What has not been done is the reading across them. *Epistemic* marks the kind of work outstanding: it is the work of coming to know.

The term names four kinds of content. A recoverable observation is something that was recorded and can be got back. A potentially meaningful relationship is a connection between artifacts that might matter and has not been examined. An unresolved contradiction is a place where two records disagree and no one has worked out why. An unexamined implication is a conclusion that would follow from what is recorded and that nobody has drawn.

![Recorded artifacts, possible connections and the subset of explicit claims](figures/F1.png)

The first kind can be seen at the scale of a single field. The materials science literature holds, in the methods sections of its papers, a very large number of accounts of how particular inorganic compounds were made: which starting compounds, which operations, at what temperatures, for how long. Each account was written for the reader of that one paper.

Kononova and colleagues noted that the information was there in unstructured and arbitrary form, which obstructed its use, and that no comprehensive database of synthesis procedures existed, so that data-driven prediction of how to make a new material had nothing to learn from. Their pipeline classified paragraphs, picked out targets, starting compounds, operations, and conditions, and wrote each procedure as a structured entry with a balanced chemical equation. The dataset they reported consists of "19,488 synthesis entries retrieved from 53,538 solid-state synthesis paragraphs." [^7]

Every one of those recipes had been in print. Collectively they had never existed as something a person or a program could survey. The observations were recorded, and the relationships among them, which precursor is used for which class of target, which temperature goes with which operation, had not been available to examine.

The limits of this case are the limits of extraction. The authors evaluated how accurately their pipeline pulled out what the papers said. Extracting a recipe from text is not independent proof that the recipe will reproduce in a laboratory, and the case establishes no dates from which a discovery lag could be inferred. [^7]

The second and fourth kinds show in the patent record. Van Raan and Winnink took Sleeping Beauties in physics, chemistry, engineering, and computer science and asked which of them were cited in patents, and when. One of their central measurements was, in their words, the "time lag between the publication year of the SB-SNPRs and their first citation in a patent," where an SB-SNPR is a Sleeping Beauty cited in a patent as a scientific non-patent reference. They found papers that were first cited by a patent while still asleep in the journals, and they report that this lag has been shortening, so that in more recent years such papers tend to be picked up by inventors before scientists pick them up. They also found that only a small minority of these papers had an author who was an inventor on the citing patent. [^8]

The people who saw a use for the paper were usually not the people who wrote it. The relationship between the paper and the technical problem existed from the day of publication, and for years the scientific citation record did not show it. A patent citation is evidence of attention or use. It is not proof of commercial value, and the study does not establish discovery dates for individual cases. [^8]

#### What the backlog is not

The founding text does not leave the term there. The paragraph that follows is as much a part of the definition as the term itself.

> This backlog is not a fixed inventory of truths waiting to be retrieved. Its boundaries depend on the accessible corpus, the available methods of inference, the questions being investigated, and the standards of evidence applied. Some candidates will be false, others trivial, and others impossible to resolve without new observations. [LIA §2]

Each clause removes a tempting picture. The backlog is not a warehouse with a list of contents.

What can be found depends first on the accessible corpus: the Kononova dataset contains nothing from papers before 2000, because those papers were not in the corpus. [^7] It depends on the available methods of inference: the text in the Herculaneum scans was there before the methods that could read it. It depends on the questions being investigated: the fish-oil papers yielded a hypothesis about Raynaud's syndrome because someone asked about Raynaud's syndrome. [^1] And it depends on the standards of evidence applied, because whether something counts as found depends on what is required before saying so.

The last sentence of the paragraph gives three ways a candidate can fail to be worth having. It can be false: two records can seem to imply a third thing that is not so. It can be trivial: true, unstated, and of no interest to anyone. Or it can be impossible to resolve without new observations, in which case the existing record can raise the question and cannot answer it. Swanson's hypothesis was of that third kind until the trial was run. [^2]

Then comes the shortest sentence in the section.

> LIA exists to distinguish among them. [LIA §2]

That sentence assigns the discipline its job. Generating candidates from the backlog is the easier half, and the arithmetic of pairs shows why: there are always more possible connections than anyone can check. The work that makes LIA a discipline is sorting the candidates into the false, the trivial, the unresolvable, and the few that hold.

The founding text adds one more paragraph to this section, an economic thesis about the cost of investigating the backlog, which has its own chapter below. Before it comes the framing's statement of what kind of knowledge the backlog holds, which is easy to confuse with two older ideas.

<a id="chapter-i-03"></a>

### I-03. Known by civilization, not by any individual

Gregor Mendel's paper on his experiments with pea hybrids was printed in 1866 in the proceedings of the natural science society at Brünn, in the volume for the year 1865. He paid for 40 offprints and sent them to colleagues around Europe. One went to Kerner von Marilaun, who had taught him at the university in Vienna. That copy survives with its pages uncut, so it was evidently never read.

In 1881 Wilhelm Olbers Focke published a compendium of work on plant hybridization in which Mendel's experiments are mentioned. Charles Darwin owned a copy of Focke's book. [^9]

Some historians have suggested that Mendel also mailed an offprint to Darwin. The historical analysis this chapter relies on says those claims are impossible to substantiate, and it adds that there is no copy of the offprint in the existing Darwin archive. [^9] That is all the record supports, and the matter stays there.

In 1900 the paper came back into use, and the way it came back is less tidy than the usual telling. Hugo de Vries had begun a program of plant breeding in 1892 and found a 3:1 ratio in the second generation of his crosses. When he published in a French journal in 1900 he did not mention Mendel. He conceded Mendel's priority a few weeks later, in a German version of the same article, after Carl Correns criticized him.

Correns reported that he had found the same ratio in 1899 in peas and maize, and he wrote that Mendel had preceded them both. He appears to have come to Mendel through Focke's compendium. Erich von Tschermak published in June 1900, after he had read both de Vries and Correns, and that sequence, together with doubts about how well he understood Mendel's argument, has put his standing as an independent rediscoverer in question. Since the 1950s historians have regularly questioned both the chronology and the separate contributions of all three men. [^9]

The parts of this account that are not in dispute are enough for the present purpose. A result was in print in 1866. Offprints were in at least 40 hands. A standard reference work of 1881 pointed to it. And in the 1890s, people who were breeding plants to answer the question the paper had addressed were working without it. [^9]

Mendel himself knew what he had found, so the case is not a pure example of a result that no person held. It shows the adjacent condition: a result that was on the shelf, was indexed in the literature of its own subject, and was absent from the working knowledge of the people who needed it.

#### The key distinction

The framing, David Murray's companion statement to the founding text, states what it calls the key distinction in two sentences.

> The key distinction: conventional research seeks to produce new information. LIA seeks to discover what is effectively already known by civilization, but not known by any individual within it. [Framing, Intro]

The first sentence is about the ordinary business of science. An experiment, a survey, a field season, or an observing run adds something to the record that was not there before. The second sentence sets a different target. The thing sought is already in the record, in the sense that the record supports it. What is missing is a person who knows it.

The word *effectively* matters. Civilization does not know things the way a person does. What it has is the record: the papers, the data, the patents, the notebooks. To say that civilization effectively knows something is to say that the record contains what is needed to establish it, so that a reader who gathered the right artifacts and reasoned correctly could arrive at it without making a new observation. To say that no individual knows it is to say that no one has yet done so.

The founding text carries the same distinction in the terms of its preamble, as the asymmetry between what humanity has recorded and what humanity has recognized. [LIA Preamble] The record is on one side and persons are on the other.

Two older ideas sit close to this one and are often mistaken for it. One concerns knowledge that is spread across many people. The other concerns discoveries that are made more than once. LIA borrows from the facts each idea rests on, and it is a different claim from both.

#### Knowledge spread across people

In 1945 the economist F. A. Hayek published an essay on how a society makes use of what its members know. His starting point was that the knowledge an economy needs is never collected in one place. It never exists in concentrated or integrated form, he wrote, and exists only as dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge held by separate individuals. The economic problem, as he put it, is one of using knowledge that is not given to anyone in its totality. [^10]

Hayek's subject is knowledge that people do have. A shipper knows that a vessel is sailing half empty. A manager knows that a machine is standing idle.

Each piece is in someone's head. Nobody has all the pieces, and nobody could, because much of it is knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place that changes before it could be reported to a center. [^10] His question was how the actions of all these people can be coordinated when the knowledge cannot be pooled.

Michael Polanyi asked a version of the same question about science. In his account, scientists choose problems independently and coordinate by adjusting to one another's published results, and the outcome is a joint achievement that none of them planned. His image for it was a group of people working on one very large jigsaw puzzle within sight of each other. [^6]

Both writers give an account of a real feature of the world that LIA depends on. It is the first of the six causes the founding text gives for the backlog: knowledge production is distributed. [LIA §2] And both are writing about a different situation from the one the framing's distinction picks out. In Hayek's economy and Polanyi's republic, every piece of knowledge has a holder.

What is distributed is the holding. The problem is coordination among people who each know their part.

In Swanson's case the pieces had holders and the conclusion had none. The fish-oil researchers knew what fish oil does to blood. The Raynaud researchers knew what is abnormal in their patients' blood. That fish oil might help a patient with Raynaud's was, on the evidence of the published record, known to neither group and stated by no one. [^1]

No one was waiting to be consulted. The statement did not exist until someone derived it. Swanson's name for this was knowledge that is public and yet undiscovered: independently created pieces that are logically related and have never been retrieved, brought together, and interpreted. [^1]

Hayek's problem and LIA's problem therefore have different remedies. Dispersed knowledge is put to use by a mechanism that lets each holder act on what they know, without anyone needing the whole. Unstated implications are found only by an act of assembly: someone, or something, has to put the pieces side by side and draw the conclusion. Coordination among specialists will not do it, because each specialist can be perfectly coordinated with the others in their own field and still never see the other field's half.

One caution follows from Hayek and applies to LIA directly. Much of the knowledge he has in view was never written down. It lives in practice and in the circumstances of the moment. LIA's material is the informational artifact, the preserved record, and the claim that civilization effectively knows something is a claim about what the record supports. It is not a claim that everything anyone knows could be recovered from an archive. [^10]

#### Discoveries made twice

The second nearby idea is the multiple discovery. In 1922 William Ogburn and Dorothy Thomas published a short article with the title "Are Inventions Inevitable?" They began with cases any reader would know: Newton and Leibniz and the calculus, Darwin and Wallace and natural selection, Gray and Bell and the telephone. They went on to cases less well known, among them the discovery of oxygen by Scheele and by Priestley, and the invention of decimal fractions, credited to three men. Their appendix lists 148 such cases from mathematics, astronomy, chemistry, physics, medicine, biology, psychology, and practical mechanics, and they remarked that the list could be considerably extended. [^11]

Their interest was in what they called the status of culture. An invention, on their argument, needs certain earlier elements to exist before it can be made, and once those elements exist, more than one person may put them together. They were cautious about how far this went. To say that culture is a determining factor, they wrote, does not tell us which elements and conditions are the necessary ones, and in most cases the situation was not known well enough to predict an invention's timing. [^11]

Their catalogue is a historical list. It is not an experiment, and it does not prove that any given discovery had to happen. Robert K. Merton later took up the same subject in a 1961 paper, "Singletons and Multiples in Scientific Discovery," which is cited here by its title and subject only. [^12]

For LIA the useful thing in Ogburn and Thomas is one plain inference from their list. If the same step was taken independently by two or three people who had access to the same prior record, then the prior record was sufficient to support that step. Each multiple is a small demonstration that the materials for a discovery can be present in what is already known, since more than one person reached it from there.

The differences are just as plain. A multiple discovery is a discovery that was made, and made more than once. The questions it raises are about timing and credit. The situation LIA addresses is the one in which the record supports a step and nobody has taken it.

The multiples in the 1922 list also happened among people working on the same problem, who knew what the problem was. In Swanson's case there was no shared problem. The two halves lay in literatures whose authors had no reason to read each other, so the ordinary process by which several workers converge on the same result had nothing to act on. [^1] The fish-oil case is, in that sense, the opposite of a multiple: a step the record supported, with no one positioned to take it.

Mendel's case has sometimes been told as a multiple, with three men converging in 1900. The disputed history above is a reason to be careful with that telling. [^9] What the case shows without dispute is the other thing: that being in print, and even being indexed, is not the same as being known by the people at work on the question.

#### Three examples

With those two ideas set to one side, the framing's distinction can be read against the three examples the founding text gives in its preamble.

> An observation made in one field may resolve a problem in another. A failed experiment may contain the evidence needed for a future invention. Two independently established facts may imply a third that nobody has articulated. [LIA Preamble]

In each of the three, the record holds the material and no individual holds the result.

In the first, an observation has a holder, the person who made it, and a problem has holders, the people who are stuck on it. They work in different fields. The observer does not know the problem exists, and the people with the problem do not know the observation was made. Each side knows its own part completely. The relation between the parts is what nobody knows.

In the second, the experiment was run and written up as a failure, judged against the purpose it was run for. The record of it holds measurements that mean something else under a different question, and the person who made them filed them under the question they were asking at the time. The evidence is recorded. Its bearing on a later invention is not known to anyone until someone reads the old result with the new purpose in mind.

In the third, two facts are each established, each with its own evidence and its own readers, and a third fact follows from them together. This is the form of Swanson's case, and it is the cleanest illustration of the framing's sentence. Both premises were known. The conclusion was, in the framing's word, effectively known by civilization, because the record supported it. It was not known by any individual, because no one had drawn it. [^1]

None of these is a failure of the people involved. The fish-oil researchers were not negligent for not reading the literature on a circulatory disorder, and Mendel's contemporaries were not fools. The six causes in the founding text's second section explain how careful people, each doing their own work well, leave results of this kind lying in the record. [LIA §2]

A result of this kind does not surface on its own, because someone has to pay for the search and for the reasoning. The founding text and the framing both make a claim about that cost, and the next chapter takes it up.

<a id="chapter-i-04"></a>

### I-04. The cost of a connection

In 2011 the journal *Scientometrics* carried a study by Chihmao Hsieh on how inventors search for useful inventions. Hsieh treated an invention as a combination of features, and he asked how the usefulness of an invention depends on how closely related its features already are. His data came from a public database covering every invention granted a United States patent between 1975 and 1999, with the citations each patent made and received. From 2.14 million patents granted between the start of 1975 and the end of 1993, he selected the 18,882 for which relatedness could be measured through two generations of earlier citations. He measured usefulness by the citations a patent later received. [^13]

His central result was an inverted U. Inventions whose features were barely related tended to be less useful, and so did inventions whose features were very closely related. The most useful lay in between. Hsieh explained the shape with two costs that move in opposite directions.

When features are unrelated, the expensive part is finding any situation in which they belong together. It takes a wide knowledge to see it. When features are closely related, they tend to serve similar purposes, and the expensive part is working out what could be gained by combining them at all. He put the turning point in one sentence: "The difficulty shifts from the cost of connecting unrelated features to the cost of synthesizing overly related ones." [^13]

He gave a homely example of the second cost. Take shoes with wheels that pop out for skating, and add planks that pop out and lock together into a skateboard. The two features are closely related, since both are ways of rolling along a pavement, and they are substitutes for each other. To his knowledge no such product existed, and it is hard to say what anyone would do with one. [^13]

The study is a mechanism and a pattern in citation data. It does not put a price in dollars or hours on any connection, and it does not estimate how much time a discipline like LIA could save. [^13] What it supplies for this chapter is a plain observation with data behind it: making a connection costs something, there is more than one kind of cost, and the costs change with how far apart the things to be connected are.

#### The premise

The framing closes with a section headed "The bigger idea," and that section opens with an economic premise.

> LIA could be formalized around an economic premise: the marginal cost of generating information has historically been lower than the aggregate cost of connecting all available information. [Framing §6]

The next line gives the consequence.

> Civilization has accumulated an enormous backlog of unmade connections. [Framing §6]

The premise compares two costs. The first is marginal: what it takes to add one more item to the record, one more paper, dataset, patent, or filing. The second is aggregate: what it would take to check everything in the record against everything else. The premise says that, historically, the first has been lower than the second.

The arithmetic from the chapter on the backlog shows why the comparison comes out that way. Adding one artifact to a record that already holds *n* artifacts is one unit of work for its author. It also creates *n* new possible pairs, one with each artifact already there, and someone would have to examine each of those to know whether it means anything. The cost of producing grows with the number of artifacts.

The cost of connecting all of them grows with the number of relationships among them, which is far larger. As long as people produce steadily and can read only so much, the unexamined share rises. That is how a backlog accumulates without anyone neglecting anything. The founding text states the same cause in its list: the possible relationships between artifacts grow faster than our ability to investigate them. [LIA §2]

Hsieh's two costs add a refinement. The price of a connection is not uniform across the record. Connections between distant things are costly to find. [^13]

The same holds in the library. Swanson's two literatures were far apart, and the expensive part of his work was the finding: once the fish-oil papers and the Raynaud papers were on the same desk, the argument took three sentences. [^1] The backlog should therefore be deepest where the search cost is highest, which is between fields.

#### What has been measured

Three studies already cited in this piece bear on the premise. Each measures something real, and each measures it through citations.

Feng Shi and James Evans, in a 2023 study in *Nature Communications*, built a model of what combinations to expect, using corpora of papers and of patents, and scored each new paper or patent by how surprising its combination of contents and of cited contexts was against that expectation. They report that surprise of this kind predicts outsized impact, and, as the title of their paper states, that such combinations tend to emerge with scientific outsiders from distant disciplines. [^14] The association with top-decile impact is observational. It does not prove that the unusual combination caused the citations, or that every surprising bridge is a useful one. [^14] Read alongside Hsieh, it says that distant connections, when they are made and hold up, are disproportionately noticed afterward, and that the people who make them tend to come from outside the field where the result lands.

Van Raan and Winnink measured one interval directly. For Sleeping Beauties that were later cited in patents, they took the difference between the paper's publication year and the filing year of the first patent to cite it. In one of their two sets the lag ran from 1 to 18 years, with an average of 8.5.

In the other it ran from 1 to 14 years, with an average of 6.7. They also report that the lag has been getting shorter over time. [^8] That is a measured wait between a result entering the record and its first documented technical use, for a selected group of papers. It is a figure for attention and use as patents record them, and it does not show what any of those patents was worth. [^8]

Ke and colleagues measured dormancy across 22 million papers and found that late recognition often arrives from a discipline other than the paper's own. [^5]

All three are measures of citation and attention, and none of them is a direct measurement of what a connection cost to make. They are consistent with the premise: unusual combinations are rare and rewarded, the wait for use across the line between science and technology runs to years, and recognition from another field is often late. They do not establish the premise. No one has yet put the two costs it compares, producing and connecting, on one scale and measured both.

#### What changes the price

The framing then says what might alter the comparison.

> Agentic AI, cheap inference, multimodal analysis and persistent computational systems potentially change the economics of excavating that backlog. [Framing §6]

The founding text makes the corresponding claim in the last paragraph of its second section, and names four improvements.

> The economic thesis is that improvements in search, machine reasoning, representation learning, and computational capacity can reduce the cost of investigating this backlog. If true, activities that were previously impractical may become routine. [LIA §2]

The two lists give one change from two sides. The founding text names capabilities: search, machine reasoning, representation learning, computational capacity. The framing names the forms in which those capabilities now arrive.

Cheap inference means each act of machine reading or reasoning costs little. Multimodal analysis means the reading is not confined to text, so that images, tables, and scans come within reach. Agentic AI means a system can carry out a chain of steps, searching, reading, comparing, and checking, without a person directing each one. Persistent computational systems are ones that keep working over a corpus for a long time, where a single query is asked once and answered once.

Each of these bears on the search cost in Hsieh's account, the cost of finding out that two distant things belong together.

One published example shows what "previously impractical" can look like. In 2019 Vahe Tshitoyan and colleagues collected approximately 3.3 million abstracts from materials science and related journals, published between 1922 and 2018, and trained a model that represents each word as a vector according to the words that appear around it. No chemistry was put in by hand and nothing was labeled. They then ranked materials by how close each one's vector lay to the vector for the word "thermoelectric," keeping only materials that had never appeared in an abstract alongside thermoelectric keywords. [^15]

To find out whether such a ranking meant anything, they repeated it with the literature cut off at each of eighteen successive years, from 2001 to 2018, each time training only on abstracts published before the cutoff and taking the top fifty predictions. They report that materials from those lists were on average eight times more likely to be studied as thermoelectrics within the following five years than materials chosen at random from those not yet studied, and three times more likely than a random material with a nonzero band gap. [^15]

The test has limits that the later chapters return to. What was scored is whether a material later appeared in the literature alongside thermoelectric terms. It is a measure of what researchers went on to study, and it is not an experimental confirmation of each predicted material. [^15]

For the question of cost the relevant point is narrower. Ranking every candidate material against a property, using the wording of 3.3 million abstracts, is a search no person could have carried out by reading. With the model it was a computation. The authors drew the conclusion themselves: latent knowledge regarding future discoveries is to a large extent embedded in past publications. [^15]

A second study bears on where the cheapened search should be pointed. Jamshid Sourati and James Evans, writing in *Nature Human Behaviour* in 2023, built models that take into account the distribution of human expertise: which scientists work on what, and so which connections experts are in a position to make. They distinguish two uses. One is predicting the discoveries that human scientists are about to make. The other is tuning the model to avoid the crowd and generate promising hypotheses that, in their description, are unlikely to be imagined or pursued without intervention until the distant future. [^16]

The second use is the one that matters to the premise. If the backlog is deepest where nobody is positioned to look, then a method that knows where the experts are can be aimed at where they are not.

#### A research proposition

The founding text ends its statement of the economic thesis with a sentence that fixes its status.

> This is a research proposition, not an established universal law. [LIA §2]

The framing's wording is in step with it: the new tools "potentially change" the economics. [Framing §6] Both texts state the thesis as something to be found out.

There are concrete reasons for the status. The thesis says the cost of *investigating* the backlog can be reduced, and investigating includes validating. A system that proposes connections cheaply may also propose false ones cheaply, and each proposal still has to be checked.

If checking stays expensive, then lowering the price of generation moves the cost downstream to the reviewer and leaves the total where it was. Hsieh's second cost applies here too: finding that two things are related is not the same as showing that the combination is worth anything. [^13] Tshitoyan's ranking produced fifty candidates a year at little cost, and whether a given candidate is in fact a good thermoelectric is settled in a laboratory. [^15]

The founding text says how the proposition is to be tested. Its research agenda in §10 includes an entry for this purpose.

> **Excavation economics.** Measure cost per defensible finding, reviewer time, reproducibility, and the marginal benefit of additional computation or data. [LIA §10]

Each measurement answers a part of the question. *Cost per defensible finding* is the figure the thesis is about. It counts everything spent, on computation and on people, and divides by the number of findings that survive scrutiny, with the candidates that were generated and discarded counted in the cost.

*Reviewer time* isolates the human share, which is where a downstream bottleneck would show. *Reproducibility* asks whether a second team following the same record reaches the same finding, since a result that cannot be reproduced was not cheap at any price. *The marginal benefit of additional computation or data* asks whether spending more keeps yielding more, or whether returns flatten.

The same section lists "cost per validated result" among the useful metrics, and adds that no single score can adequately represent all of the dimensions it names. [LIA §10]

The founding text commits to the measurement and not to the answer. The first line of its agenda says the discipline should be judged by what it can demonstrate, not by the ambition of its premise. [LIA §10]

The framing's "bigger idea" ends by naming what a favorable answer would allow: a system that is more than a better research assistant, an autonomous discovery engine that continually searches the space of things humanity could already know, but doesn't. [Framing §6] Part IV takes up what such a system would have to be, and the next chapter settles why a discipline with this object is named after the study of buried things.

### I-05. Why archaeology

In 1901 a corroded mechanism was recovered from a shipwreck, and it is known as the Antikythera mechanism. It survives as fragments. In 2005 those fragments were imaged again, and in 2006 Freeth and colleagues published the results in *Nature* under the title "Decoding the ancient Greek astronomical calculator known as the Antikythera Mechanism." Their work used, in the paper's words, "surface imaging and high-resolution X-ray tomography of the surviving fragments." From the images the team reported a reconstruction of the functions of the gears, and they reported inscriptions that had not been deciphered before. [^17]

The corroded pieces had kept detail of gears and of lettering beyond anything an inspection of their surfaces could show. The 2006 reconstruction came from joining the geometry of the fragments to the inscriptions on them and working out what astronomical functions that combination could have served. [^17]

One hundred and five years separate the recovery in 1901 from the publication in 2006, and the figure has to be read correctly. It is the interval from recovery to one specific reconstruction. It is not the interval to first recognition. Scholars had recognized the object as an astronomical mechanism well before 2005. [^17]

The fragments were a known object in a museum for a century. What changed was how much of them could be read.

That is a case from archaeology in the ordinary sense, and it has the features that led the founding text to choose its name.

#### The name

The eleventh section of the founding text is titled "Why archaeology?" and its first sentence gives the reason.

> Archaeology is an appropriate metaphor because the investigator encounters a record shaped by time, loss, preservation, selection, and context. [LIA §11]

The sentence names five forces, and each of them acts on informational artifacts as it acts on objects in the ground.

*Time* changes what a record can say. The vocabulary of a field drifts, and the reader of a paper written decades ago is not the reader it was written for. The fragments from Antikythera were the same in 1901 and in 2005. The means of examining them were not. [^17]

*Loss* removes part of the record for good. Whatever was once written and no longer exists cannot be consulted.

*Preservation* is the other face of loss, and it is rarely neutral. What survives often survives because of an accident or an interest that has nothing to do with its later importance. The library at Herculaneum exists as a library because an eruption buried and carbonized it, and for the same reason it could not be read. [^4]

*Selection* is what people did on purpose: what a society chose to print, what an editor chose to accept, what a collector chose to buy, what a laboratory chose to write up. A published literature is a selected record, and the selection had purposes of its own.

*Context* is what surrounded the artifact when it was made and when it was found: what was beside it, what it answered, who made it, and for what.

An investigator of the existing record does not get to design the record. It arrives already shaped, and the shaping has to be understood before anything in it can be trusted.

#### Position matters

The second paragraph of the section turns to the single artifact.

> An artifact rarely explains itself. Its position relative to other artifacts matters. [LIA §11]

The archive of Zenon shows what position is worth. Zenon, son of Agreophon, was born about 285 BC in Kaunos, a city in Caria, and made his career in Egypt. He worked first as business representative and private secretary to Apollonios, the finance minister, and later managed Apollonios's estate at Philadelpheia in the Fayum. He kept his papers. The archive dates from 263 to 229 BC and holds more than 1,800 texts, most of them in Greek, which makes it the largest archive of Ptolemaic times. [^18]

The papers were dug up at the site of Philadelpheia by people digging the ancient mounds for fertilizer. According to the Trismegistos archive record, "The first text surfaced in 1911 in London and Strasbourg," and the major part was sold on the antiquities market from about 1914 onward. The Egyptian Museum in Cairo acquired the bulk. The rest went in bundles and in loose fragments to collections in Europe and the United States: the record lists holdings in more than twenty institutions, among them London, Florence, Manchester, Ann Arbor, New York, and New Haven. [^18]

A single papyrus from this archive is a letter about a shipment of grain, or an account, or a contract. It names people it does not introduce and refers to business it does not explain. Read in the collection where it happens to lie, apart from the others, it is a document about an unknown man's errands.

Reassembled, the papers show a career. The archive was pieced together by a team under Pestman, whose *Guide to the Zenon Archive* appeared in 1980 with a survey by type and by date, a register of the persons named, and indexes. Ordered by date, the texts fall into phases that follow Zenon's life: a journey through Palestine as Apollonios's representative, inspection tours of the Nile Delta as his secretary, and then the management of the estate. Some letters that Zenon himself had sent turn up in his own archive, because the papers of his predecessor on the estate, to whom he had written, later passed into his hands. [^18] That fact can only be seen by someone who knows where each letter sits among the others.

The record of this case has limits, and the catalogue states them. Some texts are still unpublished, and new fragments still surface, some of which join papyri already published. The source documents the dispersal and the 1980 guide. It does not by itself demonstrate a complete reconstruction of the network of people in the archive, and it gives no pair of dates from which a discovery lag could be inferred. [^18]

The same principle governs artifacts that have nothing to do with antiquity. A paper's meaning depends on what it cites and what cites it, on what else was known when it was written, and on what was published beside it. The fish-oil reports said what they said about blood. Their bearing on Raynaud's syndrome appeared when they were placed next to the Raynaud reports, and not before. [^1] The Zenon case also explains why sellers and collectors do damage that readers must later undo: the archive had probably been kept in an order, and the dispersal broke most of it. [^18]

#### What an absence means

The paragraph ends with a sentence about what is not there.

> An absence may be meaningful, or it may simply reflect what was never recorded or what failed to survive. [LIA §11]

The sentence allows three readings of a gap, and from where the investigator stands they look alike. The thing may be absent because it did not happen. It may have happened and never been recorded. It may have been recorded and the record may not have survived.

The question of Mendel and Darwin is a small and exact instance. Some historians have suggested that Mendel mailed Darwin a copy of his paper. There is no copy of the offprint in the existing Darwin archive. The historical analysis already cited calls the claims impossible to substantiate. [^9]

The absence of an offprint from the archive is consistent with Mendel never having sent one. It is also consistent with one having been sent and discarded, or lost at some point in the century and a half since. The archive cannot tell these apart. The honest statement is the one the historians made: the claim is unsubstantiated. This chapter adds nothing to it.

The discipline here is to leave unresolved what the record leaves unresolved. Swanson practiced the same restraint about the literature. Having found no article that linked fish oil to Raynaud's syndrome, he reported that his search had found none, said the search was not exhaustive, and asked readers to tell him of any he had missed. [^1] He did not convert "I found nothing" into "there is nothing."

An absence can also be evidence. That the fish-oil literature and the Raynaud literature shared no articles and did not cite each other was the observation on which Swanson's case rested. [^2] An absence of that kind has to be established with care, by a stated search over a stated body of records, and it holds only for that body. The founding text builds this into its account of the epistemic boundary in §4, where every claim about what is or is not in the record is relative to a corpus, a date, and a method.

#### The rule

The third paragraph of the section is two sentences long.

> The archaeologist cannot legitimately manufacture missing evidence and present it as recovered material. Neither can the information archaeologist. [LIA §11]

In field archaeology the rule is easy to picture. An excavator who carves an inscription and reports having dug it up has committed fraud. An excavator who restores a broken vessel with new plaster, and marks the plaster as new, has not. The offense lies in presenting what was made as what was found.

The rule carries over to informational artifacts with one complication, which is the tool. A large language model produces fluent text in response to whatever it is given. Asked for the passage in which a source supports a claim, it can produce a passage.

Asked to complete a damaged text, it can supply the words. Asked for a reference, it can produce a title, authors, a journal, and a year. The output looks the same whether or not anything stands behind it.

The rule forbids presenting such output as recovered material. Text that a model generated to fill a gap is not a reading of the artifact. A quotation that cannot be found in the source is not a quotation. A citation to a paper that cannot be produced is not evidence.

The rule does not forbid reconstruction. Reconstruction is the second term of the definition. What it requires is that the made part be marked as made and that the reconstruction be answerable to the artifact. The Vesuvius Challenge is a worked example of the difference. The ink in those scrolls was detected by trained models, so the risk that a model might produce letter-like shapes of its own was plain to everyone involved, and the review was designed around that risk.

The technical team reran the submitted code. The papyrologists independently reviewed 15 columns in anonymized submissions. The organizers announced the winning entry in February 2024. [^4] The winning entry's results were corroborated across three different model architectures, with the further measures the organizers list against overfitting and hallucination. [^4] The reading was accepted because every letter in it could be traced back to a signal in the scan by someone other than the people who claimed it.

A filled gap presented as a find destroys the value of the finds beside it, because a reader can no longer tell which is which.

#### Four words for the work

The section closes by naming the work.

> The work is excavation, reconstruction, interpretation, and verification. It is as much about understanding the limits of the record as it is about discovering what the record contains. [LIA §11]

*Excavation* is getting the material out with its context intact: finding the artifacts, and recording where each came from and what lay beside it. The scanning of the fragments from Antikythera in 2005 was excavation of this kind, carried out on an object that had left the sea a century earlier. [^17]

*Reconstruction* is assembling the pieces into a whole that can be examined: the gear functions worked out from the fragments, the columns of Greek laid out from a scan, the phases of Zenon's career ordered from papers held in more than twenty collections. [^17] [^4] [^18]

*Interpretation* is saying what the reconstructed thing means. The gears are read as serving astronomical functions. The Greek columns are read, provisionally, as a text about pleasure. Swanson's two literatures are read as implying a treatment worth testing. [^17] [^4] [^1]

*Verification* is the check: independent readers, reproduced code, a clinical trial. [^4] [^2]

The second sentence of the closing paragraph gives the limits of the record equal weight with its contents. Every case in Part I has come with a statement of what it does not show.

The trial of fish oil supported a benefit in one subgroup of 32 patients. [^3] The scroll result covers passages from one scroll. [^4] The citation studies measure attention. [^5] The Zenon catalogue documents a dispersal and a guide. [^18] These statements are part of each finding.

Part I has set out what LIA is, why there is a backlog for it to work on, what the work costs, and why it carries the name it does. All of it rests on the word *latent*, which the founding text divides into classes in its third section, and Part II begins there.

## Part II: What is latent

<a id="chapter-ii-01"></a>

### II-01. Five kinds of latency

In 1911 a fragment of papyrus came onto the antiquities market and surfaced in London and Strasbourg. It was the first text to surface from the papers of Zenon, son of Agreophon, a Greek from Kaunos who managed an estate at Philadelpheia in the Fayum in the third century BC. Local diggers had found the archive at Gharabet el-Gerza, the site of the ancient town. From about 1914 onwards the major part of it was sold.

The Egyptian Museum in Cairo acquired the bulk, and its director, C. C. Edgar, published that share in five volumes. The rest went to collections in Europe and the United States. The catalogue entry for the archive lists 21 holding collections, from Alexandria and Ann Arbor to Manchester, New Haven, and Paris. It counts 1,819 texts as certainly belonging to the archive and 14 more as uncertain. [^18]

The sellers had sold the papyri in bundles, and small fragments that had fallen off in handling were sold separately, some to Giessen and some to Cairo University. A letter could be in one country and the piece broken from its edge in another. In 1980 a team under P. W. Pestman published *A Guide to the Zenon Archive*, which put the scattered texts back into one sequence, with a survey by type and by date, a register of the persons named, and indexes. The work has not ended. The catalogue reports that some texts are still unpublished and that new fragments still surface, some of which join papyri already published. [^18]

For most of the twentieth century, then, the archive was hidden in two different ways at once. Some of its pieces sat unread or unidentified in a collection. And the connections between pieces in different collections, which letter answered which, which account belonged to which year, were recorded nowhere until someone worked them out. [^18]


![Five non-exclusive classes of latency with examples](figures/F3.png)

#### Latency is relational

The third section of the founding text is titled "What is latent?" It begins with a statement about the word itself.

> Latency is relational. A claim can be explicit in one archive, invisible to another community, and genuinely unarticulated when considered across an entire body of evidence. [LIA §3]

The sentence gives three positions a single claim can occupy. It can be written out plainly in one place. It can be unknown to people who do not read that place. And it can be something no one has written at all, which appears only when a whole body of evidence is considered together. A Zenon letter in Florence was explicit to the papyrologist who had it on the table.

What it meant for a letter in London was not explicit to anyone until the two were read together. Asking whether something is latent therefore always needs a second term: latent to whom, in which record.

The founding text then names the kinds.

> LIA recognizes several kinds of latency: [LIA §3]

| Class | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Archival** | Information explicitly recorded but subsequently forgotten, inaccessible, or overlooked. |
| **Relational** | Meaningful connections among artifacts that have not been recognized or documented together. |
| **Inferential** | Conclusions or hypotheses obtainable by combining observations, claims, or constraints. |
| **Contextual** | Previously known information whose relevance becomes apparent under different conditions or in a different domain. |
| **Representational** | Structure or associations encoded in a computational representation, such as an embedding or trained model, that require elicitation and independent verification. |

There are five classes. Each can be shown in one case.

#### Archival

Archival latency is the plainest of the five. The information was "explicitly recorded." Nothing has to be inferred. What stands between the record and a reader is that the record was forgotten, could not be reached, or was passed over.

Every Zenon papyrus is a written document, and most of the texts are in Greek, a language papyrologists read. A receipt in the archive says what it says. But a receipt in an unpublished lot, or a fragment in a box with no note of where it came from, is recorded and unavailable in the same moment. The catalogue's remark that some texts remain unpublished means that part of this archive is in that condition now. [^18]

#### Relational

Relational latency concerns "connections among artifacts." Each artifact may be well known. What has not been recognized or documented is that they belong together.

The clearest case is the one the prologue told. Swanson found one set of articles reporting what dietary fish oil does to the blood and a second set reporting what is abnormal in the blood of patients with Raynaud's syndrome. Each set was published, indexed, and read by its own specialists. In his account three years later, "these two literatures had no articles in common." The connection between them was the thing that was missing from the record, and he supplied it in 1986. [^2]

The Zenon archive carries the same class in a second form. Once the pieces were ordered, the texts fell into phases that follow one man's career. That ordering is a set of relations among documents, and no single document states it. The catalogue is the evidence for the dispersal and for the 1980 guide. It does not by itself demonstrate a complete reconstruction of the network of people in the letters. [^18]

#### Inferential

Inferential latency is a conclusion or hypothesis "obtainable by combining observations, claims, or constraints." The third word matters. A constraint is a fact about what is possible, and combining a claim with a constraint can show that the claim cannot be true.

Bernard Madoff reported steady investment returns for years and said he produced them with a strategy that involved trading options. In 2009 the Senate held a hearing on the failure of the Securities and Exchange Commission to identify his fraud. The testimony of the SEC's inspector general records that between June 1992 and December 2008 the agency received six substantive complaints.

One of them was submitted in versions in May 2000, March 2001, and October 2005. Another surfaced in April 2004, when SEC examiners found a series of internal emails at a different firm. Employees of that firm had done due diligence on their own Madoff investment using widely available information. Among the warning signs they listed was, in the words of the testimony, "his unusually consistent, non-volatile returns over several years." [^19]

One of those emails set out the inference step by step. Madoff could not be trading his options on an exchange, because the volume traded there was insufficient. He could not be trading them privately either, because it was inconceivable that he could find a counterparty. The reported strategy and the capacity of the market could not both be real. [^19]

No new fact about Madoff was needed to reach that conclusion. It came from setting his own claims against a known limit.

Eight years separate the first reported warning in 2000 from the public exposure in 2008. That figure is an oversight interval: it measures how long the warnings went unacted on, and it is not a discovery interval for every observer, since some observers had already drawn the conclusion. The exposure in 2008 confirmed the fraud. No single public statistic had proved it alone. [^19]

#### Contextual

Contextual latency is defined as "Previously known information whose relevance becomes apparent under different conditions or in a different domain." Here nothing was forgotten and nothing was unconnected within its own field. The knowledge was in active use. It had a second use in a field where nobody had looked for it.

In 1822 Joseph Fourier published his treatise on the conduction of heat. The heat equation and the methods for solving it were studied from then on. In 1973 Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published "The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities." They derived a differential equation for the value of an option and then changed variables. Of the result they wrote that "differential equation (10) is the heat-transfer equation of physics," and they cited a 1963 reference for its solution. [^20]

The mathematics that priced the option had been worked out for a different purpose. The reduction is exact under the assumptions of the model. It does not validate those assumptions, which include markets without friction, and it says nothing about trading profit. The 151 years between Fourier's treatise and the 1973 paper are a chronology of a method's transfer. They are not evidence that the option formula could have been known in 1822, when the problem it answers had not been posed in that form. [^20]

#### Representational

The fifth class is the newest. Representational latency is defined as "Structure or associations encoded in a computational representation, such as an embedding or trained model, that require elicitation and independent verification." A word embedding is a list of numbers assigned to each word in a body of text, computed so that words used in similar contexts receive similar lists.

In 2019 Tshitoyan and colleagues trained such embeddings on about 3.3 million abstracts from materials science and related fields. They gave the algorithm no chemistry. They then measured how close the embedding of each material's formula lay to the embedding of the word "thermoelectric," which names materials that convert heat to electricity. Some materials scored high although they had never appeared in an abstract with that word or with any other word that identifies a thermoelectric. The authors treated those materials as predictions. [^15]

As a first check, the authors compared their ranking with a database of thermoelectric properties computed from physical theory. The ten highest-ranked candidates had an average computed power factor of 40.8 μW K⁻² cm⁻¹, against 11.5 for candidate materials as a whole and 17.0 for materials already known as thermoelectrics. [^15]

The association was in no single abstract. It was in the geometry of the embedding, which summarized how millions of sentences used their words. The authors reported that the method could "recommend materials for functional applications several years before their discovery." [^21] The support is retrospective: enrichment in later literature and agreement with computed properties. It does not establish experimental performance for every material the ranking suggested. [^21]

The class definition ends with a condition the other four do not carry. Such structure must "require elicitation and independent verification." The numbers in an embedding do not state anything until someone asks them a question, and the answer is a lead.

#### More than one class at once

The founding text adds a sentence after the table.

> These classes are not exclusive. An excavation may recover an obscure observation, connect it to a separate field, and reveal its usefulness in a new technological context. [LIA §3]

That sentence walks through three classes in order. Recovering an obscure observation is archival. Connecting it to a separate field is relational. Finding its use in a new technological context is contextual.

Real cases combine classes in the same way. Zenon is archival and relational. The Madoff inference was also contextual, because the size of the options market, a fact from one setting, became decisive when applied to the claims of a fund. [^19] The Herculaneum scrolls from Part I are both archival and representational. The writing survived in the scrolls, and a trained model made it legible. [^4]

#### The care that models need

Section 3 closes with a caution.

> A model's ability to articulate a claim does not establish that the claim was encoded as truthful knowledge, nor that the claim is supported by its training data. Representational latency requires particular care because the original evidentiary trail may be inaccessible. [LIA §3]

Two separate things are denied here. A model producing a statement does not show that the model holds the statement as knowledge. And it does not show that the texts the model was trained on support it.

In 2023 Farquhar and colleagues at Google DeepMind tested methods that were meant to find what a language model knows by examining its internal activations, without labeled examples of truth. They proved that the consistency condition used by one leading method is satisfied by arbitrary features, and not only by knowledge. They then ran experiments. In one, they appended to each of a set of movie reviews a sentence giving the opinion of a character named Alice.

The method stopped classifying the sentiment of the review and classified Alice's opinion. It had found the most prominent feature in the activations. The authors state the scope of the result: it concerns the methods they tested and the problem of telling knowledge apart from other features. It is not a proof that latent knowledge can never be recovered from a model. [^22]

The second half of the caution concerns the trail. Swanson could list the articles behind each premise. A reader of the Zenon guide can go to the papyrus. When a claim comes out of a trained model, the documents that produced the association may be impossible to name. Part IV returns to what that requires of a system that uses models.

The five classes say how a thing can be hidden. The framing adds a second sorting, into four layers of latent information, and the next chapter takes it up.

### II-02. Four layers

In 2019, Olga Kononova and colleagues published a dataset of 19,488 synthesis entries they had extracted from 53,538 paragraphs on solid-state synthesis. A paragraph might name which compounds the researchers started with, what they did to them, at what temperature, for how long, and what they meant to make. Those details were written for someone reading about one experiment. Kononova's team put them in a common format so a researcher could look for the same starting compound or operation across papers. The team checked the extraction, while reproducing a recipe would take another experiment. [^7]

A reader can find an individual procedure in the article that reports it. To find all the published procedures that used a particular combination of starting compound and operation, the reader needs details from many articles. In both cases the details are already on the page. What changes is how much of the record a reader has to put together. [^7]

> There are four distinct layers of latent information. Google largely operates in the first layer. Deep research agents operate in the first two. The third and fourth layers are where LIA becomes particularly interesting. [Framing §1]

The framing's four layers sort findings by how far they are from being stated in the record. A finding's validity is a separate question. An explicit statement can be false, and an unstated connection can be well supported.

| Layer | Name | Definition | Who works here (David's placement) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Layer 1** | Stated and indexed | A claim that one artifact states outright, so a single lookup can return it. | Search engines; deep research agents. |
| **Layer 2** | Stated but scattered | A claim that no single artifact states, but whose parts are all written down across several artifacts, vocabularies or formats, so answering a question means collecting and assembling them. | Deep research agents. |
| **Layer 3** | Unstated connection | A relationship between artifacts, or encoded in a model trained on them, that no record states. Each side is on the record, the link between them is not, and the finding is the link itself: that A bears on B. | LIA. |
| **Layer 4** | Unstated implication | A claim, hypothesis, opportunity or question that goes beyond the link, which no artifact contains and which follows only once such connections are made. This includes questions nobody has asked. | LIA. |

![Four layers from stated and indexed to an unstated implication](figures/F2.png)

*Figure F2. Four layers of latent information, ordered by how far a finding is from being stated in the record.*

#### What lookup and assembly can do

At layer 1, one artifact gives the answer. Anyone looking for the number of synthesis entries can find it in Kononova's dataset paper. Finding the paper can still take work, especially if it is hard to access or uses unfamiliar terms. Once a reader finds the passage, the count is right there. [^7]

At layer 2, the answer takes several records. A researcher looking for all recipes that used a family of starting compounds to make a particular material needs the procedures from many papers. The dataset gathers the starting compounds, products, operations, and conditions so the researcher can compare them. The team checked how well it had extracted those 19,488 entries from the papers. Whether the 19,488 recipes would work again is a question for the lab. [^7]

A person can look up a statement, and a machine can gather scattered statements. If one artifact gives the answer, the finding is at layer 1. If the answer has to be assembled from facts already stated in several places, it is at layer 2. Gathering the recipes changes what a researcher can compare, even though every ingredient and operation was already on the page. [^7]

#### When the link is the finding

Swanson found papers about what fish oil did to the blood and a separate set about what blood changes might help people with Raynaud's syndrome. He saw that the effect reported in one literature matched a need reported in the other. That link between the fish-oil and Raynaud's literatures is layer 3. The further proposition, “fish oil may treat Raynaud's,” is layer 4. [^1]

The link can stand even if a treatment fails. Papers in two fields can bear on each other without establishing a therapy. For a layer-3 finding, name both sets of papers and show how one bears on the other. For a layer-4 finding, write down the claim that follows and what would test it. A reader needs both steps to see where the argument might fail. [^1][^23]

Arrowsmith helps readers inspect the link. In a two-node search, a researcher starts with two literatures, A and C, that usually have no papers in common. Arrowsmith filters for words and phrases, B, that appear in both sets of titles. It puts the A–B and B–C titles side by side so the researcher can judge whether B names something that matters to both. In a one-node search, the researcher starts with one literature and uses Arrowsmith to look for another that might bear on it. [^24]

A word in a title is there to be looked up at layer 1. Gathering the titles that contain it is layer 2. At layer 3, the question is whether the papers on either side support a relationship that no paper states. The titles give the reader a way back to the claims behind a proposed link. A therapeutic hypothesis drawn from that link belongs to layer-4 work and needs a test of its own. [^24][^1]

A layer-3 link can be enough to report. Two archives might turn out to hold independent accounts of the same event, or results in one field might rule out an explanation in another. Either finding has value before anyone proposes an intervention. If someone does propose one, the record needs to show how they got there and what evidence would settle it. [^1][^23]

A link can also be found in a model trained on the records. The previous chapter calls this representational latency and requires an independent check. A model can give a reader an association even when the reader cannot trace it to particular training documents. An unstated association is layer 3. A further claim drawn from it is layer 4. [LIA §3]

#### What the implication adds

In February 2020, Richardson and colleagues used a knowledge graph built from the literature to bring together facts about COVID-19 and the known properties of baricitinib. On that basis they proposed testing the drug as a treatment. The proposal is layer 4 because it goes beyond the links that led them to the drug. [^25]

In December 2020, the ACTT-2 trial reported a median recovery time of seven days with baricitinib plus remdesivir, against eight days with remdesivir alone, in the trial population. The trial supplies evidence for that combination in that population. The mechanisms in the original argument would need their own tests. The same implication can start as a candidate and acquire separate empirical support later. [^55]

Once the hypothesis has been published, a search can return it as a layer-1 statement. In February, when the researchers put the proposal together, the letter itself was outside the record they were working from. A later reader can look it up because it is now part of the record. The same claim can occupy different layers as the record changes. [LIA §4] [^25]

A question can also occupy layer 4. A connection can suggest an experiment nobody has thought to run. A layer-4 record gives the question, the link that led to it, and what the experiment would distinguish. Look for the question in the record first, and judge its novelty, provenance, and validity separately. [LIA §4, LIA §8]

#### Two maps of the same record

The five classes in the founding text ask how information is hidden. An overlooked statement can be archival and layer 1, and a set of statements gathered from scattered papers can be archival and layer 2. An unstated link can be relational or contextual and layer 3. A hypothesis drawn from that link is inferential and layer 4. [LIA §3]

Representational latency tells the reader where the information was found. A model can be the place where an investigator finds anything from a fact already on the page to a hypothesis the record has yet to state.

Kononova's team did extensive computational work, and the resulting recipes still belong in the first two layers. Swanson could state his link in a short sentence, and it belongs in the third because the relationship was absent from the papers he examined. The layer-4 hypothesis goes a step further. Its distance from the original statements says nothing about its odds of holding up in a trial. [^7][^1]

The framing places search mainly in the first layer, deep research agents in the first two, and LIA's particular interest in the third and fourth. The founding text calls for independent verification of associations elicited from models. [Framing §1, LIA §3]

Each step leaves something a reader can check. A lookup needs a source, and assembly needs a record of which statements came from which places. A connection needs evidence from both sides and has to survive a challenge. An implication needs its own test. Give the reader only the model's layer-4 claim and there is no way to check how it was reached. [^23]

The layers establish what sort of output is being sought. To decide whether that output was latent at a particular time, rather than already explicit in the available record, the inquiry needs a boundary around the corpus, a cutoff, and a method. That is the boundary the next chapter makes formal. [LIA §4]

<a id="chapter-ii-03"></a>

### II-03. The epistemic boundary

In 2019 Tshitoyan and colleagues at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the University of California, Berkeley, published an experiment that ran the same procedure eighteen times. They had collected about 3.3 million abstracts of scientific papers, published between 1922 and 2018, and had shown that word embeddings trained on them could rank materials by how closely each was associated with the word "thermoelectric." To find out whether such a ranking would have been of use in the past, they built eighteen historical versions of their collection.

Each held only the abstracts published before a cutoff year, and the cutoff years ran from 2001 to 2018. For each version they trained separate embeddings from the start. From each they took the 50 highest-ranked materials that had never yet appeared in an abstract alongside a thermoelectric keyword. [^15] [^21]

Then they read forward. For every year after each cutoff, they counted what share of those 50 materials had since been reported in the literature next to a thermoelectric keyword. Averaged over the eighteen runs, a material from a top-50 list was about eight times more likely to have been studied as a thermoelectric within the next five years than an unstudied material chosen at random from the collection as it stood at that date. It was about three times more likely than a random material with a non-zero computed bandgap, a property most thermoelectrics need. [^15]

They followed one cutoff in detail. Using only abstracts published before 2009, the five top-ranked predictions included CuGaTe2, which was reported in 2012 and which the authors count among the best thermoelectrics of the present day. Two more, ReS2 and CdIn2Te4, were proposed as good thermoelectrics in the literature some eight to nine years after the date at which the method would first have placed them in the top five. The last two, HgZnTe and SmInO3, contain expensive or toxic elements and had not been studied. [^15]


![Recoverable candidates relative to a corpus, date and method](figures/F4.png)

#### The parts of the experiment

The design has four parts, and each can be named.

The first is a collection of documents with a date on it: all the abstracts published before a given year. The second is a method: the embedding algorithm, the choice of the target word, and the rule for ranking. The third is the list of everything the method puts forward from that collection. The fourth is a record of what had already been said, which the authors used to remove from the list every material that had already appeared with a thermoelectric keyword. What remained after that removal was their set of predictions. [^15]

The fourth section of the founding text, "The epistemic boundary," gives those four parts symbols.

> Let `C(t)` be a defined corpus containing artifacts available at or before time `t`. Let `E(t)` denote the claims explicitly documented within a specified reference record by that time. Let `M` denote a specified set of retrieval, reasoning, and analytical methods.
>
> Define `H(C(t), M)` as the candidate claims recoverable or constructible from the corpus using those methods.
>
> A working discovery space is:
>
> ```text
> L(C, t, M) = H(C(t), M) \ E(t)
> ```
>
> [LIA §4]

Read against the experiment, the symbols fall into place. `C(t)` is the set of abstracts published before the cutoff year `t`. `M` is the embedding and the ranking.

`H(C(t), M)` is what that method can put forward from that corpus: materials ranked by their association with the target word. `E(t)` is the set of claims already documented by that time, here the materials already reported as thermoelectrics. The backslash is set subtraction. `L(C, t, M)` is what is left: the candidates the method produces from the corpus that the reference record does not already contain. [^15]

One instance makes this concrete. Take `t` as 2009. `C(t)` is every abstract in the collection published before that year.

The method, applied to that corpus, ranks CuGaTe2 close to the target word, so the claim that CuGaTe2 is a promising thermoelectric is in `H(C(t), M)`. No abstract before 2009 had reported the material alongside a thermoelectric keyword, so the claim is not in `E(t)`. It is therefore in `L(C, t, M)`. The first published report came in 2012. [^15]

Each definition carries a qualifying word that does work. The corpus is "defined": someone has to say what is in it. The reference record is "specified": someone has to say which record counts as what is already documented.

The methods are "specified" too. Nothing in the formula can be computed, even in principle, until those three choices have been made and written down. In the experiment they were: a list of journals and a date, a list of nine keywords that mark a material as already studied, and a published algorithm. [^15]

The word "candidate" in the definition of `H` is also chosen with care. `H` holds claims that are "recoverable or constructible." A recoverable claim is one that is in the corpus and can be found. A constructible claim is one the method builds from what is in the corpus. Neither word says the claim is true.

#### What the subtraction means

Two sentences follow the formula in the founding text, and both limit it.

> The subtraction is semantic rather than literal: paraphrasing an existing claim does not make it novel. This is a conceptual model, not an assertion that the sets are fully enumerable or that membership can always be established. [LIA §4]

The first sentence closes an easy loophole. If `E(t)` were a list of exact sentences, any claim could be made to look new by rewording it. The subtraction removes meanings.

A claim already documented in other words is in `E(t)` and comes out. The experiment handled this for one narrow kind of claim by matching nine different keywords, from "thermoelectric" to "seebeck" and "thermopower," so that a material studied under any of those names counted as already known. [^15] For claims in general, deciding that two sentences say the same thing is a judgment, and the founding text does not pretend otherwise.

The second sentence states what kind of object the formula is. It is a conceptual model. Nobody can list every claim constructible from 3.3 million abstracts, and for many claims nobody can say with certainty whether the record already holds them. The formula fixes what has to be specified before the word "latent" means anything definite. It does not promise a count.

The founding text then draws out what follows.

> Three important consequences follow:
>
> - Latency is relative to a corpus, a historical boundary, and a method of investigation.
> - A candidate in the discovery space is not necessarily true. The space includes unsupported conjectures and erroneous inferences.
> - Novelty is relative to a documented reference record. The absence of an earlier publication cannot prove that no person previously knew a claim.
>
> [LIA §4]

#### First consequence: corpus, boundary, method

`L` has three arguments, and changing any one of them changes what is in it.

Change the corpus. The same authors trained the same algorithm on the full set of Wikipedia articles, about ten times more text than their collection of abstracts, and it performed substantially worse on materials-science analogies. They also found that discarding abstracts unrelated to inorganic materials improved performance. What can be recovered depends on what is in `C`, and more is not always better. [^15]

Change the method. With the embeddings left as they were and only the target word replaced, the authors ran the same historical test for "photovoltaics," "topological insulator," and "ferroelectric," and reported similar trends for all three. A different question put to the same corpus gives a different set of candidates. [^15]

Change the time. This is the argument that most needs care, and a case from finance shows what care looks like. In 2008 Cohen and Frazzini published a study of firms linked as customer and supplier. Accounting rules require a firm to report the identity of any customer that represents more than 10 percent of its sales, so the links were public.

Cohen and Frazzini matched the named customers to traded companies and assembled 11,484 unique supplier-customer relationships between 1980 and 2004. In their linked sample the average customer accounted for 20 percent of the supplier's sales. Their own illustration is Coastcast Corporation, a manufacturer of golf club heads whose major customer since 1993 had been Callaway Golf. As of 2001 Callaway accounted for 50 percent of Coastcast's sales, and on June 8, 2001, Callaway lowered its revenue projection for the second quarter from $300 million to $250 million. [^26] The link between the two firms had been in public filings for years before that news arrived.

The authors asked whether news about a customer was reflected promptly in the stock price of its supplier, and they reported that it was not: supplier returns predictably followed customer returns. [^26]

A test like that is worthless if it uses a link before the market could have known of it. A relationship reported for a fiscal year becomes public only when the statements for that year are issued, which is after the year ends. So the authors imposed a six-month gap between fiscal year-end dates and the stock returns they examined, to ensure, as they put it, that the relations were known before the returns they were used to explain. [^26] That gap is a rule for `t`. It says an artifact counts as part of `C(t)` only from the date it was in fact available, and that the date printed on it can be earlier.

The study is retrospective and observational. It is not a prospective test, it does not prove cause, and it is not evidence of trading profit available today. [^26]

The Berkeley group and the two finance authors could enforce the boundary because they controlled what went into the analysis. A large language model makes that harder. A 2024 paper in the Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics observes that the training data of such models are vast and drawn from wide-ranging sources, so that they could explicitly or implicitly include the very data a model is later tested on. It adds that detection is difficult, because the training data are opaque and access to the models is black-box. The paper proposes a way to detect such contamination from a model's outputs. [^27]

A detection method is not a guarantee. When the method `M` is a model whose training text nobody can inspect, `t` has moved to wherever that training stopped, and nobody chose it. The corpus the investigator defined and the corpus the method has in fact seen are then two different things.

#### Second consequence: a candidate is not necessarily true

The second consequence is stated flatly: "The space includes unsupported conjectures and erroneous inferences."

The top five from the 2009 cutoff show the range. One became a leading thermoelectric. Two were later proposed as good ones. Two had not been studied, and one of those, SmInO3, fell appreciably in the ranking as more abstracts were added. [^15]

All five were in `L` for that corpus, cutoff, and method. Their being there settled nothing about whether they work.

Even the successes were scored against a particular yardstick. The endpoint of the historical test was whether a material later appeared in an abstract beside a thermoelectric keyword. That is a record of later attention from researchers. It is not a laboratory measurement of each predicted material. [^15]

This design has a name in the field Swanson started, literature-based discovery. Thilakaratne and colleagues, in a 2019 systematic review, call it time-sliced evaluation. The data are divided at a cut-off date.

The method is run on the earlier segment. A gold standard, meaning the list of correct answers the output is scored against, is built from associations that are present after the cut-off and absent before it. The reviewers rate it the most objective evaluation technique the field has, because it scores the whole output and does not depend on knowing the answer in advance. They also report that evaluation remains an open problem and that the field has no comprehensive gold standard datasets. [^23]

The reason bears directly on the second consequence. A gold standard made from later publications contains only what someone went on to publish. A candidate that is true and was never followed up is scored as a miss.

A candidate that was followed up and co-mentioned is scored as a hit whether or not the result held. The score measures agreement with the later record. Truth is a separate matter. [^23]

#### Third consequence: novelty is relative to a record

The third consequence is the one most often overlooked, and the second sentence of it is exact: "The absence of an earlier publication cannot prove that no person previously knew a claim."

`E(t)` is a record. It is whatever has been specified as the reference: a journal database, a patent register, the holdings of a library. It is not the contents of every mind. A claim can be missing from `E(t)` and present in a laboratory notebook, a conversation, or the working knowledge of a trade.

The dependence on a record runs the other way as well. A result can be new in one respect when the larger fact was already on the record. The Antikythera mechanism was recovered in 1901.

In 2006 Freeth and colleagues published a reconstruction of its gear functions from new X-ray tomography, and reported inscriptions not deciphered before. The 105 years between those two dates are the interval from recovery to that specific reconstruction. They are not the interval to first recognition, because earlier scholars had already recognized the object as an astronomical mechanism. [^17] What was new in 2006 was new relative to the record of 2005: particular gear trains, particular lines of text. Stating the novelty of the 2006 work correctly means naming that record.

So a claim of novelty has the same structure as a claim of latency. It needs a named record and a date. Chapter II-05 returns to this.

#### What the boundary does not decide

The section ends with one sentence.

> A separate validation process determines what epistemic status each candidate warrants. [LIA §4]

The formula marks out a space of candidates. It says what would have to be specified for a claim to count as latent, and it says nothing about which candidates are true, which are trivial, and which cannot be resolved. That work belongs to validation, and the founding text keeps it separate on purpose. The next chapter takes up the line between a candidate and a discovery.

### II-04. Discovery and invention

On February 4, 2020, the Lancet published a letter from a group at BenevolentAI in London. The disease it concerned had been known for weeks and still carried a provisional name, 2019-nCoV acute respiratory disease. The authors called their company's knowledge graph "a large repository of structured medical information, including numerous connections extracted from scientific literature by machine learning." They had searched it for approved drugs that might block the process by which the virus infects cells. [^25]

Their reasoning ran through a protein. Most viruses enter cells by receptor-mediated endocytosis, and one known regulator of endocytosis is an enzyme called AAK1. The graph held 378 inhibitors of AAK1. Of these, 47 had been approved for medical use, and six inhibited the enzyme with high affinity.

Several of the six were cancer drugs with serious side effects, which the authors set aside. One was baricitinib, a janus kinase inhibitor already approved for medical use. It also binds a second regulator of endocytosis, and its concentration in the blood at the ordinary dose of 2 or 4 milligrams once daily is enough to inhibit AAK1. "We identified baricitinib," they wrote, "which is predicted to reduce the ability of the virus to infect lung cells." They suggested that it could be trialled. [^25]

No patient had been given the drug for this disease. Nothing in the letter was a measurement of the new virus. Every premise was a fact already recorded about a drug, an enzyme, or the way viruses enter cells.

In December 2020 the New England Journal of Medicine published the results of ACTT-2, a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial. It randomized 1,033 hospitalized adults. All received the antiviral remdesivir, and they received either baricitinib or a placebo with it. Patients given baricitinib had a median time to recovery of 7 days, against 8 days for the control group. Among patients who were on high-flow oxygen or noninvasive ventilation when they enrolled, the figures were 10 days and 18 days. [^55]

Those are two events with two dates, about ten months apart. The first produced a candidate from existing information. The second tested it on patients. The fifth section of the founding text is about the difference between them.

#### What counts as a discovery from the record

The section opens by refusing a narrow reading of what the record can yield.

> LIA does not require that discoveries be logically entailed by the historical record. Some are deductive consequences; others are statistical patterns, explanatory hypotheses, analogies, or proposed transfers between domains. [LIA §5]

A claim is logically entailed when it must be true if the premises are. The founding text names that kind and then four others that carry no such guarantee. The cases in this part supply one of each.

A deductive consequence: the email that showed Madoff's stated options trading was impossible. If the exchange volume was insufficient and no private counterparty could exist, the trades as reported did not happen. Given those premises the conclusion follows. [^19]

A statistical pattern: the ranking of materials by their closeness to the word "thermoelectric." No sentence entails that a highly ranked material will work. The ranking is a regularity in how millions of abstracts use words, and its worth was shown by how often the ranked materials were later studied. [^21]

An explanatory hypothesis: the proposal, made by the Co-Scientist system presented in a 2026 Nature paper, that a class of mobile genetic elements in bacteria spreads between species by interacting with the tails of diverse phages. A hypothesis of this kind is offered because it would explain an observation, here the broad host range of those elements. [^28]

An analogy: Black and Scholes observing that their equation for an option's value, after a change of variables, is the heat-transfer equation of physics. Two problems with no subject matter in common had the same form. [^20]

A proposed transfer between domains: baricitinib, a drug approved for another purpose, put forward for a viral pneumonia. [^25]

Only the first of the five is safe from being wrong once its premises are granted. The other four can each fail, and section 4 already said that the discovery space includes erroneous inferences. Section 5 includes all four among the possibilities the record can yield.

#### Where the evidence came from

The next sentence of the section draws the line that gives it its title.

> The distinction is the origin of the evidentiary substrate, not whether the investigator employed creativity. [LIA §5]

The evidentiary substrate is the material a claim rests on. The sentence says that what sets an archaeological finding apart from other intellectual work is that this material already existed. It explicitly denies that the difference lies in creativity.

Assembling a candidate from the record can take as much invention as any other kind of work. The BenevolentAI authors had to decide which protein to reason through, which of six drugs to set aside for their side effects, and whether an ordinary dose would reach the needed concentration. None of those steps was mechanical. [^25]

A patent shows this plainly. In 1984 Charles Hull filed for what became United States patent 4,575,330, published in 1986, titled "Apparatus for production of three-dimensional objects by stereolithography." The patent defines its own coined word as "the application of lithographic techniques to production of three-dimensional objects." Its background section lists what was already known.

Many liquid chemicals could be made to harden into solid plastic by ultraviolet light, and such chemicals were then in use as ink for high-speed printing, in coatings for paper, and as adhesives. Computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing are called important and rapidly growing technologies. The stated object of the invention is "to harness the principles of computer generated graphics, combined with UV curable plastic and the like," so as to produce three-dimensional objects directly from computer instructions. [^29]

Every ingredient was drawn from an existing field, and the patent says so in its own text. The combination was Hull's. The law calls the result an invention, and the word is fair.

Hull drew on existing knowledge and brought its parts together in a method for building an object layer by layer. The grant of a patent documents the apparatus and the claims. It is not proof of broad commercial performance, and it does not show that no one had seen the combination before the filing. [^29]

Hull's patent illustrates a distinction relevant to excavation. The origin of the evidence is one question. What the inventor made by combining it is another.

#### Candidate and validated discovery

The third paragraph of the section gives two terms.

> A useful boundary is between an **archaeological candidate**, generated from existing information, and a **validated discovery**, which has survived the appropriate tests. A candidate may require new experiments before it becomes a discovery about the physical world. [LIA §5]

The definitions are short. A candidate is defined by its source: existing information. A validated discovery is defined by what it has come through: the appropriate tests.

The word "appropriate" leaves open what the tests are, because they differ by the kind of claim. A claim about a drug needs a trial. A claim about a text needs readers who can check the reading.

The fish-oil case shows the boundary and also shows that crossing it changes the claim. In 1986 Swanson published the hypothesis that dietary fish oil might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome. It rested on two literatures that had no articles in common. It was an archaeological candidate in the exact sense: generated from existing information, tested on no one. [^2]

The Prologue introduced the 1989 fish-oil trial by DiGiacomo, Kremer, and Shah. Its double-blind, controlled, prospective design put the therapeutic hypothesis to a clinical test. They randomized thirty-two patients with primary or secondary Raynaud's phenomenon to fish oil or to an olive-oil placebo. Each patient took twelve capsules a day. The patients were examined at the start and after six, twelve, and seventeen weeks, with a finger placed in water baths cooled in steps to 10°C while blood flow and pressure in it were measured.

An attack was recorded when flow in the finger stopped. Raynaud's is called primary when it occurs alone and secondary when it accompanies another disease. In the fish-oil group the median time before an attack began rose from 31.3 minutes at the start to 46.5 minutes at six weeks. The authors concluded that fish oil benefited patients with the primary form and not those with the secondary form. In the primary group, five of eleven patients taking fish oil could no longer have an attack induced at the six-week or twelve-week visit, against one of nine taking the placebo. [^3]

Swanson's candidate had been stated for Raynaud's patients in general. What survived the test was narrower: an effect in primary Raynaud's, in one trial of thirty-two people. The trial does not validate every mechanism Swanson's argument proposed. [^3] The test confirmed part of the candidate and marked where the rest stopped. A candidate that passes its test often comes out smaller than it went in.

The same care applies to what counts as a test. The Co-Scientist paper reports candidates checked at different levels. For acute myeloid leukaemia, the system proposed existing drugs for repurposing, and the authors report that these were validated through in vitro experiments, meaning in cultured cells. One compound that the paper calls a completely novel candidate, an inhibitor named KIRA6, was tested in several leukaemia cell lines and in a non-malignant control line.

The authors report an 18-fold separation between its effect on one leukaemia line and its effect on the control, and they call that a promising in vitro therapeutic window. For liver fibrosis, the system proposed epigenetic targets, and the reported activity was observed in human hepatic organoids, which are small laboratory-grown models of liver tissue. [^28]

Each of those is a real test, and each supports a statement at its own level: this compound kills these cells in a dish, this target shows this activity in an organoid. Neither in vitro activity nor organoid activity establishes clinical efficacy. [^28] A candidate is validated as far as its test reached. Baricitinib was tested in a randomized trial of more than a thousand patients, and the finding it supports is specific too: that regimen, in that population, shortened recovery. The same trial reported 28-day mortality of 5.1 percent with the combination and 7.8 percent in the control group, with a hazard ratio for death of 0.65 and a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.39 to 1.09. [^55]

An interval that includes 1 is compatible with no difference, so the trial supports the claim about recovery time and leaves the claim about deaths open. It also does not test every mechanism the original letter proposed. The letter argued from blocked viral entry and from reduced inflammation. The trial measured how patients fared and could not say which of the two did the work. [^25]

#### Two activities

The section ends with two sentences that assign the work.

> In that case, LIA identified the research opportunity; experimental science established its empirical validity.
>
> Neither activity replaces the other. [LIA §5]

"In that case" refers to a candidate that needs new experiments. The division is exact for baricitinib. The search of the knowledge graph found the opportunity: one drug, out of hundreds of inhibitors, worth giving to patients. ACTT-2, run by other people under a different discipline, established what happened when patients received it. [^25]

The last sentence cuts both ways. The record cannot substitute for the experiment. However well a candidate is argued from prior literature, the argument does not tell anyone how patients will respond, and the fish-oil trial showed a candidate being narrowed by its test. [^3]

The experiment cannot substitute for the record either. A trial tests the candidates that someone has proposed, and it can only be run on a question that has been asked. The knowledge graph alone held 47 approved inhibitors of a single enzyme. The letter gave a reason to try one of them and reasons to leave others aside. [^25]

The founding text therefore places LIA beside experimental science and does not rank them. One works on what has been recorded and produces candidates. The other produces new observations and decides what the candidates are worth. The next chapter takes up a question the boundary leaves open. A candidate can be new or already known, and that is a separate matter from whether it is true.

### II-05. New to whom, and as of when

A research group studying antibiotic resistance had a question and, privately, an answer. The question concerned a class of mobile genetic elements in bacteria called capsid-forming phage-inducible chromosomal islands, or cf-PICIs. These elements carry genes for virulence and for antibiotic resistance, and they are found across diverse bacterial species, including *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae*.

The puzzle was how they come to exist in so many different species. The group had worked on it at the bench and had found a mechanism. They had not yet published it. [^28]

They gave the question to Co-Scientist, a system built at Google on a large language model. The 2026 Nature paper that reports the system says the researchers "instructed the system to explore a topic their group had independently discovered, but not yet published."

The system was given only minimal background information. It worked for two days and returned a ranked list of hypotheses. The one it ranked first was that cf-PICIs interact with diverse phage tails to expand their host range. According to the paper, that proposal mirrored the researchers' unpublished experimental findings, which were set out in reports released at the same time as the paper. [^28]

Whether the hypothesis was new depends on where one looks. The published literature did not contain it, so a search of that literature on the day would have found nothing. The people who had asked the question already knew it. One hypothesis, on one day, was absent from one record and present in another.

#### The sentence in section 4

The founding text anticipated the case in the third consequence of its formal boundary, which the chapter before last quoted.

> Novelty is relative to a documented reference record. The absence of an earlier publication cannot prove that no person previously knew a claim. [LIA §4]

In the cf-PICI case the second sentence is literally true. No earlier publication existed, and persons did previously know the claim.

The case is unusual only because the people who knew were the ones running the test, so the knowledge is documented. Ordinarily it is not. A search of the published record can show that a claim is missing from the published record. It can show nothing about notebooks, unpublished reports, or what a specialist would have said if asked.

The researchers set the test up this way on purpose. Because they held the answer and the literature did not, a match could not be the system repeating a published result. The paper calls the outcome a recapitulation of a then-unpublished discovery, and that word is accurate. Relative to the literature the hypothesis was novel. Relative to the laboratory it was a rediscovery. [^28]

#### An interval that measured approval

Dates invite a second mistake. When a claim appears on the record at one date and is acted on at a much later date, the gap looks like a period of latency. Sometimes it is something else.

Thalidomide was a sedative withdrawn after it caused severe birth defects. In 1965 a therapeutic response to the drug was reported in erythema nodosum leprosum, or ENL, a painful inflammatory complication of leprosy. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration granted marketing approval for thalidomide for the skin manifestations of ENL in 1998. A published regulatory history says the approval was "based on historical data," [^30] and the agency's orphan-drug listing gives the approval date as July 16, 1998. [^56]

Thirty-three years separate the two dates. They are not thirty-three years in which nobody saw the connection. The therapeutic connection was recognized in 1965, when it was reported.

What took until 1998 was regulatory approval in one country for a drug with a catastrophic history. The interval measures regulation. It is not an estimate of efficacy and not a general endorsement of the drug's safety. One caution belongs with the case: the regulatory history used here is a secondary source, and the case is flagged in the research ledger as resting on weaker sourcing than the others in this part. [^30]

Asking "new as of when?" therefore requires a second question: which event does each date mark? A report, a recognition, an approval, and a first use are four different events. Subtracting one date from another gives a number in every case. Only some of those numbers measure how long something lay unrecognized.

#### A connection its authors did not call new

A third case shows the honest handling of novelty from the inside. In 1998 Lawrence Page, Sergey Brin, and two colleagues at Stanford wrote a technical report, "The PageRank Citation Ranking: Bringing Order to the Web." It proposed ranking web pages by the structure of the links between them. Their estimate of the web at the time was over 150 million pages, and they reported computing the ranking over as many as 518 million hyperlinks. [^31]

The idea had an evident ancestor. Scholars had long counted citations to judge the importance of academic papers, and a link from one web page to another resembles a citation. The authors said so. "It is obvious," they wrote, "to try to apply standard citation analysis techniques to the web's hypertextual citation structure. One can simply think of every link as being like an academic citation." And in the first section of the paper: "there is already a large literature on academic citation analysis." [^31]

They then stated where the web differs. Academic papers are scrupulously reviewed, while web pages "proliferate free of quality control or publishing costs." A simple program can create huge numbers of pages and inflate citation counts artificially. Simple counting also ignores who is doing the citing: a single link from a major page should count for more than many links from obscure places. Their method weighted each link by the rank of the page it came from. [^31]

The paper claims a method and its application to the web. It does not claim to have discovered that citations carry information about importance, and it names the prior literature in its own text. The source documents the comparison and the ranking algorithm. It does not establish any direct line of descent from earlier work in other fields, such as input-output models in economics, and none is claimed here. [^31]

The founding text asks for exactly this behavior in its third principle.

> **Novelty is investigated, not presumed.** Rediscovery is valuable, but must not be misrepresented as original discovery. [LIA §6]

#### Two axes

Section 8 of the founding text proposes a record format for findings and a set of statuses. One sentence in it carries the point of this chapter.

> Suggested statuses are `candidate`, `corroborated`, `validated`, `rediscovered`, `rejected`, and `unresolved`. Novelty and validity are separate axes: a rediscovery can be true, and a novel candidate can be false. A mature standard should record those dimensions independently. [LIA §8]

There are two questions about any claim drawn from the record. Is it new, relative to a named record at a named date? Is it true, as far as the appropriate tests have reached? The answers are independent. The cases in this part illustrate three of the possible combinations.

New and supported. The 2009 thermoelectric ranking put CuGaTe2 in its top five when no abstract had yet reported it as a thermoelectric, and the material was reported as a good one in 2012. [^15] Fish oil for Raynaud's was absent from both literatures in 1986 and found support in a trial in 1989, for the primary form of the condition. [^2]

New and not established. From the same 2009 list, HgZnTe and SmInO3 were equally new and had not been studied. Their newness is not evidence in their favor. [^15]

Not new and true. The cf-PICI hypothesis was a rediscovery relative to the laboratory that posed it, and it matched that laboratory's experimental findings. [^28] The 2006 reconstruction of the Antikythera mechanism added gear functions and inscriptions to an astronomical reading that earlier scholars had already reached. [^17]

A familiar claim can also remain unestablished when nobody has tested it.

The six statuses record positions on these axes. `candidate`, `corroborated`, and `validated` mark how far testing has gone. `rejected` marks a test that went against the claim. `unresolved` marks a claim that could not be settled.

`rediscovered` is a statement about novelty: the claim was already on some record. The founding text does not treat it as a failure. A rediscovery made from the record alone shows that the method can find things that are real. It also gives the earlier discoverer their due.

The section asks for the two dimensions to be recorded independently because a single label hides one of them. A finding marked only "rediscovered" says nothing about whether it is true. A finding marked only "validated" says nothing about whether anyone knew it before. The proposed record has separate fields for the two: `novelty_check`, which holds the scope, methods, and results of the search for prior statements, and `validation`, which holds the tests and their results. [LIA §8]

#### The clock

Both axes depend on a date, and the date has become harder to hold.

The oldest test in literature-based discovery is replication. A system is given only the literature published before a known discovery and is checked for whether it finds the same connection. Thilakaratne and colleagues report that this is by far the most commonly used evaluation technique in the field, and that the most popular targets are Swanson's first two discoveries, fish oil for Raynaud's and magnesium for migraine. They also list its weaknesses.

The original discoveries were never developed rigorously as a gold standard. And a system tuned to reproduce one famous case may perform well on that case and poorly elsewhere, which is overfitting. Their example is Arrowsmith, which had been evaluated by replicating known discoveries and failed to recognize the links in a third Swanson case, between somatomedin-C and arginine. [^23]

Replication was at least clean with respect to time when the system was a program reading a dated set of abstracts. The input could be cut at 1985, and nothing later could get in.

A large language model does not read a dated set. It has been trained on text gathered up to some recent point, and that text includes the later papers, the reviews, and the textbook accounts of the very discoveries used as tests. The contamination paper cited in chapter II-03 states the difficulty: the training data could explicitly or implicitly include test data, and the opacity of that data and black-box access to the models make contamination hard to detect. Its authors report finding a high potential for contamination in one widely used model on one standard benchmark. [^27]

A model asked today to connect fish oil and Raynaud's from pre-1986 literature may simply have read that the connection exists. Its answer is no evidence that the connection was recoverable from the 1985 record by that method. The same is true, less visibly, of any historical case a model might have seen discussed.

That is why the cf-PICI test was built on an unpublished result. It is one way of holding the clock still: choose a claim that cannot be in the training text because it is in no text. The seventh principle of the founding text states the general rule.

> **Time is an evidentiary constraint.** Claims about what could have been discovered earlier must exclude information unavailable at the historical cutoff, including information introduced through model training or retrieval. [LIA §6]

The last clause names the two routes by which later information gets in: what a model learned in training and what it fetches at the time of the question. The proposed record gives each source an `available_at` field for "date or documented uncertainty," so the date of each piece of evidence is written down with it. [LIA §8]

Part II has set out what is buried. There are five ways a thing can be latent. There is a formal boundary that says latent relative to which corpus, which date, and which method.

There is a line between a candidate drawn from the record and a discovery that has survived its tests. And novelty and validity are separate axes, each needing a named record and a date. Part III turns to the cases at length, field by field. Part IV returns to the clock and to what it takes to test an archaeological claim without knowing the answer in advance.

## Part III: Excavations

<a id="chapter-iii-01"></a>

### III-01. Swanson and undiscovered public knowledge

In April 1986 *The Library Quarterly*, a journal for librarians published by the University of Chicago Press, carried a sixteen-page paper by Don R. Swanson of the university's Graduate Library School. Its title was "Undiscovered Public Knowledge."

The paper is mostly philosophy and information retrieval. It sets out a view of scientific knowledge taken from Karl Popper, argues that no literature search can ever be shown to be complete, and builds three examples to show how knowledge can be in print and still be undiscovered. One of the three contains a medical hypothesis: that dietary fish oil might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome, a disorder in which the fingertips and toes blanch in episodes. A footnote says that a fuller account of that example is to appear in a biomedical journal, where it will reach readers in a position to criticize the argument. [^1]

It appeared the same year. The bibliographic record gives the title as "Fish oil, Raynaud's syndrome, and undiscovered public knowledge," in *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine*, volume 30, pages 7 to 18. [^32] The Prologue told the fish-oil example as Swanson gave it in the library paper. This chapter takes the rest: the argument that surrounds the example, the model he later made of it, the trial, and the second case.

The founding text and the framing both put this work first in their lists of precedents.

> Don R. Swanson demonstrated how separately developed bodies of biomedical literature could support a previously unrecognized hypothesis. His 1986 work on fish oil and Raynaud's syndrome is a foundational precedent. [LIA §9]

> Don Swanson connected two separate bodies of medical literature to hypothesize a previously unrecognized application of fish oil. [Framing §2]

Both sentences are about the medical case. The library paper is where Swanson explained why such a case should exist at all, and why there should be more of them.

#### The argument in the library paper

Swanson starts from Popper's account of science. In 1934 Popper published *Logik der Forschung*, translated into English in 1959 as *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, and Swanson takes two things from it. The first is that theories are conjectures, which can be tested and criticized and never proved true.

The second is a distinction between knowledge in a person's mind and knowledge in the record. Popper divided reality into three worlds: World 1 is the physical world, World 2 is the world of mental states, and World 3 is the world of the products of the human mind, such as problems and theories, once they are recorded. Swanson uses the word "knowledge" for the third unless he says otherwise. [^1]

World 3, in this account, is made by people and still holds things no person has seen. Swanson repeats Popper's example of the prime numbers: people invented the number system, and the primes followed as a consequence nobody intended, waiting to be found. He then makes his own claim, that large areas of World 3 are undiscovered for one reason only, which is the limited ability to index, organize, and retrieve what has been published. His opening image is a puzzle whose pieces were made by different people for different purposes. In his words, "independently created pieces of knowledge can harbor an unseen, unknown, and unintended pattern." [^1]

The three examples are three shapes such a pattern can have. The first is a hidden refutation, and it is imaginary. Suppose a theory says that all swans are white. Suppose that reliable observers in a remote place publish, in a local wildlife journal, an incidental remark about a family of black swans, without knowing that anyone holds the theory, and that indexers pass over the remark. The theory is then refuted in print, and the theory and its refutation are known together to no one. [^1]

The second example is a missing link. One report says that A causes B. A separate report, by authors unaware of the first, says that B causes C. The proposition that A causes C then exists as a hypothesis, and it can be tested or built on only when one person capable of seeing the implication knows both reports. This is where Swanson places fish oil and Raynaud's syndrome, as a live instance of the abstract form. For each premise he cites five journal articles, which he says were selected from a larger number. [^1]

The third example is the cumulative strength of weak tests. A theory can be supported by a series of independent tests, each of which is unconvincing alone because other explanations fit the same evidence. Taken together they can be much stronger than any one of them, and that combined strength is recognized only if someone brings them together. Swanson's instance is smoking and lung cancer. He says there was a period, many decades before he wrote, when case-control studies, mortality statistics, and animal experiments each bore on the hypothesis and no article had assembled them, so that the degree to which the hypothesis had already withstood testing was itself undiscovered. [^1]

The second half of the paper explains why these situations are permanent. A search retrieves documents by attributes such as title words and index terms. Any request for everything relevant to a theory assumes that some combination of those attributes will find it all.

Swanson treats that assumption as a universal hypothesis and applies Popper to it. It cannot be verified, because verifying it would mean inspecting everything ever published, and more is published while the inspection goes on. It can be refuted by a single relevant document that lacks the attributes. A search is therefore a conjecture about the contents of World 3 in the way a theory is a conjecture about World 1. It can be criticized and improved and never certified. [^1]

Two practical points follow in the paper. Searching should be done in stages, each search revised in light of what the last one returned. And the structure of unintended connections is orderly enough to study. He adds, in a final footnote, that finding new knowledge in the literature was neither unprecedented nor unusual. His proposal was that the logic of it be examined. [^1]

#### The case restated as a model

The 1986 medical paper is held for this book as a bibliographic record without an abstract, so nothing here is drawn from its text. [^32] What follows on the fish-oil case comes from Swanson's own later accounts.

In a report published in the *Bulletin of the Medical Library Association*, he summarized the analysis this way. One set of articles showed that dietary fish oils lead to certain blood and vascular changes. A second set contained evidence that similar changes might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome. Then comes the observation on which the case rests: "these two literatures had no articles in common." They had never been cited together, and neither mentioned the other or suggested that fish oil might help these patients. [^2]

Ten years after the library paper, Swanson and Neil Smalheiser, of the Department of Pediatrics at Chicago, restated the idea as a model with two conditions. Two literatures are complementary if, considered together, they reveal useful information that is apparent in neither alone. They are noninteractive if they do not cite each other and are not cited together by other papers. Where both conditions hold, it is possible that nobody has considered the two together.

The authors labeled the parts with letters. One literature associates A with B, another associates B with C, and the reader is led to an unreported association between A and C. For fish oil, A, and Raynaud's, C, they give one B-linkage as an example: dietary eicosapentaenoic acid can decrease blood viscosity, and abnormally high blood viscosity had been reported in patients with Raynaud's. [^33]

The model's claims are bounded. Whether an A-to-C connection has occurred privately to someone is unknowable. The test they apply is whether there is printed evidence to the contrary, because their subject is the state of the record and not the state of anyone's mind. And joining the two literatures is not offered as proof that A affects C. Its purpose is to call attention to an apparently unnoticed association that is worth investigating. [^33]

#### The trial

The first clinical test was published in 1989 by DiGiacomo, Kremer, and Shah. The Prologue gave its design and Chapter II-04 its main results: 32 patients with primary or secondary Raynaud's, randomly assigned to fish oil or to an olive-oil placebo, with benefit concluded for the primary form and not the secondary. [^3]

The abstract adds detail that fixes the size of the claim. The fish-oil patients took 12 capsules a day containing a total of 3.96 g of eicosapentaenoic acid and 2.64 g of docosahexaenoic acid. Between weeks 12 and 17 every patient, in both groups, took olive oil.

The rise in median time to onset in the fish-oil group, from 31.3 to 46.5 minutes at six weeks, is reported with p = 0.04. The comparison in the primary subgroup, five of 11 against one of nine patients in whom an episode could no longer be induced, is reported with p = 0.05. In the 10°C water bath, mean systolic pressure in the finger was 32 mm Hg higher in primary patients on fish oil than in primary patients on olive oil, with p = 0.02. [^3]

The abstract also gives the trialists' own reason for the study. It says that omega-3 fatty acids could benefit these patients because, among other effects, they "induce a favorable vascular response to ischemia." It does not mention blood viscosity, platelet aggregation, or Swanson. [^3] That the trial was reported independently, by others, is Swanson's statement. His retrospective accounts count two years from his analysis to the trial. [^2] [^33]

By year of publication the dates are 1986 and 1989: three calendar years from explicit hypothesis to the first reported trial, a validation interval. How long the antecedent literature had been available before 1986 is not dated here. [^2]

The trial is a limited clinical test in 32 people. It supports a benefit in primary Raynaud's under one regimen. It is not a universal recommendation for primary and secondary Raynaud's, and at the level of its abstract it does not test the two intermediate links Swanson's argument ran through. [^2] [^3]

#### Migraine and magnesium

The second case came in 1988, in the same medical journal: "Migraine and magnesium: eleven neglected connections," pages 526 to 557 of volume 31. That paper too is held as a bibliographic record without an abstract, and its eleven connections have not been checked one by one for this book. [^34]

Swanson's later accounts give the outline. Eleven indirect connections between the two literatures led to the inference that magnesium deficiency might be a causal factor in migraine headache. [^2] The ten-year update names two of the eleven. Magnesium can inhibit spreading depression in the cortex, and spreading depression may be implicated in migraine attacks.

A second connection in the same passage runs through epilepsy, which the update says has been associated with migraine. The same update reports that, after the analysis was published, more than 12 groups of medical researchers reported a systemic or local magnesium deficiency in migraine or a favorable response of migraine patients to dietary magnesium. [^33]

The evidence for this second case is of a different grade from the first. For fish oil there is a trial abstract that can be read apart from Swanson. For magnesium, what is held is a title and the author's retrospective summary. An author's account of what followed his own prediction is not an independent review of those later studies. [^34] [^33]

#### The case in LIA's terms

The founding text names five kinds of latency, and two of them apply to the fish-oil case. Relational latency is "Meaningful connections among artifacts that have not been recognized or documented together." Inferential latency is "Conclusions or hypotheses obtainable by combining observations, claims, or constraints." [LIA §3] The first is the condition Swanson found: two sets of informational artifacts, each in print, that shared no article and no citation. The second is what he produced from them: a hypothesis obtained by combining a claim about fish oil with a claim about the blood of patients. [^2] [^1]

The case also has two dates with two different standings. In 1986 the hypothesis was an archaeological candidate. It had been generated entirely from existing information, with its premises and citations laid out for criticism, and it had been tested on no one. In 1989 it became a tested claim, supported for one form of the condition by one trial. [^2] [^3]

What is established is that Swanson gave a general argument for why public fragments go unconnected, a two-condition model for finding them, and one case carried through to a controlled trial. The next chapter takes up what became of the method when it was turned into software and then into a field, literature-based discovery.

### III-02. Literature-based discovery

In 2006 Neil Smalheiser and colleagues published an account of five years spent putting a search tool called Arrowsmith in front of working neuroscientists. Smalheiser had begun collaborating with Don Swanson in the 1990s, and the two had turned Swanson's procedure into what the paper calls a systematic computer-assisted search strategy, with a demonstration website. The tool offered two searches.

In a one-node search the user starts from a single literature, such as the articles on a disease, and looks for a second, unknown literature that holds complementary knowledge, such as one on possible therapies. In a two-node search the user names both literatures, A and C. Arrowsmith lists the words and phrases that occur in the titles of both, removes common words with a stoplist, and presents what is left as a B-list. For any B-term the user can put the titles that contain A and B beside the titles that contain B and C, and judge whether the term marks a biologically meaningful link. [^24]

Swanson and Smalheiser had given an earlier account of the same design in 1996. One function produced a list of candidate literatures complementary to a starting literature. The other took two literatures, built a file of titles for each from a title-word search of MEDLINE, which then held 8 million records, and listed every word common to the two sets of titles except those on a stoplist of about 5,000 words. [^33]

In both searches the program retrieves, intersects, filters, and arranges, and a person reads the paired titles and decides whether a shared word carries an argument. Swanson and Smalheiser stated the reason in 1996: recognizing a meaningful association takes a high level of subject expertise, the natural language of titles and abstracts is an obstacle to fuller automation, and their aids were designed to stimulate the human ability to see connections. [^33] The one-node search generates hypotheses. The two-node search assesses and ranks hypotheses the user already has. Swanson had first emphasized the one-node search; the 2006 paper reports that the two-node search turned out to be more generally applicable and closer to the way most biomedical investigators handle information. [^24]

The founding text names literature-based discovery in the first of its precedents, beside Swanson, and then says what it proposes to add.

> The proposed contribution of LIA is to place the informational backlog itself at the center of a general, cross-domain discipline: to investigate its structure, develop methods for its excavation, and establish shared standards for the resulting findings. [LIA §9]

The rest of this chapter covers what happened when investigators used the tool, and the difficulty the field reports in judging its own results.


![Dated timeline of literature-based discovery precedents](figures/F10.png)

#### What the field testers did

As of 2000, the 2006 paper says, a single Arrowsmith search took many hours, counting the crafting of queries, navigation of the website, and analysis of the results, and it was unclear that bench scientists wanted what the tool provided. A five-year project began in May 2001 at the University of Illinois at Chicago to find out.

Field testers were placed in large neuroscience research groups. They chose their own searches in the course of their own work and recorded them in electronic notebooks. Over 125 searches were entered and analyzed. The paper makes no attempt to assess the tool's performance; its subject is how the testers changed the tool. [^24]

The first finding was about volume. A rebuilt site returned a raw B-list in three minutes or less, without significant training. The raw list held hundreds to a few thousand terms in alphabetical order, which most testers found too long to scan. Two filters were added.

One assigned title terms to 134 semantic categories, grouped into about 15 super-categories, so that a user could ask only for diseases, or only for genes. The other removed, for literatures of more than 1,000 articles, any term that appeared in only one paper in either literature. That frequency filter removed about three-quarters of the B-terms, and users judged informally that few interesting ones were lost. Eight filters were eventually implemented. [^24]

One of the paper's illustrations shows the scale. A search on retinal detachment against aortic aneurysm returned a raw B-list of 741 shared title terms. Only one paper in MEDLINE had both conditions in its title. Filtered to the category of disease or syndrome, the list led the user to Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and the paired titles confirmed that the syndrome involves both conditions. [^24]

The second finding was about behavior. The developers had recommended a two-step procedure: first search thoroughly for any direct literature on A and C together, then run the two-node search on narrowly defined queries. To the authors' knowledge, none of the field testers ever followed it.

The testers treated the two-node search as an extension of an ordinary PubMed search, a quick way to answer a specific question, and they wanted meaningful links whether or not these were already well known. Many searches were not causal chains at all. They asked for items studied in both literatures, or for the papers in an unfamiliar literature most relevant to a familiar one. [^24]

Some searches did what the model intended, and the paper reports them at the level they reached. A tester studying learning in rabbits searched theta rhythm against neurogenesis. No article mentioned both. The B-terms led him through REM sleep to the hypothesis that depriving a rat of REM sleep should decrease neurogenesis in the hippocampus.

He submitted an animal protocol, and resources to run it were not immediately available. Papers that appeared later reported that sleep deprivation, not specific to the REM stage, decreases cell proliferation there. In a second case, a neurologist outside the project found no prior studies on sildenafil and Parkinson's disease in PubMed, ran a two-node search, found a link through cyclic GMP, and wrote a grant proposal that was funded. A funded proposal and a later consistent literature are what the paper reports. Neither is a test of the hypothesis as stated. [^24]

#### A field with a workflow

The method became a field. Thilakaratne and colleagues, in a 2019 systematic review, date literature-based discovery, abbreviated LBD, as a research field from the medical discoveries Swanson published from 1986. [^23] Henry and McInnes, in a 2017 review held here at the level of its abstract, present a unifying framework for LBD systems. The abstract lists what the review covers: term and document representation, system components, co-occurrence models, semantic models, and distributional models, the filtering of uninformative terms, term ranking, the display of results, and evaluation, with drug development, drug repurposing, and adverse drug event prediction as application areas. All of it is in the biomedical domain. [^35]

The 2019 review states the field's shared premise: "Most of the LBD literature is based on the fundamental premise introduced by Swanson, namely the ABC model" of the previous chapter. It names the two variants open discovery and closed discovery. In open discovery only the starting concept is known and the process finds the rest, which makes it a generator of hypotheses. In closed discovery the user supplies both ends and the output is the set of intermediate concepts that bridge them. These are Arrowsmith's one-node and two-node searches under general names. [^23] [^24]

The reviewers also record the model's limits. Intermediate terms multiply the search space, and the target terms produced are too many to interpret by hand, which is the problem the field testers met as the long B-list. [^23]

#### How the field checks itself

Chapter II-05 introduced the two main evaluation designs: replicating a known discovery from the literature that preceded it, and time-slicing, in which a system run on data before a cut-off date is scored against associations that appear after it. That chapter also gave the review's example of overfitting. This chapter adds the field's verdict on the whole set of techniques. The review opens the subject with this sentence: "Evaluating the effectiveness of the LBD results is challenging and remains to be an open issue." The reason given is that the output is knowledge not yet published anywhere, so its usefulness has to be shown some other way. [^23]

Replication is the technique used most. The review tabulates twelve discoveries that later systems have been asked to reproduce. [^23] Six of them are on the list of their own examples that Swanson and Smalheiser gave in 1996. [^33]

The reviewers observe that for fish oil and Raynaud's, Swanson suggested three intermediate connections, found by trial and error, and that nothing shows these to be the only connections between the two literatures. A system is scored on whether it recovers his three. Whatever else it returns is not assessed. [^23]

Time-slicing answers four stated weaknesses of replication. It scores the complete output. It can be repeated for many starting concepts; one study the review cites used 100.

It does not require knowing the answer in advance, so parameters cannot be tuned toward it. And it gives numbers that allow one system to be compared with another. Its weak point is the choice of date, which the review says is crucial because "it decides the time period that turns a hypothesis into a true discovery." The gold standard is future co-occurrence, and the cut-off determines how much future there is. [^23]

The remaining techniques each cover less. Expert review, by one or two domain experts, is rated expensive, time-consuming, and subjective. Evaluations of whether users can form hypotheses from a system's output are rare in the literature the review covers. Experimental proof is feasible only for the association at the top of a list, and so says nothing about the accuracy of the rest. [^23]

#### What LIA takes from this

The founding text's research agenda takes up the same problem and keeps both designs. Its first priority is historical replay.

> Freeze a corpus at an earlier date and measure whether a method recovers discoveries documented afterward. Control for training-data leakage, hindsight in selecting tasks, and selection bias. [LIA §10]

The controls in the second sentence correspond to what the LBD reviewers found. Tuning a system toward a known answer is hindsight in selecting tasks. Scoring against a dozen famous discoveries is selection bias. The agenda's second priority, publishing timestamped candidates before their outcomes are known, is the prospective form of the same test, and its third names literature-based discovery systems among the baselines that LIA methods are to be compared with. [LIA §10] [^23]

LIA takes from this field the A-B-C form, the distinction between generating a hypothesis and assessing one, the place of the expert reader, and an evaluation problem already mapped by the people who have worked on it longest. The field has been biomedical in its literature, its reviews, and its application areas. [^35] [^23] What §9 proposes to add is scope and a center: the epistemic backlog itself, across domains and across kinds of informational artifact, as the object of study.

Literature-based discovery is established here as a working tradition with tools, a common model, and a candid account of how hard its results are to score. The next chapter turns to a method that needs no shared title word, the word embeddings that Tshitoyan and colleagues trained on materials science abstracts in 2019.

### III-03. Latent knowledge in materials science

In July 2019 Nature published a letter by Vahe Tshitoyan and eight colleagues at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the University of California, Berkeley. They had collected approximately 3.3 million abstracts of scientific papers, published between 1922 and 2018 in more than 1,000 journals judged likely to carry materials research.

The text contained a vocabulary of about 500,000 words. They ran a standard algorithm over it that assigns each word a list of 200 numbers, called a word embedding, chosen so that words used in similar surroundings receive similar lists. Nobody labeled anything, and no chemistry was supplied. To the algorithm, the formula of a compound was a string of characters like any other word. [^15]

The authors then asked which material formulas had embeddings close to the embedding of the word "thermoelectric," which labels a class of functional materials whose performance is scored by a figure of merit called zT. Some formulas ranked high although they had never appeared in an abstract together with that word or with any related keyword. The authors treated those formulas as predictions and tested them three ways: against computed properties, against measured properties, and against the later literature. [^15]

Earlier chapters used this paper for the cost of search, for the formal boundary, and for novelty. This chapter takes it as a whole, because the framing singles it out.

> The 2019 Nature paper, "Unsupervised word embeddings capture latent knowledge from materials science literature," is particularly relevant. It suggests some future scientific discoveries are already implicitly encoded in existing publications. [Framing §2]

The founding text lists the same work as the second tradition in its lineage, in more guarded words.

> **Computational extraction of latent scientific knowledge.** Tshitoyan and colleagues demonstrated in 2019 that representations learned from materials science literature could recover domain relationships and retrospectively recommend materials before their later documented investigation. [3] [LIA §9]

The founding text's sentence has two parts. One is about relationships the representation recovers. The other is about recommendations made in retrospect. The paper supports the two parts with different evidence, and the sections below take them in turn.

#### What the embedding holds

The training method is simple to state. The algorithm takes one word at a time and is trained to predict the words that appear near it. The numbers it adjusts along the way become that word's embedding. Two battery cathode compounds that keep company with the same neighboring words, such as "cathodes" and "electrochemical," end up with nearly the same numbers. Closeness between two embeddings is measured by the angle between them, a quantity called cosine similarity. [^15]

The authors first checked that the resulting geometry agreed with chemistry they already knew. They asked for the five compositions most similar to LiCoO2, a well-known lithium-ion cathode compound, and all five returned were also lithium-ion cathode materials. They posed analogies by adding and subtracting embeddings.

Taking the embedding of "ferromagnetic," subtracting that of the alloy NiFe, and adding that of IrMn gave a point whose nearest word was "antiferromagnetic," which is the correct answer for IrMn. The step from a metal to its principal oxide pointed in a consistent direction for zirconium, chromium, and nickel, and so did the step from each metal to its crystal structure. Across categories of such analogies, accuracy was close to 50 percent, which the authors compare to the baseline set by the original study of the algorithm on general text. When the embeddings of the chemical elements were projected onto two dimensions, their arrangement reflected their positions in the periodic table. [^15]

None of these facts was new. What the check showed is where the knowledge resided after training: in the positions of the embeddings, which no person had arranged. This is the first half of the founding text's sentence, the recovery of domain relationships. It is also an instance of the class the founding text's table of latency calls representational, which the table defines in one line.

> Structure or associations encoded in a computational representation, such as an embedding or trained model, that require elicitation and independent verification. [LIA §3]

The line has two requirements, elicitation and independent verification. The ranking against the word "thermoelectric" is the elicitation. The three tests are the verification, and each one reaches a different distance.

#### Three tests

The first test used computed properties. A dataset holds power factors, a quantity that forms an important component of zT, calculated from physical theory for approximately 48,000 compounds. A total of 9,483 compounds appeared both in that dataset and more than three times in the abstracts. Of those, 1,820 had already been mentioned alongside a thermoelectric keyword, and 7,663 had not.

The 7,663 were the candidates. Ranked by the embedding, the top ten had an average computed maximum power factor of 40.8 μW K⁻² cm⁻¹. The average for all candidates was 11.5, and the average for the known thermoelectrics was 17.0. The embeddings had been trained on text alone and were never adjusted using the computed data. [^15]

The second test used measurements. For 83 materials that appeared both in the abstracts and in a set of experimental results, the authors compared the order the embedding gave with the order the laboratory gave. The rank correlation was 59 percent for maximum power factor and 52 percent for maximum zT.

The computed dataset used in the first test showed a 31 percent rank correlation with the same experimental power factors. This test concerns materials already measured, so it shows that the ranking tracks measured performance where measurements exist. It does not measure any of the new candidates. [^15]

The third test is the historical one, which Chapter II-03 set out step by step. The authors built 18 historical collections of abstracts, one for each cutoff year from 2001 to 2018, each holding only abstracts published before its cutoff. They trained separate embeddings on each, took the top 50 predictions, and counted for every later year the share of those 50 that had since been reported in the literature alongside a thermoelectric keyword. [^15]

#### What the figures mean

The paper reports two figures from the historical test. Averaged over the runs, a material from a top-50 list was eight times more likely to have been studied as a thermoelectric within the next five years than a randomly chosen unstudied material from the collection at that time. It was three times more likely than a random material with a nonzero computed bandgap, a property most thermoelectrics need. [^15]

Both figures are ratios against a stated baseline. Eightfold does not mean that 80 percent of the predictions came true, and it is not a lead time of eight years. It means the predicted group was taken up by researchers at eight times the rate of the comparison group. The second baseline is the stricter one, because choosing among materials with a bandgap already applies domain knowledge. [^15]

The endpoint of the test is a later abstract that places the material next to a thermoelectric keyword. That records what researchers went on to study and report. It is not a laboratory measurement of each predicted material, and the computed power factors of the first test are calculations, which are not measurements either. The research ledger states the level plainly: historical enrichment and computed-property checks support a retrospective ranking result, and they do not establish experimental performance for every suggested compound. The ledger also assigns the case no lag, because a cutoff year and the publication year of a retrospective study are not one candidate's interval from availability to recognition. [^21]

The whole test was run in 2019 on collections cut to look like the past. No list was issued in 2009 and then watched. The authors' own summary, in their abstract, is that an unsupervised method can "recommend materials for functional applications several years before their discovery." [^21] In the body of the paper the claim is conditional in tense: CuGaTe2 would have been predicted as a top-five compound four years before its publication in 2012. [^15]

The paper also shows how a single prediction arises, which matters for anyone who has to check one. For the third-ranked candidate, CsAgGa2Se4, the authors traced the context words that link the formula to the target word. The compound's embedding sits near "chalcogenide," "band gap," "optoelectronic," and "photovoltaic applications." Many good thermoelectrics are chalcogenides, most need a bandgap, and thermoelectric materials overlap heavily with optoelectronic and photovoltaic ones. The prediction is the sum of those associations. [^15]

The closing paragraph of the founding text's section on latency applies here.

> A model's ability to articulate a claim does not establish that the claim was encoded as truthful knowledge, nor that the claim is supported by its training data. Representational latency requires particular care because the original evidentiary trail may be inaccessible. [LIA §3]

In this paper the trail is unusually accessible. The corpus is a dated list of abstracts, the method is public, and the path from a formula to the target word can be drawn. The paper supports the framing's "some": in one field, for one kind of claim, a representation of past publications ranked materials in a way that anticipated later study. It does not show that every future result is recoverable from present publications.

The authors name limits of their own. Word embeddings of this kind register which words occur together and cannot represent negation. Abstracts were workable partly because they tend to state positive relationships. Full texts hold more negative and more varied statements and would require more powerful methods. [^15]

#### Two successors

Two later studies took the same idea in different directions. In 2023 Mario Krenn and colleagues applied it to the literature of artificial intelligence itself. From 143,000 papers posted to arXiv between 1992 and 2020 they extracted 64,719 concepts and built a network in which two concepts are joined when they appear together in a title or abstract, with each link stamped by date.

The task, which they released as a benchmark called Science4Cast, is to take the network as it stood one, three, or five years before 2021 and rank 10 million unjoined pairs of concepts by the probability that they will be joined by 2021. Only about 1 to 3 percent of such pairs do join. Ten methods were compared. In the authors' words, "the most powerful methods use a carefully curated set of network features, rather than an end-to-end AI approach." [^36]

What this predicts is narrower than what the materials paper predicts. A new link means that two concepts were studied together in a later paper. It forecasts where attention will go, which the authors call a first step toward suggesting research topics. It is not a validated scientific result about either concept. [^36]

Jamshid Sourati and James Evans, also in 2023, changed what goes into the model. Their abstract observes that models trained on published findings typically ignore the scientists whose choice of research topics shapes what gets discovered. They build in the distribution of human expertise by training on simulated inferences that are cognitively accessible to experts, and report that this improves prediction of future discoveries, especially where the relevant literature is sparse.

The models succeed, the abstract says, by predicting human predictions and the scientists who will make them. Tuned the other way, to avoid the crowd, the same approach generates "scientifically promising ‘alien’ hypotheses unlikely to be imagined or pursued without intervention until the distant future." [^16] Only the abstract of this paper is held in the research record, so the chapter reports no figures from it. [^16]

Read together, the three studies separate two questions that the eightfold figure runs together. One is what researchers will study next. The other is what is true and still unstudied.

The endpoint of later literature mention measures the first. Sourati and Evans state the difference outright, and their crowd-avoiding hypotheses are aimed at the second. For LIA the second question defines an archaeological candidate, and only independent tests of the kind that line requires can answer it.

The 2019 paper establishes, at retrospective level, that a representation trained on a dated corpus of abstracts can rank candidates the corpus never stated, and that the ranking anticipated later study at eight and three times its two baselines. The next chapter takes up the third precedent in the lineage, a system that generates hypotheses and criticizes them before a scientist tests any.

### III-04. The co-scientist

On May 19, 2026, Nature published "Accelerating scientific discovery with Co-Scientist," by Juraj Gottweis and colleagues at Google with collaborators at universities including Stanford and Imperial College London. The paper presents a system built on Gemini, Google's large language model. A scientist states a research goal in ordinary language, along with any constraints and preferences. A Supervisor agent reads the goal and assigns work to six specialized agents, each a copy of the model with its own instructions. The Generation agent searches the literature and writes first hypotheses.

The Reflection agent reviews them as a peer reviewer would, for correctness, quality, and novelty. The Ranking agent runs a tournament in which hypotheses are compared in pairs through simulated scientific debates. The Evolution agent revises the ones that rank highest. The Proximity agent measures how closely hypotheses resemble one another, and the Meta-review agent looks for patterns across the reviews and debates and feeds them back to the others. The scientist receives a ranked overview and can respond, redirect, or add hypotheses of their own at any point. [^28]

A first account of the system had appeared as a preprint on February 26, 2025, under the title "Towards an AI co-scientist." It reported the same design on Gemini 2.0 and summed it up as generate, debate, and evolve. The preprint states that the work does not aim to automate the scientific process completely, and that the system is built for a scientist who stays in the loop.

In the preprint's account, the goal a scientist supplies can be anything from a single statement to a long document accompanied by hundreds of earlier publications, and it can carry constraints specific to that scientist's laboratory. The system parses the goal into a plan that fixes, among other things, the criteria by which hypotheses will be judged. While a run is under way the Supervisor agent periodically computes statistics on its progress and uses them to decide where to spend further computation and when to stop. The two documents are versions of one report, fifteen months apart, and this chapter cites each for what it says. [^37]

Both canon documents list the system among the precedents.

> **AI-assisted scientific discovery.** Co-Scientist illustrates how multi-agent hypothesis generation and criticism can be integrated into scientific investigation. [4] [LIA §9]

> **Google AI Co-Scientist (2025):** Multi-agent systems designed to generate, challenge and refine scientific hypotheses. [Framing §2]

The founding text's sentence has three parts: generation, criticism, and integration into investigation. The design above supplies the first two. The third depends on what happened when the hypotheses reached a laboratory, and the paper reports three such cases, each tested to a different level.

#### Generation and criticism

The system's default criteria for a hypothesis are that it fit the stated goal and be plausible, novel, testable, and safe. Novelty here means going beyond a synthesis of existing information, which the paper notes is a capability that existing research tools already offer. [^28]

The tournament is the system's main form of criticism. Each hypothesis carries an Elo rating, the kind of score used to rank chess players, which rises or falls with each pairwise comparison.

The authors tracked these ratings across 203 research goals and found that hypotheses produced later in a run scored higher than those produced early. On a subset of 15 goals written by seven biomedical experts, the system's ratings came to exceed those of several other language models and of the experts' own best-guess answers. The rating is assigned by the system itself, so these results show that longer runs satisfy the system's own judges better. The authors add that the improvement holds provided the research goal is tractable and suits a search-and-explore approach. [^28]

A human check was smaller. The experts who wrote 11 of those goals ranked and rated the outputs of Co-Scientist and three other models. Co-Scientist was the most preferred, with average ratings of 3.64 for novelty and 3.09 for impact on a five-point scale. The paper states that these are subjective expert assessments and not objective ground truth, and that the evaluation was small. [^28]

The authors also removed components to see what each contributed. Giving the Reflection agent access to external search prevented hypotheses that looked novel and were implausible. Using a debate format in the Ranking agent improved the ranking and reduced its sensitivity to the order in which hypotheses were presented. [^28] Criticism in this system therefore has two sources: other agents arguing from their instructions, and the literature reached through search.

#### Leukemia and liver fibrosis

The first laboratory case was drug repurposing, the search for new uses of drugs already approved. The system was restricted to a curated list of 2,300 approved drugs across 34 cancer types, and oncologists reviewed its predictions. For acute myeloid leukemia, or AML, 30 top-ranked candidates went to expert oncologists, who chose five for testing: binimetinib, pacritinib, cerivastatin, pravastatin, and dimethyl fumarate. Three of the five, binimetinib, pacritinib, and cerivastatin, inhibited the viability of AML cells in culture. Binimetinib, already approved for metastatic melanoma, did so at concentrations as low as 2 nanomolar in three of the four AML cell lines tested. [^28]

A second run asked for candidates with no prior preclinical evidence in AML and gave the system no expert feedback while it worked. Experts picked the top three for testing: nanvuranlat, KIRA6, and leflunomide. KIRA6 inhibited cell viability, with a half-maximal concentration of 10 nanomolar in one AML line against 180 nanomolar in the non-AML control line, the 18-fold separation that Chapter II-04 cited.

Two other AML lines were markedly less sensitive to it. Nanvuranlat and leflunomide showed limited effect. The system's own novelty review had rated the KIRA6 hypothesis as moderately novel, noting that the drug's target had been explored in AML before with other compounds. [^28]

The authors then tested seven drug combinations the system proposed. In one cell line the responses were predominantly synergistic, meaning the drugs together did more than their separate effects would predict. In the other, the results mixed synergy with antagonism. [^28]

These are in vitro results: cultured cells, a few cell lines, three independent experiments per condition. The paper is direct about the level. It calls the experiments a viability check and says "they are not a replacement for the rigorous preclinical and clinical assessment that is typically required for therapeutic validation." [^28]

The authors add that strong in vitro results, even for a hypothesis that oncologists have reviewed favorably, do not guarantee efficacy in a living organism or success in a clinical trial, and they list the reasons: how much of a drug reaches its target, how the body handles it, effects on other targets, and which patients are selected. [^28] The experiments do not show that any of these drugs helps a patient with AML. The count is part of the evidence too. Of eight single drugs chosen by experts from the system's lists, four showed activity and four did not. A system that generates candidates produces some that fail, and the paper reports the ones that did.

The second case concerned liver fibrosis. The system was asked for hypotheses about epigenetic changes that could be targeted, and experts selected three top-ranked ones. The system named three epigenetic modifiers and drugs that act on them. The drugs were tested in human hepatic organoids, which are small laboratory-grown models of liver tissue.

Two of the three showed significant anti-fibrotic activity without cellular toxicity. One of the two, vorinostat, is already approved in the United States for a cancer indication. [^28] An organoid is closer to an organ than a layer of cells is, and it is still not a patient. The result establishes activity in that model. The authors point to the link the system drew between a cancer drug and a liver disease as an example of a connection across separate fields of medicine. [^28]

#### The mechanism the laboratory already held

The third case is the one Chapter II-05 used for novelty: a hypothesis about how the mobile genetic elements called cf-PICIs spread across bacterial species, put to the system by researchers who had found the mechanism and not yet published it. That chapter asked to whom the hypothesis was new. Here the same case is evidence about the method.

It is a stronger test of the method than the first two cases in one respect and a weaker one in another. In the AML and fibrosis cases the system's hypotheses were tested by experiments run afterward, and the experiments could only speak to the candidates experts chose to test. In the cf-PICI case the answer existed before the system ran, and it was absent from the published literature the system could search. The system was given minimal background, worked for two days, and ranked first the hypothesis that cf-PICIs interact with diverse phage tails to expand their host range, which matched the laboratory's experimental finding. [^28]

A system that only retrieved published statements could not have returned that answer, because no publication held it. This is historical replay in its cleanest form, with the laboratory's unpublished result serving as the held-back record.

The weaker respect is that the case is one question, chosen by people who knew it had an answer. It shows the system can assemble a correct mechanism from public evidence when one is there to be assembled. It does not show a discovery of something unknown to everyone, and the co-timed reports are the laboratory's own work, not an independent replication of the system's output. [^28]

#### Integrated into investigation

Across the three cases the division of work is constant. A scientist wrote the goal. The system generated, criticized, and ranked. Experts chose which hypotheses to test, and laboratory scientists ran the experiments.

The paper says that all three validations included an expert in the loop and that experts set the priorities for laboratory work. [^28] That is what "integrated into scientific investigation" means in the founding text's sentence: the system occupies the stage where hypotheses are formed and screened, and the stages on either side remain with people.

The paper's stated limitations bear on LIA directly. The system relies on open-access literature, so it can miss earlier work behind paywalls and has systematically little access to negative results. The quality of its hypotheses depends on a source literature of mixed and contradictory quality, which carries a risk of propagating erroneous or irreproducible findings. It inherits imperfect factuality from the underlying model. The authors call their validation preliminary, and they name as future work agents that can trace a claim to the specific figure or data behind it. [^28]

Each of these is a statement about the corpus and about provenance. A method that excavates the published record is bounded by what the record contains and by how well each claim can be followed back to its source.

Co-Scientist shows hypothesis generation and criticism joined to laboratory work, with results that reach in vitro activity, organoid activity, and one recapitulated mechanism. In every case it began from a question that a scientist had already asked. Part IV takes up what changes when a system has to find the questions, and the next chapter turns to the first of the framing's six domains, materials.

<a id="chapter-iii-05"></a>

### III-05. Materials

Metal-organic frameworks, or MOFs, are crystalline materials built from a lattice of metal ions held together by organic linkers. The first were synthesized in the 1990s.

By 2023 more than 100,000 MOF structures had been reported in the Cambridge Structural Database. That database held comprehensive structural information for each framework: which atoms sit where. On how each one had been made, its data were scarce and inconsistent. The solvent, the metal source, the linker, and the synthesis route were in the methods sections of the papers that first reported the structures, written as ordinary sentences. [^38]

In 2023 Glasby and colleagues published DigiMOF, a database that joins the two. They took the publication identifiers attached to the MOF entries in the structure database, used them to download the papers automatically, and ran the text through rule-based parsers that looked for a framework's name together with one of five properties: synthesis method, solvent, metal precursor, organic linker, and topology. They chose the first four because earlier cost studies had found that solvents, linkers, and metal precursors drive what a MOF costs to produce.

The structure collection covered 43,281 unique publications. From it the parsers drew 15,501 pairings of a MOF name with a property, and the authors report that they "text-mined over 52,680 associated properties." The database holds a name and at least one such property for about 15 percent of the structures in the collection. [^38]

None of this was secret. Each fact had been printed in a journal article that the structure database itself pointed to. It was absent only from the place where a person screening frameworks for a use would look.

> **Materials science:** Forgotten polymers, alloys, catalysts and manufacturing techniques that become commercially viable under new conditions. [Framing §4]

This is the first of the six domains the framing names. The founding text gives the reason the MOF record counts as latent at all.

> Latency is relational. A claim can be explicit in one archive, invisible to another community, and genuinely unarticulated when considered across an entire body of evidence. [LIA §3]

The synthesis details were explicit in the journal archive and invisible in the structure archive. DigiMOF is an excavation of the first into the second, and its limits are stated by its authors. On a test set of 50 randomly chosen papers the parsers reached an overall precision of 77 percent, so roughly one extracted pairing in four is wrong. The parsers favored precision over recall and so missed much of what the papers contain.

A compound that a paper labels only "1" or "2" could not be tied to any property. Some articles could not be accessed, and some were not in English. The parsers found 5,677 hydrothermal syntheses and 3,672 solvothermal ones, which the authors call surprising, since solvothermal routes are the usual laboratory method; many papers imply that route by naming the solvents and never use the word. A mined database reports what the text said in the forms the parser could read. It does not show that any synthesis will succeed when repeated. [^38]

#### Recipes for inorganic solids

The 2019 study by Olga Kononova and colleagues, introduced in Chapter I-02, shows how extraction works at a larger scale for inorganic solids. The authors note that the widely used materials databases, curated by hand over decades, store structures and properties, and that efforts to collect synthesis data are recent. Their pipeline scraped 4,204,170 papers and found 6,218,136 paragraphs in the experimental sections. A classifier judged 188,198 of those paragraphs to be about inorganic synthesis, and 53,538 of them about one family of methods, solid-state synthesis. The published dataset consists of "19,488 synthesis entries retrieved from 53,538 solid-state synthesis paragraphs." [^7]

Each entry is what the authors call a codified recipe: the target material, the starting compounds, the operations performed and their conditions, and a balanced chemical equation for the reaction. The authors report that the balancing step works as a check: it places added constraints on the extracted targets and precursors and helps to reduce errors from parsing chemical compositions. The dataset is public, as a single file, and each record carries the identifier of the paper it was drawn from and a snippet of the source paragraph, so that any entry can be followed back to the text. [^7]

The authors measured what was lost. The overall yield of the pipeline was 28 percent of the paragraphs. They drew 100 solid-state paragraphs at random and found that 30 did not contain a complete set of starting materials and products, so that a human expert could not have rebuilt the reaction from them either. Of the 70 that did, the pipeline failed to reconstruct 42.

They then checked 100 finished entries by hand against the source paragraphs. Targets were extracted with 97 percent precision and balanced reactions with 95 percent. Mixing conditions fared worst, with an F1 score of about 65 percent. Counting only the chemistry, 93 percent of entries were correct. Counting every item of the recipe, 51 percent were.

These figures measure agreement between the dataset and the text. They are not a test of whether a recipe, followed at the bench, yields the target. [^7]

#### Phase diagrams rebuilt from sentences

The third case goes a step past recovery. In 2020 Court and Cole reported a pipeline that began as the other two did. From a corpus of 74,000 journal articles it extracted about 20,400 records, each pairing a compound with the temperature at which it changes magnetic or superconducting phase. After cleaning, the final records came from only 3,668 of the articles. The records were then joined to elemental and structural data from existing repositories. [^39]

With that database the authors "successfully reconstruct the phase diagrams of well-known magnetic and superconducting compounds." For a series of rare-earth manganites, the transition temperatures mined from text were plotted against the radius of the rare-earth ion and compared with reference values taken from articles outside the corpus; both curves showed the same non-linear dependence. For an iron-based superconductor doped with nickel, the diagram assembled from mined values was set beside a published one, and both showed the superconducting state emerging from an antiferromagnetic one. A phase diagram of this kind had been drawn before by specialists. Here it was assembled by machine from numbers scattered across independent papers. [^39]

The pipeline then predicted. In a series of rare-earth orthochromites the mined data lacked five members: the thulium, europium, neodymium, praseodymium, and cerium compounds. A model trained on the rest predicted their transition temperatures, and the best version came within a mean absolute error of 3.1 percent of reported values. For a second series, of superconductors, the model gave critical temperatures for several compounds whose reference values, the authors note, had not been reported when the paper appeared. The platform links each prediction to experimental datasets carrying their own identifiers, so that a user can check it against first-hand data. [^39]

The level of this evidence is computational. The reconstructions were checked against known diagrams, and the predictions for missing compounds against published values where those existed. No new sample was measured for the paper, and the predictions with no reference value remain untested. The authors state that their models predict for known compounds and that predicting new classes of compounds is a goal still ahead. Chapter III-03 told the neighboring case in full, in which word embeddings trained on dated abstracts ranked thermoelectric candidates; that result too was retrospective. [^39] [^21]

#### What the framing names and what the cases show

The framing's sentence is about materials and techniques: polymers, alloys, catalysts, and ways of manufacturing that were set aside and that become commercially viable when conditions change. In the founding text's terms that is contextual latency. None of the three audited cases shows it. No forgotten alloy returns to use in them, and none of them reports a commercial result.

What the cases show lies under that. The record of how things were made, and of what was measured, exists as prose in tens of thousands of papers, and it can be recovered into a form that can be searched, counted, and computed on, at error rates the excavators publish. That is archival and relational latency in DigiMOF and the recipe dataset, and representational latency in the phase diagrams.

A search for an abandoned material that has become viable would need exactly this kind of record to work from. The cases establish that the record can be built. They do not establish that such a search has succeeded.

Three excavations of the materials literature have recovered synthesis conditions, recipes, and phase behavior into usable form, each validated as extraction or computation and none as a new measurement. The next chapter, III-06, takes up patents, the second of the framing's domains.

<a id="chapter-iii-06"></a>

### III-06. Patents

In 1984 Charles Hull filed the application that became United States patent 4,575,330, published in 1986. Its first paragraph names the subject as stereolithography and defines the coined word as the "application of lithographic techniques to production of three-dimensional objects." The problem it starts from is ordinary. A plastic part is designed, a prototype is made by hand at considerable time and expense, the design is reviewed, and the cycle repeats until the design is settled. Production then usually means injection molding, whose tooling costs make it practical only in high volume. [^29]

The patent's answer is to build the part as a stack of thin cross sections. A vessel holds a liquid that hardens under ultraviolet light. A programmed, movable spot of that light draws one cross section of the object on the liquid's surface, and the liquid it touches turns solid.

A platform then moves the solid lamina away from the surface by the thickness of one layer, fresh liquid flows over it, and the spot draws the next cross section, which adheres to the one before. The steps repeat until the whole object exists. A computer generates the cross sections and sends the commands, so that a designer who is satisfied with a shape on the screen can have the part made directly from it, with no drawings and no tooling. [^29]

> **Patent archaeology:** Abandoned inventions whose original technological constraints no longer exist. [Framing §4]

This is the second of the six domains the framing names. Its sentence is about inventions that were given up. The audited cases in this chapter are about something else that the patent record holds, and the chapter returns to the framing's own phrase at the end. What the cases show is that a patent is a dated public document in which someone joins fields and says which ones.

#### Three records of joined fields

Chapter II-04 used Hull's patent to separate invention from excavation, and quoted its list of what was already known: liquids curable by ultraviolet light, then used as printing ink, paper coatings, and adhesives, and the growing techniques of computer-aided design and manufacturing. The patent adds a third ingredient by definition. Lithography, it says, is the art of reproducing graphic objects, and it counts photographic reproduction, xerography, the microlithography of electronics, and a computer's drawing on a plotter as examples.

The patent also places itself against earlier work. It cites four prior United States patents for systems that cured a fluid at points deep inside its volume, where separate beams of radiation intersected, and it states that those systems had trouble with resolution and exposure control as the points moved deeper. Hull's method forms every cross section at the surface, with a single moving spot of light. [^29]

Carl Deckard's patent, United States 4,863,538, was filed in 1986 by the University of Texas System and published in 1989. Its background section argues from machining. Casting and extrusion suit large runs. For a few parts a shop cuts material away from a block, which produces waste, takes expensive set-up, wears the tools, and cannot reach many internal shapes. The patent notes that lasers had recently been used to add material, with earlier patents cited, but mainly to coat articles that had already been machined.

Deckard's design applies the laser to loose powder. A dispenser lays powder in a target area. The laser beam is steered over it in a raster scan by two mirrors, each turned by a galvanometer, and is switched on only while its aim falls inside the boundary of the current cross section, so that only that powder sinters. More powder is laid and the next cross section is sintered onto the last.

The abstract puts the control in one clause: the apparatus includes a "computer controlling a laser to direct the laser energy onto the powder." The computer, which the patent says can be a CAD/CAM system, works out the boundary of each layer from the dimensions of the part. [^40]

The third patent crosses a wider gap. United States 6,334,856, to Mark Allen, Mark Prausnitz, Devin McAllister, and Florent Cros, has a priority date in 1998 and was published in 2002, with Georgia Tech Research Corporation as applicant. Its field is drug delivery. The background lists the standard routes and their limits: many drugs taken as pills are degraded in the gut, an ordinary needle causes pain and leaves a wound, and a skin patch works for few drugs because the outer layer of the skin lets few drug molecules through.

The proposal is an array of needles small enough to pass through that outer layer without reaching the nerves beneath it. The means of making them comes from another industry. The patent states that its fabrication processes are based on well-established methods used to make integrated circuits and other microelectronic devices, and it lists lithography, etching, thermal oxidation of silicon, and electroplating among them. In one process, deep plasma etching of a silicon wafer, "Needles are patterned directly using photolithography," which the text says gives greater control over needle geometry. [^41]

In each document the fields being joined are named by the inventors themselves, and the document carries a date. The founding text has a sentence for this shape of work.

> These classes are not exclusive. An excavation may recover an obscure observation, connect it to a separate field, and reveal its usefulness in a new technological context. [LIA §3]

#### What a patent proves

A patent shows that a combination was disclosed on a date. The three records above establish that lithography and computer graphics had been joined to curable liquids in an application filed in 1984, that a computer-directed laser had been joined to powder in one filed in 1986, and that chip-making methods had been joined to drug-delivery needles in one with a 1998 priority date. That is relational and contextual latency made explicit and placed on the public record. [^29] [^40] [^41]

A patent does not show more than that. Hull's patent says its production costs should be similar to injection molding for short runs; the sentence is a forecast in an application, and the grant does not test it. Deckard's patent establishes a disclosed design and nothing about the reliability of later machines.

Allen's patent states that the devices can be mass-produced at low unit cost; clinical efficacy, manufacturing economics, and the validity of the patent itself each need separate evidence. None of the three shows that no one had seen the combination before the filing. In the founding text's terms each is an archaeological candidate set down in public, and section 5 is specific about what stands between that and anything further. [^29] [^40] [^41]

> A candidate may require new experiments before it becomes a discovery about the physical world. [LIA §5]

#### Patents as readers

Patents also cite. Their references are mostly to earlier patents, and a smaller number point to scientific papers.

In 2018 Anthony van Raan and Jos Winnink used those references to ask who first notices a neglected paper. Chapters I-02 and I-04 drew on the same study. They began with papers in physics, chemistry, engineering, and computer science that had gone nearly or wholly uncited for years and then drew attention, and matched them against a worldwide patent database. Their measure was the "time lag between the publication year of the SB-SNPRs and their first citation in a patent," where the abbreviation stands for such papers cited in patents, and the citing year is the filing year of the first patent to cite. [^8]

One set held 282 papers that had slept for ten years at a citation rate no higher than 0.5. Patents cited 44 of them, or 16 percent, and the first patent citation came between 1 and 18 years after publication, 8.5 on average.

A second set held 176 papers with no journal citations at all for five years. Patents cited 18, and the interval ran from 1 to 14 years, 6.7 on average. In both sets the interval shortened over the period studied until it fell below the length of the sleep itself. One paper, on a switch chip, was published in 1991, received no journal citations for five years, and was first cited in a patent in 1993. The authors conclude that in recent years such papers are, on average, found by technology before science finds them. [^8]

The study counts citations. The authors note that a patent's references come from examiners as well as inventors and do not necessarily mark the scientific basis of the invention. A patent citation is evidence that a paper was noticed in a technical setting. It is not proof of commercial value. [^8]

#### The framing's phrase

The framing's words for this domain are "Abandoned inventions whose original technological constraints no longer exist." [Framing §4] The idea is contextual latency in its plainest form: a design set aside because something it needed was missing, and a later date at which the missing thing is available. No audited case of that kind is in the record used here. The chapter therefore leaves the phrase as the framing states it, without an example.

The patent record is established here as a dated archive of cross-field combinations and as a reader of neglected papers, each at the level of disclosure or citation. The next chapter, III-07, takes up pharmaceuticals.

<a id="chapter-iii-07"></a>

### III-07. Pharmaceuticals

Ghofrani, Osterloh and Grimminger report that in 1986, researchers at Pfizer's laboratories in Sandwich, England, working in a program that sought drugs for angina pectoris, identified a group of compounds that strongly inhibited an enzyme called phosphodiesterase type 5, or PDE5. One of them was first named UK-92,480 and is now known as sildenafil. The 2006 review dates the first healthy-volunteer studies to 1991 and the reports of erections to 1992, while the 2021 review says "men reported an erectogenic effect with the UK-92-480" in trials it describes as preclinical and dates to 1985. [^57][^58] In the 2006 account, some volunteers who took doses of up to 75 milligrams three times a day for 10 consecutive days reported headaches, flushing, indigestion, and muscle aches, and some reported penile erections as a side effect.

The observation was at first not thought to be of major significance, and cardiovascular uses stayed the focus of the clinical work through 1992 and 1993. By the middle of 1993 the compound looked less promising for angina. Its half-life in the blood was about four hours, so it would have had to be taken at least three times a day, and it interacted with the nitrates that angina patients already took. The first clinical study in patients with erectile dysfunction was run late in 1993. [^57] The Food and Drug Administration's approval history for Viagra carries the entry "03/27/1998 ORIG-1 Approval Type 1 - New Molecular Entity." [^59]

Pharmaceutical research is the third of the six domains in which the framing expects the most from this work.

> **Pharmaceutical research:** Unexplored relationships between compounds, biological pathways and diseases. [Framing §4]

Each of the four cases in this chapter is a relationship between a compound and a disease that existing records pointed to before anyone had tested it. They differ in who noticed the relationship, in how it was noticed, and in what kind of test or approval followed.

#### A side effect in a volunteer study

The sildenafil record contained two things in 1993. One was the volunteers' reports. The other was a body of physiology published by other people.

The angina program had been built on the pathway by which nitric oxide relaxes the smooth muscle in blood vessel walls: nitric oxide raises the level of a messenger molecule, cGMP, inside the muscle cell, and PDE5 breaks that messenger down. A drug that blocks PDE5 leaves more of the messenger in place. In the early 1990s, other researchers reported that nitric oxide is also the signal released from nerves in the penis during sexual stimulation. The Pfizer team joined the two: an inhibitor of cGMP breakdown should strengthen and prolong that response, and only during stimulation. [^57]

The inference was then tested. By the middle of 1994, two clinical studies had shown that single doses improved erectile responses and that the effect rose with the dose. By 1997 more than 4,500 subjects had been exposed to the drug and 21 separate clinical trials had shown efficacy in various patient groups. The approval rests on those trials. The early reports from volunteers are the reason the trials were run, and they are not the evidence that the drug works. [^57]

This account rests on secondary sources. The detail comes from historical reviews written years afterward, one of them by authors that included Pfizer staff. The 2021 review dates the pivot away from angina to 1986, while the 2006 review describes a continuing cardiovascular focus through 1992 and 1993. [^58][^57] Only the approval date comes from a primary record. No lag is stated for the case, because the record cited does not establish both when the connection was first available and when it was first recognized. [^42]

#### A drug with a history

Thalidomide, the sedative discussed in Chapter II-05, had caused severe birth defects. A report that it relieved erythema nodosum leprosum, or ENL, appeared in 1965. A published regulatory history says that "The FDA granted marketing approval for thalidomide for cutaneous manifestations of ENL in 1998 based on historical data," [^30] and the agency's orphan-drug listing reads "Marketing Approval Date: 07/16/1998." [^56]

The thirty-three years measure the wait for US approval after the response was already on record in 1965. These dates establish the regulatory chronology alone. As with sildenafil, part of the account comes from a later regulatory history. The relationship between compound and disease was found by clinical observation, in a disease far from the drug's first use, and what moved it into the approved record was a regulatory review of data that already existed. [^30]

#### A graph queried in a pandemic

Chapter II-04 told the baricitinib case in full. In February 2020 a group at BenevolentAI published a letter in the Lancet. They had searched a knowledge graph, built in part from connections extracted from the scientific literature by machine learning, for approved drugs that might block the entry of the new coronavirus into cells.

"We identified baricitinib, which is predicted to reduce the ability of the virus to infect lung cells." Their route ran through AAK1, an enzyme that regulates the process by which most viruses enter cells. Baricitinib was an approved inhibitor of janus kinases, and it also bound AAK1 at ordinary doses. The authors proposed that it be trialled to reduce both viral entry and inflammation. [^25]

What matters for this chapter is how the candidate reached a trial and what the trial was. The letter was a published candidate and no more: its authors called their suggestions initial thoughts that required further detailed work. [^25] The trial was designed by other people. The investigators of ACTT-2, in their own paper, noted that baricitinib had been predicted with the use of artificial intelligence algorithms to be a potential therapeutic, and they also cited three case series in which patients given the drug had shown better oxygenation. Enrollment began on May 8, 2020, and ended on July 1, 2020, at 67 sites in 8 countries.

The trial randomized 1,033 hospitalized adults. All received the antiviral remdesivir for up to 10 days, and they received either baricitinib, 4 milligrams daily for up to 14 days, or a matching placebo. "Patients receiving baricitinib had a median time to recovery of 7 days," against 8 days in the control group. [^55]

The trial tested one regimen in one population. It compared baricitinib plus remdesivir with remdesivir alone in hospitalized adults, and it did not test every mechanism the letter proposed. The candidate was published in February 2020 and the trial in December 2020. The two publications fall in the same calendar year, about ten months apart. [^25]

#### Noticed and searched for

Set side by side, the first two cases began with something a person saw. Volunteers in a dosing study reported an effect nobody was looking for, and clinicians reported a response in ENL patients. In both, the relationship arrived as an observation, and the work that followed was to explain and test it. [^42] [^30]

The third case began with a search. Nobody had seen baricitinib help a patient with the new disease when the letter was written. The relationship was assembled from records of what the drug binds and how viruses enter cells, and it existed as a written candidate before any patient was treated on its account. [^25]

Swanson's fish-oil hypothesis, told in chapter III-01, is the fourth point of comparison, and it sits with baricitinib. He found one set of articles on what dietary fish oil does to the blood and a second on blood changes that might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome, and in his words "these two literatures had no articles in common." The BenevolentAI group queried a graph that machines had built, but the route is the same: a candidate stated from the literature, then a trial run independently by others. The lag is three years from the explicit hypothesis in 1986 to the first reported trial in 1989, a validation interval. [^2]

The founding text draws the line that all four cases cross.

> A useful boundary is between an **archaeological candidate**, generated from existing information, and a **validated discovery**, which has survived the appropriate tests. A candidate may require new experiments before it becomes a discovery about the physical world. [LIA §5]

Each case here stands at a different point between the two. Sildenafil has a trial program and an approval. Thalidomide has an approval on historical data.

Baricitinib has one randomized trial of one regimen. Fish oil has a first limited trial. The route by which a candidate was found, at the bedside or by search, does not change the test it has to pass.

Four cases have shown that relationships between compounds and diseases were present in trial reports, clinical observations, and the literature before they were tested, and that each stands at the level of its own trial or approval. The next chapter, III-08, takes up finance, where the records are filings and prices and the tests are retrospective.

<a id="chapter-iii-08"></a>

### III-08. Finance

A firm in the United States that sells a large share of its output to one buyer has to say who the buyer is. Under the accounting standard SFAS No. 131, and before 1997 under SFAS No. 14, a firm reports the identity of any customer that represents more than 10 percent of its total sales. Lauren Cohen and Andrea Frazzini took those disclosures from the Compustat segment files for the years 1980 to 2004 and matched each named customer to a listed company. The matching was manual work. Before 1998 most customers were listed under an abbreviation of their name, and the abbreviation varied from firm to firm and from year to year.

The authors ran a phonetic string-matching algorithm to produce candidate matches and then matched each one by hand, checking the firm's name, segment, and industry. The final sample held 30,622 distinct firm-year relationships, representing 11,484 unique supplier-customer relationships. They then joined the links to stock returns. Their paper, published in the Journal of Finance in August 2008, opens with its result: "This paper finds evidence of return predictability across economically linked firms." A supplier's stock returns predictably followed the returns of its customers. [^26]

Financial research is the fourth of the six domains the framing lists.

> **Financial research:** Structural relationships buried across regulatory filings, supply chains, patents and economic data. [Framing §4]

The three studies in this chapter each join a public or purchasable record to market outcomes. One uses customer disclosures, one uses insiders' trade reports, and one uses satellite images of parking lots. All three are retrospective.

#### Customers and suppliers

Chapter II-03 used this study to show what a rule about time looks like, and gave its central example: Coastcast Corporation, a maker of golf club heads, half of whose sales in 2001 went to Callaway Golf. On June 8, 2001, Callaway cut its revenue projection for the quarter, and by the close of trading its shares were down 30 percent from two days earlier. [^26]

At Coastcast, the share price did not move on the news. Analysts did not revise their earnings forecast for the firm or their recommendations, and the authors' search of newswires and financial publications returned no mention of Coastcast at all in the two months after Callaway's announcement. On July 19 Coastcast announced earnings of minus 4 cents a share, and its price fell over the following two months. The fact that its largest customer had lost 30 percent of its value was public for weeks before the supplier's price reflected it. [^26]

Across the full sample, the authors sorted suppliers each month by how their customers' stocks had done in the previous month. Buying the fifth of suppliers whose customers had the best returns and selling short the fifth whose customers had the worst gave abnormal returns of 1.55 percent a month in their data. The customers in the sample were mostly large firms, on average above the 90th percentile of listed firms by size, so the news being overlooked was news about well-followed companies. [^26]

The test depends on a rule about dates. A customer link reported for a fiscal year is not public until the statements for that year are issued. To make sure each relation was known before the returns it was used to explain, the authors imposed a six-month gap between the fiscal year-end and the returns. [^26]

The study is retrospective and observational, with controls. It is not a prospective test, it does not prove cause, and it is not evidence of a net trading profit available today. Independent replication is not established in the record used here. The ledger assigns no lag, because the sample window and the paper's date do not identify when the connection first became available or was first recognized. [^26]

#### Routine and opportunistic insiders

Corporate insiders are a firm's officers, its board members, and holders of more than 10 percent of its stock. Their open-market trades in the firm's shares must be reported to the Securities and Exchange Commission on Form 4. Lauren Cohen, Christopher Malloy, and Lukasz Pomorski took those filings for January 1986 to December 2007 and asked which trades carried information. Their working paper was issued by the National Bureau of Economic Research in 2010. [^43]

Their sorting rule used nothing but each insider's own history. An insider had to have traded at least once in each of the three preceding years to be classified at all. One who had traded in the same calendar month for at least three consecutive years was labeled a routine trader, on the reasoning that trades made for liquidity or diversification are more regular in their timing than trades made on information. Everyone else was labeled opportunistic. The label was set at the start of each calendar year from past trades only, and every trade the insider made afterward went into the corresponding group. [^43]

Routine trades made up over half of all insider trades and, by the authors' results, predicted nothing. A portfolio built only on opportunistic trades showed value-weighted abnormal returns of 82 basis points a month, a basis point being one hundredth of a percent, while the returns associated with routine traders were essentially zero. The paper also reports that "opportunistic trades predict future news and events at a firm level," while routine trades do not. [^43]

The limits are stated in the paper and in the ledger. The classification is a noisy proxy; the authors say it will not sort every trade correctly. It does not reveal any insider's motive and is not proof of illegal trading. The analysis is retrospective, the source is a working paper that had not been peer reviewed when issued, and it is not an independently replicated prospective test. No lag is assigned. [^43]

#### Cars in parking lots

The third study joins a record that is not a filing. From the first quarter of 2011, a vendor named RS Metrics sold daily counts of cars in retailers' parking lots, extracted from satellite images by software and checked by human analysts. Zsolt Katona, Marcus Painter, Panos Patatoukas, and Jean Zeng obtained the store-level data: 4.7 million daily observations across 67,078 store locations for 44 major US retailers from 2011 to 2017. From it they built, for each retailer and quarter, a measure of how full its lots were, giving 650 firm-quarter observations. Their article appeared in the Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, published online in 2024. [^44]

"We focus on predicting quarterly earnings announcements," the authors write. They report that growth in the parking-lot fill rate added to what was otherwise known in anticipating a retailer's results for the quarter, and that the relation was lopsided: a fall in lot use was an especially strong sign of a weak quarter. In the three days around earnings announcements, retailers with abnormal increases in fill rate outperformed the market by 1.6 percent, and those with abnormal decreases underperformed by nearly 3 percent, net of the fees for borrowing stock to sell short. [^44]

The idea was old. The authors note that Sam Walton, founder of Walmart, was known for flying over parking lots to count cars. What changed in 2011 was that the counting could be done daily and at scale, for a price. The authors stress that price: the data was within reach of select hedge funds and out of reach of most other investors, and their own subject is the unequal access that resulted. [^44]

These are published observational backtests. Trading costs, the cost of access to the data, and live deployment are separate questions that the study's returns do not settle. No lag is assigned, because commercial availability and later publication do not establish when investors first recognized or used the signal. [^44]

#### What the three show

All three studies are retrospective and observational. None shows a profit available today after costs. Each reports a pattern in past data over a stated window, the latest of which closed in 2017. [^26] [^43] [^44]

What they establish is narrower and is the point of the chapter: the relationships were in the record. The customer's name was in the supplier's filing. The insider's habit was in years of that insider's own filings. In each case the pieces sat in separate places and the study's contribution was the join, performed under a rule that kept later information out. The founding text's first consequence of its formal boundary names the three things such a study has to fix.

> Latency is relative to a corpus, a historical boundary, and a method of investigation. [LIA §4]

Each study names its corpus, dates what was available when, and states its method of sorting. The second consequence applies as well: "A candidate in the discovery space is not necessarily true." [LIA §4] A pattern found in past returns is a candidate about future ones, and these studies did not test it prospectively.

Three retrospective studies have shown structural relationships present in filings, trade reports, and imagery before prices reflected them. The next chapter, III-09, turns to organizations, where the records are internal and the reconstruction is of what an institution had been told.

<a id="chapter-iii-09"></a>

### III-09. Organizations

On December 2, 2001, Enron Corporation, then the seventh largest publicly traded corporation in the United States, declared bankruptcy. A month later the Senate's Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations announced an investigation into the collapse. It issued over 50 subpoenas to Enron board members, Enron officers, the corporation, and the Andersen accounting firm, Enron's outside auditor.

By May 2002 the subcommittee staff had reviewed over 350 boxes of documents, among them the available meeting minutes, presentations, and attachments for the full board and for its Finance and Audit Committees. In April 2002 the staff interviewed 13 past and present directors, in sessions that lasted between three and eight hours. Five directors testified under oath at a hearing on May 7, and the report, *The Role of the Board of Directors in Enron's Collapse*, is dated July 8, 2002. [^45]

In their interviews the directors indicated that the collapse had surprised them as much as anyone. The documents gave a different account. From them the subcommittee prepared a chart of more than a dozen incidents over three years that should have raised the board's concern. The first entry on the chart is an Audit Committee meeting in February 1999. At that meeting, the report says, board members were told that "Enron was using accounting practices that" tested the limits of what was acceptable. [^45]

Two years separate the documented board warning in 1999 from the collapse in 2001. The warning was not necessarily public information. It was given to a committee of directors, and the case does not show that every investor had access to board materials in 1999 or that the collapse was mechanically predictable. [^45]

#### The fifth domain

Section 4 of the framing lists six domains under the heading "Where the greatest potential is." The fifth concerns organizations.

> **Organizational intelligence:** Reconstructing how companies actually function from fragmented operational records. [Framing §4]

The Enron report shows what "fragmented" means inside one company. The board had 15 members in 2001 and worked through five committees. It normally met five times a year.

Committee meetings generally lasted between one and two hours, and so did meetings of the full board. Minutes were kept by the Corporate Secretary, who took handwritten notes, circulated drafts, and presented them for approval at the following meeting. Management supplied agendas, briefing materials, and, for the Finance Committee, approval sheets for individual deals. Each of these papers recorded one presentation to one group on one day. [^45]

The subcommittee's work was to put those papers into a single dated sequence. Read that way, they show what was presented and when. Three times in 1999 and 2000 the board was asked to approve, and did approve, an arrangement under which Enron's chief financial officer set up private equity funds, the LJM partnerships, to do business with Enron.

Three times in 2000 it approved a set of complex transactions called the Raptors. A presentation to the Finance Committee in October 2000 stated that Enron had a total of $60 billion in assets, of which about $27 billion were held by affiliates that did not appear in Enron's financial statements. In April 2001 board members were told that 64 percent of Enron's assets were troubled or performing below expectations. [^45]

No single one of these papers states the conclusion the subcommittee reached. The conclusion came from the sequence: the report found that Enron's high-risk accounting practices were not hidden from the board, that the board knew of them, and that it took no action to prevent their use. The finding is a reconstruction, made after the fact by a Senate subcommittee holding subpoena power, from over one million pages of subpoenaed documents. [^45]

#### Complaints in a regulator's files

The second reconstruction concerns a regulator. Chapter II-01 introduced the case of Bernard Madoff as an inference that could be drawn from his own reported figures. Here the subject is the Securities and Exchange Commission and what its files held. On September 10, 2009, the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs heard the SEC's inspector general present an investigation report of over 450 pages, issued on August 31. He testified that between June 1992 and December 2008, when Madoff confessed, the SEC received six substantive complaints, and that it knew of two articles from 2001 that questioned Madoff's returns. [^19]

The second of the six complaints reached the SEC in versions dated May 2000, March 2001, and October 2005. The committee chairman said at the hearing that Harry Markopolos, an investment analyst, had given detailed analysis to SEC staff from 2000 to 2008. The inspector general testified that Markopolos's complaint set out two scenarios, a Ponzi scheme, which it rated highly likely, and front running, which it rated unlikely. A third complaint, received in May 2003 from a hedge fund manager, questioned whether Madoff was trading options in the volume he claimed. A fourth was a series of internal emails at another firm, found by SEC examiners in April 2004, in which employees listed among their warning signs "his unusually consistent, non-volatile returns over several years." [^19]

The pattern of returns was one half of the argument and the options market was the other. The 2003 complaint questioned the claimed volume, and one of the 2004 emails gave a step-by-step analysis of why Madoff must be misrepresenting his options trading. The SEC's own work added further pieces.

Two examinations ran in different offices at the same time, and neither office knew of the other; Madoff himself told one team that the other already had the material it was asking for. An assistant director obtained information from a financial institution showing no transaction activity in Madoff's account for a specified period and did not pass it to the examination team. Enforcement staff received a report that Madoff held no option positions on a certain date and took no further step. When the inspector general's office later compared customer statements with the records of an independent clearing entity for particular dates, the volumes did not come near what Madoff had reported. [^19]

That interval measures the SEC's delay. The warning was on file in 2000, eight years before the public exposure in 2008. The events of 2008 confirmed what some observers had already inferred from several pieces of evidence. [^19]

#### Where the records disagreed

The fifth principle of the founding text names the feature the two cases share.

> **Contradictions are excavation sites.** Inconsistencies can indicate missing variables, incompatible assumptions, errors, or genuine opportunities for explanation. [LIA §6]

At Enron the contradiction lay between what directors said they had known and what their own minutes and presentations recorded. One director testified that the board could not be criticized for failing to address problems that had been concealed from it, and the report answers that much of what was wrong was not concealed. The board was also told that gross revenues had risen from $40 billion in 1999 to $100 billion in 2000, and the report notes that apparently no member questioned the figure. [^45] At the SEC the contradictions were between Madoff's statements and outside records, and the inspector general testified that examiners caught Madoff in contradictions and inconsistencies and then accepted his answers at face value. [^19]

In both cases the place where two records disagreed was the place to dig, and in both the digging was done late. The principle lists four things an inconsistency can indicate, and error is one of them. A contradiction marks a site. What lies under it is settled by checking an independent record, which is the step the SEC's examiners drafted letters for and did not take. [^19]

#### The same task for a state

A state keeps operational records as a company does, over a much longer time. The Venice Time Machine, a project that began in 2013 with an agreement between the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne and Ca' Foscari University, set out to turn the holdings of the State Archives of Venice into an online information system. In 2017 the partners announced "190,000 digitizations of archival documents," along with a search engine built on names, places, and keywords that archivists had entered by hand. The cited chronology gives no pair of dates from which a discovery lag could be inferred, and digitization alone does not validate every network or event inferred from the documents. [^46]

What an organization knew is often written down inside it, in pieces, and the cases here show that a contradiction between the pieces is where reconstruction starts. The next chapter tells the Venice case in full, beside three other reconstructions from incomplete archives.

### III-10. Historical reconstruction

On February 23, 2013, the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL) and Ca' Foscari University signed the agreement that began the Venice Time Machine. The following year the State Archives of Venice joined them in a formal program whose aim was to turn the documentary heritage of the archives into an information system available online to researchers, specialists, and the general public. EPFL supplied scanners, servers, and computers for a digitization space inside the State Archives, which was inaugurated in June 2014, and it hired and trained five Italian specialists to run the scanners and annotate the documents. Two related efforts started in 2015: one to digitize the photo library of the Giorgio Cini Foundation, about one million images, and one to study more than 2,000 monographs on Venetian history through their citations. [^46]

In October 2017 the partners announced the first results of the campaign. The project's chronology lists "190,000 digitizations of archival documents," 720,000 photographic documents, and 3,000 books covering 200 years of Venetian historiography, more than 2 million digitized images in all. Archivists had entered 160,000 names, locations, and keywords by hand, and a search engine using a handwriting recognition system based on those annotations was announced with the figures.

The engine was shown to the public in 2018. It combines text search, visual search, and navigation by place and period across the sources of the State Archives and the Cini Foundation. The chronology records the end of the digitization campaign with the State Archives in 2019. [^46]

#### The sixth domain

The last of the six domains in section 4 of the framing is the one this chapter covers.

> **Historical reconstruction:** Recovering events, networks and relationships from incomplete archives. [Framing §4]

The sentence names three things to recover and one condition on the source. The four cases here are four different kinds of incomplete archive: a city's records that survive in bulk and were unsearchable, a library that survives and could not be opened, a machine that survives as corroded fragments, and one man's papers that survive scattered across more than twenty collections. In each the reconstruction is real and bounded, and the bound can be stated.

#### What the Venice figures establish

The chronology documents a body of data and a set of tools. It reports that the handwriting recognition system developed from the annotated Venetian documents, presented in 2018, performed better than an Italian reader without archival training. A document segmentation system built for the project was released as open source. These are statements about access. They say that a page can now be found by a name written on it, by an image that resembles another image, or by a location in the city. [^46]

The chronology also records one historical result drawn from digitized records. In 2020 EPFL researchers published an analysis of daily death records from the city's Patriarchal Archives covering the plague in Venice between 1630 and 1631. The deaths showed a first peak in 1630 that reached over 400 per day at its worst and a less acute but longer peak in 1631. [^46] That finding is an event recovered from an archive, which is the framing's first item, and it rests on one named series of records that could be counted.

Networks and relationships are a different matter. A search that returns every page on which a name appears has returned candidates for a network. Whether two entries refer to the same person, and whether the tie between them was what it appears to be, are questions for the documents and for a historian. The project's own chronology supports the data and the demonstrations, and digitization alone does not validate every historical network or event inferred from them. The cited record also gives no pair of dates from which a discovery lag could be inferred, and none is stated here. [^46]

#### One scroll

Chapter I-01 opened on the Vesuvius Challenge: the scans released in 2023, the grand prize announced in February 2024, the papyrologists who worked to "review 15 columns of text in anonymized submissions." [^4] What this chapter adds is how the reading was tied to the record, and how much of the record it covers.

The organizers set out the method in three steps. A scroll is scanned by X-ray tomography. The crumpled layers of papyrus are traced through the three-dimensional scan and flattened, a step called segmentation. A machine-learning model then identifies the inked regions of the flattened surface.

The entrants were independent competitors, and the winning team of three formed out of people who had each won an earlier prize for a different part of the problem. One had trained a model on a crackle pattern that another contestant had first noticed in the flattened surfaces. One had adapted ink-detection models built on detached fragments, where photographs supplied ground truth. The third had written segmentation software that the organizers' own team used. [^4]

The organizers give three reasons the images can be trusted. The technical team reran the winning code and obtained similar images. Different entrants, using different models and different training labels on the same area of the scroll, produced extremely similar results. And the models are not built on Greek letters or on a language model: they detect small spots of ink through small windows, and the writing appears only when the spots are put together. A model of that kind has little room to produce letter shapes of its own. [^4]

The limits are stated just as plainly in the same announcement. The winning entry met the launch criterion of four passages of 140 characters each with at least 85 percent of the characters recoverable and included 11 further columns. [^4] About 5 percent of one scroll had been unwrapped and read, leaving roughly 95 percent unread. The readings released were labeled initial, rough drafts.

Tracing the papyrus surface was still largely manual and had cost more than $100 per square centimeter. Four scrolls had been scanned, and the goal set for 2024 was to read 90 percent of them. [^4] About one year separates the challenge data release in 2023 from the prize announcement in 2024. The age of the ancient text is not a discovery lag. The result covers passages from a scroll, not the whole library. [^4]

#### Two cases return

Chapter I-05 introduced the Antikythera mechanism and the archive of Zenon to show what time and position do to a record. They return here for their limits.

The 2006 paper by Freeth and colleagues reports "surface imaging and high-resolution X-ray tomography of the surviving fragments." Its abstract says the imaging allowed the team to reconstruct the gear function and to double the number of deciphered inscriptions. It reports that the mechanism predicted lunar and solar eclipses and that its gearing realized a theory of the Moon's irregular motion.

The same abstract marks the edges of the result. It says it was already known that the device calculated and displayed celestial information. It says the gears and inscriptions are only fragmentary. On a display of planetary positions it says that the inscriptions support suggestions of one, and that the display itself is lost. [^17] So the 105 years run from the recovery in 1901 to the 2006 CT-based reconstruction, not to first recognition of the mechanism, and the 2006 result is one specific reconstruction. [^17]

The Zenon record is a catalogue entry for an archive of 1,819 certain texts, and it is frank about what remains open. Zenon endorsed many incoming letters with the place and date of receipt and the subject, and those notes are how scholars reconstruct where he was, especially during his travels in the Nile Delta. Letters make up almost 40 percent of the archive, and petitions and accounts about 10 percent each.

Yet the catalogue reports that it is not always easy to decide which documents belong to Zenon's personal papers and which to the estate's dossier, and that scholars have disagreed on the attribution. It is not clear how one text, dated well before the rest, fits the archive at all. The last holder of the papers, who kept them for some ten years after Zenon, is unnamed, and his identification with Zenon's brother is offered with a question mark. [^18] The catalogue documents the dispersed archive and the 1980 guide by Pestman's team. A complete reconstruction of the network of people in the papers is not demonstrated by this source alone, and no discovery lag is inferred. [^18]

#### The limits as part of the finding

Section 11 of the founding text closes its account of the archaeological metaphor with two sentences.

> The work is excavation, reconstruction, interpretation, and verification. It is as much about understanding the limits of the record as it is about discovering what the record contains. [LIA §11]

Each of the four cases carries its limit inside the source that reports the result. The Venice chronology counts digitized documents and annotations, which are measures of access. [^46] The prize announcement puts the unread share of the scroll beside the columns that were read. [^4] The Antikythera abstract separates what was reconstructed from what is only suggested and what is lost. [^17] The Zenon catalogue lists the texts still unpublished and the attributions still disputed. [^18]

The same section says that an absence in the record "may be meaningful, or it may simply reflect what was never recorded or what failed to survive." [LIA §11] In all four cases the record is incomplete for reasons unrelated to the question being asked of it: an eruption, a shipwreck, a sale in bundles on the antiquities market. A reconstruction from such a record states where the evidence stops.

Historical reconstruction from incomplete archives recovers events, texts, and mechanisms that can be checked against the artifact, and it leaves networks as archaeological candidates until someone tests them. With this the six domains of the framing have each been shown on cases. The next chapter takes up what happens between fields, when a method made in one is carried into another.

<a id="chapter-iii-11"></a>

### III-11. Between fields

In 1974 Shapley and Scarf published a model of a housing market. It has a set of agents, and each agent owns one indivisible good, a house. Every agent ranks all the houses. There is no money, so the only trade possible is house for house.

The paper credits David Gale with a procedure for this market called top trading cycles. Each agent points to the house they like best, and each house points to its owner. The pointing must close into at least one cycle. Everyone in a cycle receives the house they pointed to and leaves the market with it, and the procedure repeats among those who remain until nobody is left. [^47]

In 2004 Alvin Roth, Tayfun Sönmez, and Utku Ünver published "Kidney Exchange" in the Quarterly Journal of Economics. Their subject was patients who have a willing living donor whose kidney they cannot receive, because of blood type or because a test before transplantation shows the patient carries antibodies against the donor's tissue. The paper reports that more than 55,000 patients were on the United States waiting list for a cadaver kidney, that about 3,400 died while waiting in 2002, and that buying or selling an organ is a felony under a 1984 federal law. A market with indivisible goods, differing preferences, and no money was the market Shapley and Scarf had modeled. The authors called their proposal a "generalization of the TTC mechanism, for kidney exchange." [^47]

The founding text has this kind of event in view from its third paragraph.

> An observation made in one field may resolve a problem in another. [LIA Preamble]

It returns to the point in §10, where the list of early research priorities includes this one.

> **Cross-domain transfer.** Test whether useful principles, technologies, and explanations can be recovered across distinct vocabularies and institutional boundaries. [LIA §10]

This chapter takes four transfers of method between fields and asks the same three things of each: what was carried over, what had to change, and how far apart in time the two sides stand.

#### What carried over and what did not

In the kidney case the 2004 paper answers the first two questions itself. An incompatible donor and patient take the place of a house and its owner. Where every patient in the exchange has a donor, the authors state that the properties of the housing market carry over essentially unchanged, provided each donor's preferences are taken to match those of the intended recipient. Gale's procedure can then be used directly. [^47]

Medicine does not stop there. Some patients have no living donor, and cadaver kidneys belong to nobody in particular. For this the authors drew on a second housing model, a 1999 study of college dormitories in which some students already hold rooms, others are newcomers, and some rooms are vacant. Newcomers correspond to patients without donors and vacant rooms to cadaver kidneys.

Here the correspondence breaks, and the paper names the break: a housing office knows which rooms are vacant, while nobody knows which cadaver kidneys will arrive or when. Rooms can be assigned all at once. Cadaver kidneys cannot. The authors therefore added an option that the housing models lack, in which a patient trades the donor's kidney for priority on the waiting list, and they extended the cycles into chains that end at that list. They also assumed that every operation in a cycle is carried out simultaneously, since a donor's willingness could change once the intended recipient has a kidney. [^47]

Thirty years separate the 1974 house-allocation model from the 2004 kidney-exchange formulation. The figure is not a delay in performing transplants. The paper itself records that exchanges between two donor-patient pairs had been proposed in 1986 and were already being performed in small numbers, and that a three-pair exchange took place at Johns Hopkins in 2003. What the paper contains is theoretical analysis and simulations. Later clinical programs are separate empirical validation, and constraints of medical feasibility between each donor and each patient rule out a literal transfer of the house market. [^47]

The second transfer was introduced in chapter II-01 as the example of contextual latency. Fischer Black and Myron Scholes set out in 1973 to value an option, which their paper defines as a security giving the right to buy or sell an asset, subject to conditions, within a specified period. They assumed what they called ideal conditions: a known and constant interest rate, a stock price that follows a random walk in continuous time, no dividends, no transaction costs, and no penalty for short selling.

Under those assumptions a position that holds the stock and sells the option short can be kept free of risk, so it must earn the riskless rate. That condition gives a differential equation for the option's value. After a substitution of variables the authors wrote that "differential equation (10) is the heat-transfer equation of physics," and took its solution from a 1963 textbook on Fourier series and boundary value problems. [^20]

What carried over was a solved equation. What had to be supplied was everything that made the option's value obey it, and that argument is economic and owes nothing to heat. A later set of lecture notes states the relation in one line: the "Black-Scholes PDE can be reduced to the linear heat equation." [^60]

The reduction is exact under the model's assumptions and does not validate them. Fourier's treatise on the conduction of heat dates from 1822, so 151 years separate it from the 1973 paper. This is a method-transfer chronology, not evidence that the formula was knowable in 1822. [^20]

The third and fourth cases are brief. Chapter II-05 told how Page, Brin, and their colleagues in 1998 treated a link between web pages as a kind of academic citation, and how they named the difference: web pages are not reviewed and links can be manufactured. Their paper says "there is already a large literature on academic citation analysis." No lag is stated for this case, because that literature has no single defensible year of availability, and no line of descent from Leontief's input-output models is claimed. [^31]

The fourth case rests on a secondary scholarly history, the Dictionary of Scientific Biography, and not on the original memoirs. According to that entry, Ostrogradsky spent 1822 to 1827 in Paris, where Fourier and Poisson worked. The method of separating variables had been applied by "Fourier in his work on the conduction of heat (1822)," and by Poisson, to individual problems. In a note on the theory of heat, "presented in 1828 and published in 1831," Ostrogradsky was the first to formulate a general schema for solving boundary-value problems by that method.

The same entry reports that his larger aim was a single method covering hydromechanics, elasticity, heat, and electricity, and that the aim was never realized. Six years separate Fourier's 1822 treatise from the 1828 presentation; a lag measured to publication would end in 1831. The entry also records that Lamé and Duhamel presented analogous general results in 1829, so the case does not establish that the insight was Ostrogradsky's alone. [^48]

#### The form of a problem

In none of these cases was the earlier work lost. The housing model was cited in economics, the heat equation was taught, and citation counts were in use. Something else was missing, and the fourth principle of the founding text names it.

> **The question may itself be the discovery.** Recognizing that a problem can be asked differently is a legitimate outcome. [LIA §6]

Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver asked a question about organ shortage as a question about trading indivisible goods without money. Black and Scholes asked a question about the value of a security and arrived at an equation whose solution was in print. Page and Brin asked which web pages matter as a question about who cites whom.

Each time the step was seeing that one problem had the form of another, and each time the authors then listed where the forms part. The listing is what made the transfers usable. A kidney exchange that ignored the difference between a vacant room and a cadaver kidney would have been a mechanism for a market that does not exist.

Stuart Kauffman offers a term for why such a step is open at one time and not at another. Answering a 2004 question that asked contributors to propose a law, he wrote: "The adjacent possible, for a chemical reaction graph, is the set of novel molecules that can be created out of those existing now." He extended the thought to technology and attached his own caution: "I'm very unsure about my candidate law." [^49]

The term fits the cases as a way of speaking. The house-market model could be adapted to kidneys only once paired exchange was a practice with something to organize. The heat equation could price an option only once the hedging argument existed. Kauffman's remark is commentary and supplies no forecasting rule, and none is drawn from it here.

#### Candidates on the same pattern

The project record contains a set of cross-field candidates written to the pattern the four cases show. The file `projects/latent-information-archaeology/research/latent-connections-crossfield-v0.1.md` states that eight candidates were each examined by a dedicated skeptic for prior art, for whether the shared structure was the same mathematical object or a loose analogy, for a prediction that could turn out wrong, for derivability, and for triviality. It records seven as killed and one as surviving with the status of an exploratory candidate. The survivor maps a scaling law from condensed-matter physics, the Kibble-Zurek mechanism, onto the freezing of opinion into domains.

The file gives the disanalogy: the mapping fails on the voter model, the standard model of opinion, and holds only on a majority-vote model. It lists the nearest prior art, including a 2023 paper that applied the same mechanism to a musical transition. It names the test, which is to predict an exponent in advance and then check it in simulation, with the voter model as a negative control. The file states that novelty is unverified, that no novelty claim is made, and that the candidate is not promoted into the claims register.

Four audited cases now show a method crossing from one field to another, with what changed in the crossing and the two dates each interval joins. Seven of the eight candidates above were closed on review, and the next chapter takes up connections that failed.

<a id="chapter-iii-12"></a>

### III-12. Connections that failed

In November 2008 Nature published online a paper by Ginsberg and colleagues at Google. Its abstract begins the proposal plainly: "Here we present a method of analysing large numbers of Google search queries" to track influenza-like illness in a population. The authors took five years of search logs, from 2003 to 2008, and computed weekly counts for 50 million of the most common queries in the United States. They tested each query separately for how well it fit the weekly percentage of physician visits for influenza-like illness reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention across nine regions.

The 45 best-scoring queries were combined into a single measure, and a linear model was fit to it. On 42 data points per region held back from fitting, the estimates had a mean correlation of 0.97 with the CDC figures. The CDC published with a delay of one to two weeks. The model could report with a lag of about one day. [^50]

In March 2014 Lazer, Kennedy, King, and Vespignani published a critique in Science. Its central figure carries the finding: "GFT overestimated the prevalence" of flu in the 2012–2013 season, and overshot the actual level in 2011–2012 by more than 50 percent. From 21 August 2011 to 1 September 2013, Google Flu Trends reported flu prevalence that was too high in 100 out of 108 weeks. The critique opens on February 2013, when Nature reported that the model was predicting more than double the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness that the CDC was, although it had been built to predict the CDC's reports. [^61]

The founding text has a principle for a case like this one.

> **Negative results belong in the record.** Failed connections, rejected hypotheses, and uncertainty should be retained when possible so future investigators do not repeat the same excavation. [LIA §6]

This chapter gives failed connections the treatment the preceding chapters gave successful ones: what was proposed, what was checked, what the verdict was, and what remains for the next investigator.

#### How the model drifted

The 2014 critique points to two causes. The first lies in how the queries were chosen. Fifty million candidate terms were matched against 1,152 data points, and with that many candidates some will track the flu season without having anything to do with flu. [^61]

The 2008 paper had itself noted that queries about high school basketball scored well, since the basketball season and the flu season coincide. The critique reads that as a warning of overfitting. The original model then missed the 2009 H1N1 pandemic, which arrived outside the usual season, and was revised in 2009. [^61]

The second cause is that the source of the data kept changing. Search behavior depends on what the search engine shows people, and the engine was under constant revision; the critique counts 86 changes announced in June and July 2012 alone. In 2011 Google began suggesting related search terms, and in 2012 it began returning possible diagnoses for searches on symptoms such as fever and cough. The model assumed a fixed relation between the share of certain queries and the level of illness. The service that produced the queries did not hold that relation fixed. [^61]

The critique also compared the model with something simple. A projection built only from CDC data already two or three weeks old tracked current flu levels better than Google Flu Trends did. Over the out-of-sample period the mean absolute error was 0.486 for Google Flu Trends, 0.311 for the lagged CDC model, and 0.232 for a model that combined the two.

The authors did not conclude that search data are worthless. Their combined model outperformed either source alone, and they called the original aim fundamentally correct. They also reported that the 45 queries had never been published, so the original analysis could not be replicated from outside. [^61]

Six years separate the original model's publication from the 2014 critique. The errors emerged within that interval, so the figure is not a precise failure-onset lag. The case shows a connection that passed its first validation and then failed as the conditions that produced its data changed. [^50]

#### Two candidates closed at first review

The project record behind this text holds two cross-field candidates that failed earlier than that, before any data were touched. Each was closed at its first review with the same verdict: "KILL-NOVELTY + KILL-SPECIFICATION + DEFER-CORPUS." The first two terms close the claim to novelty and the specification as written. The third defers any corpus work. [^51] [^52]

The first candidate linked information theory to editorial normalization. It proposed a ratio law: when a text or a piece of code is standardized, the ratio of the information tied to its producer to the remaining uncertainty should move in a set direction, and the direction should depend on whether the standardizing was done by a human editor's attention or by a mechanical rule. The review file `projects/latent-information-archaeology/research/constraint-distillation-stage0-results-v0.1.md` records what was checked.

The algebra came first. For the ratio to rise, the numerator must fall proportionally less than the denominator, and the candidate's own falsifier required the opposite while calling the result enrichment. No universal sign follows in any case, since coarsening can lower both quantities and leave the ratio to rise or fall. The division of standardizers into attention and rule was not identified, because a human editor follows rules and software, and an autoformatter runs rules that people designed.

Then came prior art. The file cites a 2024 study by Balla, Gabbrielli, and Zacchiroli that ran two formatting tools over a paired corpus of 590 Python files by 59 authors and saw authorship attribution fall from 67.86% on the original files to 52.68% formatted and 50.00% minified, with signal remaining. The effect of mechanical normalization on producer signal was already measured and published. The file states what was not run: no corpus was acquired, no intervention was performed, and no mutual-information estimate was fitted. [^51]

The second candidate proposed carrying estimators from survey sampling into archives. Historical records survive unevenly. If each surviving item could be weighted by the inverse of its probability of surviving, as a survey statistician weights respondents, the distribution of features among the lost items could be corrected. The review file `projects/latent-information-archaeology/research/survival-ascertainment-stage0-results-v0.1.md` records the checks. The estimator in question is unbiased for totals when inclusion probabilities are known and positive; it does not make nonlinear quantities such as entropy unbiased.

Survival is not a probability sample, and items destroyed because of their features cannot be restored by reweighting the survivors. The null hypothesis the candidate offered was false as stated. Prior art in the humanities already occupied the ground: the file cites work by Green, McIntyre, and Needham modeling the survival of incunables, the earliest printed books, by format and leaf count, and work by Lahti, Mäkelä, and Tolonen modeling how far one collection covers a larger catalog by gender, genre, and time. A proposed test bed built from deleted Git branches was found to be unrecoverable after the fact. No corpus was acquired, no propensity was fitted, and no feature distribution was estimated. [^52]

Neither file records a lag, because neither establishes a first date of availability and a first date of recognition. Neither candidate is a finding. Each is preserved as a rejected candidate. [^51] [^52]

#### What a failure leaves behind

Each of these failures leaves something a later investigator can use. Google Flu Trends leaves a benchmark and a named assumption: compare any new proxy with lagged official data, and check whether the process generating the proxy is stable. The constraint-distillation file leaves the corrected algebra, the citations that anyone proposing a similar law must read first, and a narrower replication that reports the two quantities separately and infers nothing about attention; it was named and not run. The survival file leaves a list of the conditions under which reweighting is legitimate and a narrower audit of one observable selection stage, such as which cataloged items were digitized, which concerns inclusion and never physical survival. The first file states that its replication infers no novelty, and the second that its audit never claims recovery.

A fourth failure, from machine learning, left tools behind in the same way. Burns and colleagues had proposed finding what a language model knows from its internal activations without labels, using the idea that knowledge satisfies a consistency structure. In 2023 Farquhar and colleagues at Google DeepMind proved that arbitrary features satisfy that structure too, and showed in experiments that the method returned classifiers predicting some other prominent feature. They contributed sanity checks for evaluating future methods. Their result concerns the methods they tested and does not show that no such recovery is possible. [^22]

The founding text said in advance that outcomes like these would be common.

> Some candidates will be false, others trivial, and others impossible to resolve without new observations. [LIA §2]

The record now holds one example of each kind. The sign law in the first candidate was false: its algebra ran backwards. The qualitative core of the same candidate was trivial in the sense that matters here, since the review file reports that the candidate's own text called it textbook material and carried an instruction not to claim novelty. The physical survival of archives is impossible to resolve from survivors alone, because no reweighting recovers what was destroyed on account of its features. Sorting candidates into these kinds is ordinary work, and §2 follows the sentence with one more: "LIA exists to distinguish among them." [LIA §2]

Part III has now followed excavation from Swanson's two literatures through materials, patents, pharmaceuticals, finance, organizations, historical reconstruction, and the space between fields, and has shown through these cases that discoveries are made from existing informational artifacts and that some candidates fail. Part IV, "The discipline," takes up what turns excavation into a discipline: the fundamental problem, the principles, the method, the record, and the tests.

## Part IV: The discipline

<a id="chapter-iv-01"></a>

### IV-01. The fundamental problem

In 2022 Burns, Ye, Klein, and Steinhardt posted a preprint proposing a way to find what a language model knows without asking it and without telling it what is true. Their method, contrast-consistent search, takes a yes-or-no question, writes it out twice, once with each answer attached, and records the model's internal activations for both versions. It then looks for a direction in those activations that behaves the way truth behaves under negation: a statement and its negation should receive opposite values. No labels are used, and the model's own outputs are not used either. The authors reported that across six models and ten question-answering datasets the method beat zero-shot accuracy by 4 percent on average, and they presented it as an initial step toward finding what language models know as distinct from what they say. [^53]

In 2023 Farquhar and colleagues at Google DeepMind and Google Research tested that claim. They proved that the consistency condition at the center of the method is satisfied by arbitrary features of the activations, and is not satisfied only by knowledge. They then built experiments in which a second, irrelevant feature was planted in the prompts. In those experiments the method returned a classifier for the planted feature. Their conclusion was that the unsupervised methods they tested do not find knowledge and instead find whatever feature of the activations is most prominent. [^22]

Chapter II-01 introduced this result when it took up the fifth kind of latency, and Chapter III-12 listed it among the connections that failed. This chapter uses it for what the framing uses it for.


![Six questions for evaluating a candidate discovery](figures/F11.png)

#### The problem as the framing states it

The framing lists latent knowledge elicitation among its scientific precedents.

> **Latent Knowledge Elicitation:** Attempts to extract information encoded within neural networks that isn't reliably expressed through normal prompting. [Framing §2]

The problem behind that line was posed in general terms a year before the Burns preprint. On December 14, 2021, the Alignment Research Center announced its first technical report, on eliciting latent knowledge, and called it an open problem that the authors believed to be central to the alignment of AI systems. The announcement gives the core difficulty as learning how to map between an AI's model of the world and a human's model. The announcement presents a problem. It does not present a technique that extracts truth from a model. [^54]

Contrast-consistent search was one attempt at a part of that problem, and the 2023 paper was a test of the attempt. The framing draws its warning from that sequence.

> The biggest risk is confusing a plausible inference with an actual discovery. DeepMind researchers showed that some unsupervised latent knowledge extraction techniques identify prominent neural features without actually identifying truthful knowledge.
>
> A serious LIA system needs to evaluate discoveries along several independent dimensions:
> - **Novelty:** Does anybody already know this?
> - **Provenance:** Where did the underlying information come from?
> - **Validity:** Is the inference logically and empirically sound?
> - **Falsifiability:** Can we design a test that disproves it?
> - **Utility:** Does discovering it actually matter?
> - **Temporal integrity:** Could this genuinely have been discovered using only information available at the time?
>
> [Framing §5]

The second sentence of the first paragraph refers to the 2023 paper. The rest of this chapter takes that result in more detail and then goes through the six questions, each with a case already told in which a claim fails on that question.

#### What the method found

The Burns proposal rested on a premise that the preprint states directly. A model's representation of truth must satisfy logical consistency properties, and the authors held that few other features would satisfy them. [^53]

The 2023 paper attacks the premise in two ways. The first is mathematical. The authors prove two results about the loss function that contrast-consistent search minimizes: a class of arbitrary binary classifiers is optimal under that loss, and there is a transformation that preserves the loss while turning a classifier into an arbitrary one. They draw the consequence that the consistency structure is compatible with arbitrary patterns. [^22]

The second is experimental. The authors worked with three of the datasets the original preprint had used, including the IMDb set of movie reviews labeled as positive or negative, and with three language models, one of which, T5-11B, had been used in the original work. The largest was Chinchilla, with 70 billion parameters. They trained probes from 50 random seeds for each method and ran the same activations through two standard unsupervised techniques, principal component analysis and k-means clustering, alongside contrast-consistent search. [^22]

In the first experiment they appended one of two random words, Banana or Shed, to each movie-review prompt, assigning the words by a random split of the questions. On the unmodified prompts every unsupervised method scored well at telling positive reviews from negative ones, in line with the original results. On the modified prompts the accuracy on sentiment fell to 50 percent, which is chance. Principal component analysis and k-means separated the Banana prompts from the Shed prompts with high accuracy, and a substantial number of the contrast-consistent search seeds did so perfectly. [^22]

The authors grant that no real dataset would contain a feature so crude, and the second experiment is closer to ordinary text. Each prompt introduced a character named Alice, said to be an expert whose answers everyone copies, and then stated Alice's opinion that the review was positive or that it was negative, again assigned at random. The methods learned to predict Alice's stated opinion and failed to predict the sentiment of the review.

In a third experiment the opinion was implicit. Alice was shown answering topic-classification questions correctly except when the topic was a company, and no opinion was stated on the final question. Almost half of the contrast-consistent search probes then predicted what Alice would answer on the company questions, and not the actual topic, while performing well on every other category. [^22]

A fourth experiment planted nothing. The authors took a question-answering dataset built around common misconceptions and tried three prompt templates that a practitioner could reasonably choose. The accuracy of the resulting classifiers differed from template to template. The authors observe that if the methods were detecting knowledge, an instruction placed in front of the question should not matter. [^22]

The paper states the scope of its result. It concludes that existing unsupervised methods are insufficient for discovering latent knowledge. It does not prove that latent knowledge can never be recovered from a model. Its experiments cover the methods that existed, and the authors mark the extension to future methods as a hypothesis: a model that can simulate the beliefs of other agents will hold features for those beliefs, and such features satisfy the same consistency properties that knowledge does. It contributes its modified prompts as sanity checks for any later method. [^22]

In each planted-feature experiment the method produced a classifier that met its consistency condition. The output looked the same whether it tracked the review or tracked Alice. Only an independent check against a known answer told the two apart. That is the framing's first sentence in a laboratory setting: the inference was plausible, and it was not the discovery it was taken for.

#### Six questions, six failures

The framing asks for evaluation "along several independent dimensions." The cases already told in this piece show each question failing while others are answered well.

Novelty asks whether anybody already knows the claim. Chapters II-01 and III-09 told the case of Bernard Madoff, whose reported returns and claimed options trading could not both be real given the size of the options market. An analyst who reached that conclusion in 2005 from public figures would have made a sound inference and would have failed on novelty. The inspector general of the Securities and Exchange Commission testified that the agency received six substantive complaints between June 1992 and December 2008.

One had been submitted in versions dated May 2000, March 2001, and October 2005. Another, received in May 2003, questioned the claimed options volume. A third was a set of internal emails at another firm, found by examiners in April 2004, whose authors had worked from widely available information. The inference was already held by several parties and already sat in the regulator's files. [^19]

Provenance asks where the underlying information came from. The DeepMind experiments are a failure of provenance at the smallest scale. A probe trained on the Alice prompts gives an answer for each review, and the answer comes from somewhere in the activations, but the method cannot report whether its source is the model's reading of the review or the model's record of what a character in the prompt believes. A result whose source cannot be named cannot be traced back to evidence. [^22]

Validity asks whether the inference is logically and empirically sound, and Google Flu Trends shows that the answer carries a date. Chapter III-12 told the case. The model published online in November 2008 was sound on the data it was tested against: on points held back from fitting, its estimates had a mean correlation of 0.97 with the figures of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The critique published in 2014 reported that the model overestimated flu prevalence in 100 out of 108 weeks from 21 August 2011 to 1 September 2013. [^61]

Six years separate the original model's publication from the 2014 critique; the errors emerged within that interval, so the figure is not a precise failure-onset lag. The claim was new, its sources were known, it was useful, and it had passed a test on held-out data. It then failed on validity as the behavior that produced its data changed. [^50]

Falsifiability asks whether a test can be designed that disproves the claim. The flu model's estimates could be checked against the CDC's later reports, and that check is how the failure was found. The model itself could not be tested from outside, because the 45 search queries it was built on had never been published, and the 2014 critique reported that the original analysis could not be replicated for that reason. The contrast-consistent search claim stands on the other side of this question. It was stated precisely enough that a test could be built against it, and the planted-feature experiments were that test. [^50] [^22]

Utility asks whether the discovery matters. The flu model's stated advantage was speed: the CDC published with a delay of one to two weeks, and the model could report with a lag of about one day. [^50]

The 2014 critique compared it with a projection built only from CDC data already two or three weeks old. Over the out-of-sample period that projection had a mean absolute error of 0.311 against 0.486 for Google Flu Trends. Used alone, the search signal told public health officials less than figures they already had. The critique found the value elsewhere, in a model combining the two sources, which reached 0.232 and outperformed either one. [^61]

Temporal integrity asks whether the claim could have been reached using only information available at the time. After Madoff's confession in December 2008, his steady returns read as proof. They were not proof in 2000. The emails of 2004 listed "his unusually consistent, non-volatile returns over several years" among their warning signs, and a warning sign is what the return series was.

Public exposure in 2008 confirmed the fraud. The earlier inference depended on comparing the returns with the capacity of the options market, and the clearing records supplied the independent check described earlier. On the oversight timeline, the warning dates to 2000 and the exposure to 2008. Some observers had already drawn the inference during those eight years. An account that says the returns alone revealed the fraud in 2000 imports the knowledge of 2008 into the earlier year. [^19]

#### Why the questions are separate

The framing calls the dimensions independent, and the cases show what that word requires. Each question is settled by a different act upon a different record. Novelty is settled by searching a reference record, provenance by tracing each premise to its source, validity by checking the reasoning against data, falsifiability by naming the observation that would defeat the claim, utility by comparison with what is already available, and temporal integrity by dating every piece of evidence.

A good answer to one question supplies no answer to another. The flu model had a correlation of 0.97 and unpublished inputs. The Madoff inference was valid and was not new. The Alice probe was consistent and had no traceable source. [^50] [^19] [^22]

The founding text applies this with special force to claims that come out of a model. Its section on the kinds of latency ends with two sentences on the representational kind.

> A model's ability to articulate a claim does not establish that the claim was encoded as truthful knowledge, nor that the claim is supported by its training data. Representational latency requires particular care because the original evidentiary trail may be inaccessible. [LIA §3]

The first sentence is the DeepMind result in general form. A model that can state a claim, or a probe that can extract a consistent answer, has shown that something in the model supports the statement. It has not shown that the something is truthful knowledge, and the Alice experiments show one concrete alternative, the represented opinion of someone else. [^22]

The second sentence concerns provenance. When an archaeological candidate comes from documents, each premise can be followed to a page, a date, and an author, and the remaining five questions can be put to it. When a candidate comes from a model's internal representation, that trail runs into the model's training and stops, because the documents that produced the representation are not attached to it. The founding text's remedy is in its definition of the representational class: such structure requires elicitation and independent verification. The elicited claim is treated as an archaeological candidate, and the six questions are answered from records outside the model. [LIA §3]

The fundamental problem is now stated in the framing's words, with a failure on each of the six dimensions drawn from cases this piece has already told. The next chapter prints the founding text's ten principles and shows each one deciding a case.

### IV-02. Ten principles

Submissions for the 2023 grand prize of the Vesuvius Challenge closed at midnight on January 1, 2024. The bar had been fixed at the launch: four passages of 140 characters each, with at least 85 percent of the characters recoverable.

The organizers then spent January checking the entries before any money moved. In their words, a team of papyrologists worked to "review 15 columns of text in anonymized submissions," so that each reader judged the Greek without knowing whose entry it was. The papyrologists worked independently of one another, and each of them recovered more text from one entry than from any other. A separate technical team audited the submitted code and reproduced its results. Only after both checks was the prize of $700,000 awarded, to Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor, and Julian Schilliger. [^4]

A claim to have read a sealed scroll was made, then tested by people with no stake in it, and then accepted. About one year separates the release of the challenge data in 2023 from the prize announcement in 2024, and the age of the ancient text is not a discovery lag. [^4]

The founding text turns that order into a rule, and into nine more. Its sixth section begins:

> The following principles define the proposed discipline. [LIA §6]

The ten follow as §6 prints them, each beside one decision from a case already told in this piece.

#### Evidence, provenance, novelty, questions, contradictions

1. **Evidence precedes assertion.** A plausible narrative is not a substitute for a traceable evidentiary chain.

The grand prize review in Chapter I-01 is this principle as a procedure. An image of letters produced by a model trained to detect ink is an assertion until someone else can follow it back to the scan.

The organizers' three checks on the scroll reading bear directly on this principle. The winning images held up on a rerun, and entrants working from different labels and models recovered much the same shapes from the same patch. Each model marked ink in small windows. The letters appeared when those marks were assembled, without the aid of a Greek alphabet or a language model. [^4]

Each link can be inspected by a person who doubts the reading. The scan is the source artifact, the tracing and flattening of the surface is a stated transformation, the ink detection is a second one, and the papyrologists' reading is the last inferential step. The organizers labeled the released readings as initial, rough drafts, which marks where the chain currently ends. The evidence reaches only as far as the passages read in that scroll. [^4]

2. **Provenance is part of the discovery.** A finding should preserve its source artifacts, dates, transformations, and inferential steps.

The archive of Zenon, told in Chapters I-05 and II-01, shows what is lost when provenance is not kept and what it costs to restore. Local diggers found the papers at the site of ancient Philadelpheia. According to the Trismegistos archive record, "The first text surfaced in 1911 in London and Strasbourg," and from about 1914 the major part was sold. The catalogue lists 21 holding collections. [^18]

The source artifacts survived and their relations did not. The 1980 *Guide to the Zenon Archive*, by a team under Pestman, rebuilt those relations with a survey by type and by date, a register of the persons named, and indexes. Many of the dates are Zenon's own: he endorsed incoming letters with the place and date of receipt and the subject, and scholars use those notes to reconstruct where he was. [^18]

The catalogue's open attributions matter here because a dated endorsement and a scholar's judgment support different parts of the reconstruction. The catalogue leaves the full network and any discovery interval undetermined. [^18]

3. **Novelty is investigated, not presumed.** Rediscovery is valuable, but must not be misrepresented as original discovery.

Chapter II-05 used the 1998 technical report by Page, Brin, and two Stanford colleagues to show novelty handled honestly from the inside. The report proposed ranking web pages by the structure of the links between them. Its authors did not present the underlying idea as theirs. Their first section says "there is already a large literature on academic citation analysis," and the report treats a link from one page to another as a kind of citation. [^31]

The investigation the principle asks for is visible in what the authors did next. Having named the earlier literature, they stated where the web departs from it. Academic papers are reviewed, and web pages are not. A plain count also ignores who is doing the citing, so their method weighted each link by the rank of the page it came from. [^31]

The claim that results is narrow and exact: a ranking method and its application to the web. The part that was a rediscovery, that citations carry information about importance, is credited to the literature that held it. The source documents the comparison and the algorithm. It does not establish a direct historical derivation from Leontief's input-output models, and no lag is stated, because the citation literature has no single defensible year of availability. [^31]

4. **The question may itself be the discovery.** Recognizing that a problem can be asked differently is a legitimate outcome.

Chapter III-11 told how Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver brought a housing model to transplantation in 2004. Their subject was the patient with a willing living donor whose kidney the patient cannot receive. The authors posed that patient's problem as a problem of exchange: many incompatible donor-patient pairs, indivisible goods, differing preferences, and no money, since buying or selling an organ is a felony under a 1984 federal law. [^47]

Asked that way, the problem already had a literature. Shapley and Scarf had published a model of a housing market in 1974, and the 2004 paper calls its proposal a "generalization of the TTC mechanism, for kidney exchange." The authors also name where the correspondence breaks. Nobody knows which cadaver kidneys will arrive or when, so they added an exchange of a donor's kidney for priority on the waiting list and extended the cycles into chains. [^47]

The outcome the principle recognizes here is the reformulation. Exchanges between two pairs had been proposed in 1986 and were being performed in small numbers before the paper appeared, so the paper did not introduce the practice. Thirty years separate the 1974 house-allocation model from the 2004 kidney-exchange formulation, which is not a delay in performing transplants. The paper contains theoretical analysis and simulations, and later clinical programs are separate empirical validation. [^47]

5. **Contradictions are excavation sites.** Inconsistencies can indicate missing variables, incompatible assumptions, errors, or genuine opportunities for explanation.

The Madoff case, introduced in Chapter II-01 and returned to in Chapter III-09, holds contradictions that were treated in opposite ways. The first lay between Madoff's reported strategy and the options market. Employees of another firm, doing due diligence on their own investment, listed among their warning signs "his unusually consistent, non-volatile returns over several years," and one of their emails reasoned step by step that the options trading he claimed could not be taking place, because exchange volume was insufficient and no private counterparty was conceivable. Those observers dug where the two records disagreed. [^19]

The others arose inside the regulator. The inspector general testified that examiners caught Madoff in contradictions and inconsistencies and then accepted his answers at face value. [^19]

The principle names four things an inconsistency can indicate, and error is one of them, so a contradiction does not settle what lies under it. It marks the place where an independent record has to be consulted. The clearing entity's records gave the inspector general the independent check on trading volumes described earlier. The gap from the warning in 2000 to public exposure in 2008 records eight years of missed oversight. [^19]

#### Similarity, time, negative results, methods, circulation

6. **Similarity is a lead, not proof.** Semantic proximity, analogy, and correlation may initiate inquiry but cannot independently establish causation or truth.

Chapter III-12 preserved a candidate that began as an analogy. Historical records survive unevenly, and survey statisticians have estimators for uneven samples: each respondent is weighted by the inverse of the probability of being included. The candidate proposed treating survival in an archive as inclusion in a sample, weighting each surviving item by the inverse of its probability of surviving, and so recovering the distribution of features among the items that were lost. [^52]

The stage-zero review tested whether the resemblance held where it mattered. The estimator is unbiased for totals when inclusion probabilities are known and positive, and it does not make nonlinear quantities such as entropy unbiased. Survival is not a probability sample, and items destroyed because of their features cannot be restored by reweighting the items that remain. The review also found the ground occupied: published work in the humanities already modeled the survival of the earliest printed books by format and leaf count. [^52]

The verdict was "KILL-NOVELTY + KILL-SPECIFICATION + DEFER-CORPUS." No historic inclusion probabilities and no empirical feature distribution were recovered. [^52] The analogy did what the principle allows a similarity to do. It started the inquiry, and the inquiry found that the property the transfer depended on, a known probability of inclusion, is absent from archives.

7. **Time is an evidentiary constraint.** Claims about what could have been discovered earlier must exclude information unavailable at the historical cutoff, including information introduced through model training or retrieval.

Chapter II-03 used Cohen and Frazzini's 2008 study for its rule about dates. The authors took the principal-customer disclosures that firms file, matched each named customer to a listed company for the years 1980 to 2004, and asked whether news about a customer was reflected promptly in the stock price of its supplier. They report that supplier returns predictably followed customer returns. [^26]

The claim depends on its cutoff. A customer link reported for a fiscal year becomes public when the statements for that year are issued, which is after the year ends. A test that used each link from the fiscal year-end would credit the past with information nobody outside the firm yet held. The authors imposed a six-month gap between fiscal year-end dates and the returns they examined, so that each relation was known before the returns it was used to explain. [^26]

The principle extends the rule to models. A model trained on later text, or allowed to retrieve it, carries information from after the cutoff. The cf-PICI test in Chapter II-05 was built around that problem: the laboratory's finding was unpublished when Co-Scientist was run, so the literature the system could search did not contain it. [^28] The finance study is retrospective and observational. It is not prospective validation or causal proof, and the ledger assigns it no lag. [^26]

8. **Negative results belong in the record.** Failed connections, rejected hypotheses, and uncertainty should be retained when possible so future investigators do not repeat the same excavation.

The other candidate preserved in Chapter III-12 proposed a ratio law joining information theory to editorial normalization. When a text or a piece of code is standardized, the candidate held, the ratio of the information tied to its producer to the remaining uncertainty should move in a set direction, and the direction should depend on whether a human editor's attention or a mechanical rule did the standardizing. [^51]

The stage-zero review checked the algebra, the prior art on code attribution, and whether human attention had been identified. The algebra gave no universal sign, since coarsening can lower both quantities and leave the ratio to rise or fall. The division into attention and rule was not identified, because a human editor follows rules and an autoformatter runs rules that people designed.

A 2024 study had already measured what formatting tools do to authorship attribution in Python files. The verdict was "KILL-NOVELTY + KILL-SPECIFICATION + DEFER-CORPUS." No corpus intervention and no mutual-information estimate was run. [^51]

The record that remains states what was proposed, which checks were made, what each found, and what was never attempted. An investigator who arrives at the same bridge can read that the sign is not determined, that the split between attention and rule needs a definition before it can be tested, and that a measurement of the mechanical case is in print. The candidate is kept as a rejected candidate, not as a premise for later work. [^51]

9. **Methods are replaceable; standards are not.** Human scholarship, statistical analysis, graph reasoning, language models, and future computational systems can all participate.

Two cases use methods that share almost nothing. Don R. Swanson, in the 1986 paper that Chapter I-01 opened on, worked by hand. He searched indexes, read what the search returned, revised the search, and reasoned about what he found. His claim was that "independently created pieces of knowledge can harbor an unseen, unknown, and unintended pattern." [^1]

Co-Scientist, reported in Nature in 2026 and examined in Chapter III-04, is a system of agents built on a large language model. A Supervisor agent assigns work to six specialized agents that generate hypotheses, review them, rank them in a tournament of paired debates, and revise the leaders. [^28]

The standards applied to the two outputs are the same. Swanson called the fish-oil proposal a hypothesis, said that it needed a direct test of its own, gave each premise with its citations, and stated that his search had been far from exhaustive. [^1] The Co-Scientist authors sent hypotheses chosen by experts to laboratory experiments and reported the level those experiments reached. Of the cell-line tests in acute myeloid leukemia they write that "they are not a replacement for the rigorous preclinical and clinical assessment that is typically required for therapeutic validation." [^28]

A candidate from an index search and a candidate from a tournament of agents each remain a candidate until tested.

10. **Knowledge should circulate.** Publish methods, evidence, and findings as openly as possible, consistent with privacy, source rights, safety, and the legitimate interests of affected people.

Several cases in the piece exist because someone circulated material. The Vesuvius Challenge released its high-resolution scans to anyone who wanted to work on them. [^4] Swanson sent a fuller account of his hypothesis to a biomedical journal, where readers in a position to criticize it would see it, and invited readers to tell him of any connection he had missed. [^1] The customer links in the finance study were public because an accounting standard requires a firm to name any customer that represents more than 10 percent of its sales. [^26] The cf-PICI case shows the reverse condition: while the laboratory's finding was unpublished, it was absent from the record everyone else could search. [^28]

The principle places four limits beside the instruction, in its own words: "privacy, source rights, safety, and the legitimate interests of affected people." [LIA §6] The cases show where they bear. The scanned scrolls belong to the Institut de France, a holder with rights in the source. [^4]

The Co-Scientist paper lists safety among the system's default criteria for a hypothesis. [^28] The complaints about Madoff concerned a named person and the accounts of his customers. [^19] The affected people in the 2004 paper are patients waiting for a kidney. [^47]

The ten principles of §6 are now printed in full, each with a case in which it decided something. Chapter IV-03 takes up the archaeological method of §7, the cycle of steps in which these principles are applied in sequence.

### IV-03. The archaeological method

In the paper they published in the Journal of Finance in August 2008, Lauren Cohen and Andrea Frazzini set out their procedure before they give their results. They first say what the record is. United States accounting rules, SFAS No. 131 and before 1997 SFAS No. 14, require a firm to report the identity of any customer representing more than 10 percent of its total sales, and the authors took those disclosures from the Compustat segment files for 1980 to 2004.

They then say what was done to it. Each named customer was matched to a traded company. Before 1998 most customers appeared as an abbreviation that varied across firms and over time, so a phonetic string-matching algorithm produced a list of possible matches and a person chose among them by inspecting the firm's name, segment, and industry. Customers with no unique match were excluded. To make sure each relation was known before the returns it was used to explain, a six-month gap was imposed between fiscal year-end dates and stock returns.

What remained was 30,622 distinct firm-year relationships, representing 11,484 unique supplier-customer relationships. Only then does the paper form portfolios of suppliers sorted by their customers' returns in the previous month and test the result against factor models. Its first sentence states what came of it: "This paper finds evidence of return predictability across economically linked firms." [^26]

Chapters II-03 and III-08 used this study for its rule about time and its result. This chapter uses it for its order of work.

#### The cycle

The founding text opens its section on method with one sentence and a diagram.

> LIA can be practiced as a recurring cycle rather than a one-time search. [LIA §7]

```text
Define the excavation boundary
             |
Acquire and preserve artifacts
             |
Map entities, claims, and relationships
             |
Detect gaps, anomalies, and possible connections
             |
Construct candidate discoveries
             |
Challenge evidence, novelty, and inference
             |
Validate or classify the remaining uncertainty
             |
Publish findings and rejected hypotheses
             |
             +----> Repeat with the enriched record
```

![Eight stages of the excavation method and its return loop](figures/F7.png)

The diagram has nine lines of text: eight steps and a return. Cohen and Frazzini were not following it; their paper is written to the standards of its own field. It does most of the nine things in the order shown and says in print how it did each one.

#### Nine steps on one study

*Define the excavation boundary.* The boundary is stated in three parts: a kind of informational artifact (disclosures of principal customers), a period (1980 to 2004), and a condition for inclusion (the firm is in the CRSP/Compustat database with book and market equity available, and the customer is itself a traded firm). The authors also state what the boundary leaves out. The rules require firms to report major customers and not major suppliers, so the sample holds links in one direction, and the main tests run from customer to supplier. [^26]

*Acquire and preserve artifacts.* The disclosures were taken from a named file. Customers that could not be matched were dropped under a stated rule. The paper does not print the matched table of links. [^26]

*Map entities, claims, and relationships.* This is the name matching. An abbreviation in one firm's filing had to become a specific listed company before any relationship could be drawn. The map that results has two kinds of node, supplier and customer, and one kind of edge, a disclosed sale of more than 10 percent. In the linked sample the average customer accounts for 20 percent of the supplier's sales, and on average 78 percent of the relations join firms in different industries. [^26]

*Detect gaps, anomalies, and possible connections.* The gap is between a public fact and a price. The paper's illustration, told in Chapter III-08, is Coastcast Corporation, whose customer Callaway Golf accounted for half of its sales and lost 30 percent of its market value in two days in June 2001. Coastcast's price did not move for about two months. [^26]

*Construct candidate discoveries.* One instance is an anecdote. The candidate is a general statement that can fail: stock prices underreact to news about linked firms, so a supplier's return this month can be predicted from its customers' returns last month. The authors write it as a named hypothesis and attach a rule that either earns abnormal returns in the data or does not: buy the fifth of suppliers whose customers did best and sell short the fifth whose customers did worst. [^26]

*Challenge evidence, novelty, and inference.* The larger part of the paper is spent here. The supplier's own past return could be what predicts, with the customer's return a noisy stand-in for it, so own-firm momentum is controlled.

The effect could be industry momentum, or a lead-lag relationship documented in earlier studies, so those are controlled, and the test is rerun on suppliers larger than their customers. Thin trading could produce an apparent delay, so low-priced stocks are excluded and the test is repeated on stocks that traded every day for the previous 12 months. The reported abnormal return is 1.45 percent a month against the three-factor model and 1.37 percent with a momentum factor added, and the paper gives 1.55 percent a month as its main result. Novelty is handled by naming each previously documented predictability effect and showing that the new one survives when the old ones are hedged out. [^26]

*Validate or classify the remaining uncertainty.* The study validates within its own design and says where it stops. When the authors examine whether mutual funds holding both firms trade sooner on customer news, they write that the regression is silent about causality and that, with holdings observed quarterly at best, they cannot distinguish between two hypotheses.

For value-weighted portfolios sorted on one-year customer returns, they report that the hypothesis of no predictability cannot be rejected. The evidence level is retrospective and observational, not a prospective test, not proof of cause, and not evidence of a net trading profit available today. Independent replication is not established in the record used here. [^26]

*Publish findings and rejected hypotheses.* The findings are published, with the procedure. Rejected hypotheses fare less well. Several checks are noted as run and not shown, with the results available from the authors on request, among them a test of predictability running from supplier to customer. This is the point at which the study departs from the cycle. [^26]

*Repeat with the enriched record.* The paper ends by recommending further research on how different kinds of information affect investor attention. It does not itself return to the corpus. [^26]

#### The first instruction

Two paragraphs of instruction follow the diagram. A third, on a persistent system, is the subject of Chapter IV-05.

> The investigator should define the corpus and temporal boundary, retain primary sources, normalize entities without erasing meaningful context, and explore both expected and unexpected relationships. [LIA §7]

The first three clauses are the first three steps, restated as duties. The study shows the first two.

The third clause carries a warning, and a different case shows what it warns about. Kononova's dataset, discussed in Chapter III-05, makes it possible to see what was lost when prose became data. Normalizing, in that project, meant turning a paragraph written for the readers of one paper into a fixed set of fields: target, starting compounds, operations, conditions, and a balanced chemical equation. The published dataset consists of "19,488 synthesis entries retrieved from 53,538 solid-state synthesis paragraphs." [^7]

The authors measured what the conversion cost. The overall yield was 28 percent: of the 53,538 paragraphs, 15,144 produced a balanced reaction. In a random draw of 100 paragraphs, 30 lacked a complete set of starting materials and products. Of the 70 that had them, 42 were not reconstructed. A check of 100 finished entries against their source paragraphs found the chemistry correct in 93 percent and every item of the recipe correct in 51 percent, with mixing conditions extracted worst. [^7]

The normalized form is what makes the recipes comparable, and it is narrower than the prose it came from. The instruction's answer is its second clause: retain primary sources, so that the context a normal form drops can be recovered from the artifact. These figures measure agreement between entries and text. They do not show that any recipe reproduces at the bench, and the case supports no lag. [^7]

The last clause, on expected and unexpected relationships, has a counterpart in the two searches of Arrowsmith, presented in Chapter III-02. In a two-node search the investigator names both literatures and examines a relationship already suspected. In a one-node search the investigator starts from one literature and looks for a second, unknown one that holds complementary knowledge. Cohen and Frazzini tested a link they expected to matter. [^24] [^26]

#### The second instruction

> Candidate discoveries should be subjected to competing explanations, counterexamples, source independence checks, and appropriate empirical or logical tests. Wherever possible, independent investigators should be able to reconstruct the reasoning. [LIA §7]

Competing explanations are what the customer-link study supplies in quantity: own-firm momentum, industry momentum, lead-lag effects, and illiquidity, each named and each tested. Its handling of industry also shows what a source independence check is for. The authors note that, because the customers in the sample are large, a customer's return is likely to be highly correlated with the return of its industry. Two signals that move together are not two pieces of evidence, so the test portfolios were re-examined after industry effects were controlled. [^26]

Counterexamples and reconstruction are where the field that Swanson started has the most experience. Thilakaratne and colleagues, in their 2019 review of literature-based discovery, report that the most common evaluation is replication: a method is given only the literature published before a known discovery and is checked for whether it recovers the same connection. The review's caution is a counterexample. Arrowsmith had been evaluated by replicating known discoveries, and it failed to recognize the links in a further Swanson case, between somatomedin-C and arginine. [^23]

The Arrowsmith field trial shows how easily the challenge step is dropped in practice. The developers recommended that a user first run a broad PubMed search for any direct literature on A and C together, to learn what was already known, and only then run the two-node search. To the authors' knowledge, none of the field testers ever followed that recommendation. [^24] In the cycle the check for prior knowledge is a step of its own, placed before validation.

The instruction's last sentence is met in the opening case. Cohen and Frazzini name their files, their matching rule, their exclusions, their six-month gap, and their controls, so another investigator with access to those files can attempt the same reasoning. [^26]

#### The step most often skipped

The eighth step asks for two things, and the second is rarely done. A case from Chapter III-12 shows a rejected hypothesis as a published record. In 2026 a cross-field candidate proposing a ratio law for what standardization does to the trace of a text's producer was closed at its first review with the verdict "KILL-NOVELTY + KILL-SPECIFICATION + DEFER-CORPUS."

The record states that no corpus was acquired, no intervention was performed, and no estimate was fitted. It lists eight findings, among them that the candidate's own falsifier had its sign backwards, that no universal sign follows from the algebra, and that the effect of mechanical formatting on attribution was direct prior art in a 2024 study. It then states what to delete and what narrower test would remain. This is a rejected candidate preserved in the record. It is not a finding, and it supports no lag. [^51]

A record of that kind makes the last line of the diagram a return. The rejection itself turned on an earlier publication, the 2024 study, that was already part of the record when the review searched it. [^51] Once the 2008 paper was in print, the customer-supplier link and its test were themselves informational artifacts, available to any later excavation in that corpus. [^26] The published record, with its findings and its rejections, becomes part of the next boundary to be defined.

The cycle of §7 has now been walked on one study, with its two instructions and the step most often left out. Chapter IV-04 takes up the form in which a single finding is written down so that others can audit it: the excavation record.

### IV-04. The excavation record

In 1986 Don R. Swanson published a hypothesis: dietary fish oil might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome. He had treated no one.

He set out his reasoning three years later in the *Bulletin of the Medical Library Association*. It sets reports of vascular changes from fish oil alongside evidence that those changes could help people with Raynaud's. His check was simple to state: "these two literatures had no articles in common." He also found no joint citations or references linking the literatures or proposing the treatment. [^2]

The 1989 trial supplies the test. DiGiacomo, Kremer, and Shah randomly assigned 32 patients to fish oil or an olive-oil placebo and reported benefit for primary Raynaud's alone. [^3] The published record puts three years between the hypothesis and its first reported clinical test. [^2]

Chapter III-01 told this case at length. Those two paragraphs hold nearly everything a reader needs in order to check it: the claim, the material searched, the date, the reasoning, the search for earlier statements, the test. They hold it in the order of a story. The founding text proposes a fixed order.

> A finding should be packaged in a form that other investigators can audit, challenge, reproduce, and build upon. The following is a proposed minimal record format, not a finalized technical standard. [LIA §8]

```yaml
lia_version: "0.1"
id: "<stable identifier>"
claim: "<precise candidate finding>"
corpus: "<defined source collection>"
cutoff: "<historical cutoff, if applicable>"
latency_class: "<one or more classes>"
sources:
  - reference: "<persistent source identifier>"
    evidence: "<relevant observation or claim>"
    available_at: "<date or documented uncertainty>"
inference: "<reasoning connecting the evidence>"
alternatives: []
contradictory_evidence: []
novelty_check: "<scope, methods, and results>"
status: "candidate"
validation: "<tests and their results>"
limitations: []
next_test: "<what could refute or strengthen the claim>"
```

The block has 15 top-level fields, and each entry under `sources` carries three of its own.

#### The record as of 1986

Filled in for Swanson's hypothesis on the day it was published, and using only what his later report and the 1989 trial abstract state, the record reads as follows. The identifier is made up for this chapter.

```yaml
lia_version: "0.1"
id: "fish-oil-raynaud-1986"
claim: "Dietary fish oil might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome."
corpus: "Two sets of published medical articles: one on dietary fish oil, one on Raynaud's syndrome."
cutoff: "1986"
latency_class: "Relational, Inferential"
sources:
  - reference: "Fish-oil articles, as summarized in Swanson (1989), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC225324/"
    evidence: "Dietary fish oils lead to certain blood and vascular changes."
    available_at: "Before the 1986 publication; year not dated in this record."
  - reference: "Raynaud articles, as summarized in the same report"
    evidence: "Similar changes might benefit patients with Raynaud's syndrome."
    available_at: "Before the 1986 publication; year not dated in this record."
inference: "If fish oil produces these changes, and these changes might benefit Raynaud patients, then fish oil might benefit Raynaud patients."
alternatives: []
contradictory_evidence: []
novelty_check: "Scope: the two sets of articles. Method: look for shared articles, joint citation, and mention of either topic in the other set. Result: no articles in common; never cited together; neither set mentioned the other or suggested the treatment."
status: "candidate"
validation: "None. No clinical trial reported."
limitations:
  - "Both evidence lines come from a 1989 summary by the author of the hypothesis, not from the individual articles."
  - "Publication years of the antecedent articles are not established."
next_test: "A controlled clinical trial of dietary fish oil in patients with Raynaud's syndrome."
```

Every line above comes from Swanson's report or the ledger entry for the case, including the two things the record admits it lacks. [^2] The lists for alternatives and contradictory evidence are empty because the sources used for this chapter record none as of 1986. A reader who knows of a competing explanation can see exactly where it is missing.

#### The record as of 1989

Three years later the trial exists, and nine fields change. The entries shown under `sources` and `limitations` are added to those of 1986. The other fields stand as they were.

```yaml
claim: "Dietary fish oil improves tolerance to cold and delays the onset of vasospasm in patients with primary Raynaud's syndrome."
cutoff: "1989"
sources:
  - reference: "DiGiacomo, Kremer, and Shah (1989), https://doi.org/10.1016/0002-9343(89)90261-1 (abstract)"
    evidence: "32 patients randomized to fish oil or olive-oil placebo; on fish oil, median time to onset rose from 31.3 to 46.5 minutes at six weeks (p = 0.04)."
    available_at: "1989"
alternatives:
  - "The trial abstract gives its own rationale, a favorable vascular response to ischemia. The benefit does not confirm each link of the 1986 inference."
contradictory_evidence:
  - "No benefit concluded for patients with secondary Raynaud's syndrome."
status: "corroborated"
validation: "One double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Among primary patients, an episode could no longer be induced in 5 of 11 on fish oil, against 1 of 9 on olive oil (p = 0.05)."
limitations:
  - "One trial, one regimen, 32 patients."
  - "The trial is held as an abstract only."
next_test: "A second, independent trial in the primary form. None is recorded in the sources used here."
```

The claim is smaller than it was. In 1986 it covered patients with Raynaud's syndrome in general, and in 1989 it covers the primary form, because that is what the trial's authors concluded. [^3] The secondary form moves into `contradictory_evidence`. The status is set to `corroborated` and not `validated` because the support is one trial of 32 people, and the trial is a limited clinical test, not a universal recommendation for primary and secondary Raynaud's. [^2] The founding text lists its statuses without setting thresholds for them, so the `validation` field carries the reason for the choice, where a reader can dispute it.

#### What the fields are for

Each entry under `sources` carries `available_at` because the record makes a claim about time. A candidate with a cutoff of 1986 has to rest on artifacts that existed by 1986, and the field is where that is shown or where the gap is admitted. The template allows "date or documented uncertainty," and the 1986 record uses the second option: the ledger for this case does not date the antecedent literature, so the record says so. [^2]

The `alternatives` and `contradictory_evidence` fields are lists, and the template prints them as empty brackets, not as optional lines. A record without them would read the same whether the investigator had looked for competing explanations and found none or had never looked. In the 1989 record both have entries. The trialists' abstract gives its own reason for expecting a benefit, so a positive result does not pick out Swanson's chain of reasoning over theirs. The secondary subgroup did not benefit. [^3]

The `novelty_check` field asks for "scope, methods, and results," and all three are needed. Swanson's check has a scope, two sets of articles; a method, looking for shared articles, joint citation, and mention; and a result, none. [^2] The file `../../RESCOPE.md` states the same requirement for a discovery dossier: it must give the closest prior art, the exact difference claimed, and the logged search coverage, and it must never turn an unsuccessful search into proof that nobody has made the connection.

The `next_test` field is required because a candidate that names no test cannot be moved. The 1986 record names a clinical trial, and the 1989 record exists because others ran one. [^2]

#### Six statuses

> Suggested statuses are `candidate`, `corroborated`, `validated`, `rediscovered`, `rejected`, and `unresolved`. Novelty and validity are separate axes: a rediscovery can be true, and a novel candidate can be false. A mature standard should record those dimensions independently. [LIA §8]

The fish-oil record supplies two of the six. It was a `candidate` in 1986 and is `corroborated` in the 1989 record above. [^2] [^3]

Chapter III-12 told a case that ends in `rejected`. A candidate proposed carrying sampling estimators over to the uneven survival of archives, in order to recover the feature distributions of what was lost. Its first review tested the estimator claim, the assumptions about missing items, and the prior art in the humanities, and closed it with the verdict "KILL-NOVELTY + KILL-SPECIFICATION + DEFER-CORPUS." No historic inclusion probabilities and no empirical feature distribution were recovered. [^52] A record for it has the same fields as the fish-oil record, with a different `status`, and it is kept for the same reason.

Thalidomide in erythema nodosum leprosum, or ENL, shows what `rediscovered` protects against. As Chapter II-05 records, the FDA approved the drug for ENL in 1998, thirty-three years after the response was reported in 1965. For novelty, the relevant date is 1965, when the connection entered the record. Anyone who recovered it from the 1965 report today would file it as `rediscovered`. The approval date belongs in the regulatory history, which relies in part on a secondary account. [^30]

The Antikythera mechanism shows why scope sits inside the novelty field. The mechanism was recovered in 1901.

In 2006 Freeth and colleagues published a reconstruction of its gear functions from new imaging, and additional deciphered text. Earlier scholars had already recognized an astronomical mechanism. The 105 years run from recovery to the 2006 CT-based reconstruction, not to first recognition of the mechanism. [^17] A record for the 2006 work would state its claim as the specific reconstruction and would list the earlier recognition under `novelty_check` as what was already known. The claim that survives that check is narrower than a headline about decoding the mechanism, and it is the accurate one.

The cases bound to this chapter supply no record marked `validated` and none marked `unresolved`.

The second sentence of the passage is why one `status` word is not enough. The survival-weighting candidate failed on novelty and on specification at once. [^52] The fish-oil claim was new relative to two literatures in 1986 and untested, and by 1989 it was supported for one form of the condition. [^2] [^3]

Each of these is a pair of answers, one about novelty and one about validity. The proposed format holds the first in `novelty_check` and the second in `validation`, and the founding text says that a mature standard should record the two independently. It calls the format proposed and minimal, and the six statuses suggested.

### IV-05. A discovery-driven system

A run of Co-Scientist, the multi-agent system that Chapter III-04 presented, starts when a scientist writes down a research goal. The 2026 Nature paper that reports the system puts it in one sentence of its first figure: "Given a research goal in natural language, Co-Scientist generates novel research hypotheses." The goal is the entry point. It can be a simple statement, or a document of tens of thousands of tokens accompanied by hundreds of earlier publications, and it can carry the constraints of one scientist's laboratory. The system parses the goal into a research plan configuration, which fixes what kind of proposals are wanted and the criteria by which they will be judged.

A Supervisor agent then builds a queue of tasks from that configuration and assigns them to the specialized agents. While the run proceeds, the Supervisor periodically computes statistics on progress toward the specified goal and uses them to decide where further computation goes and whether the computation has reached its terminal state. When it has, the scientist receives a ranked list of hypotheses and a research overview, and reviews the top of the list. For the leukemia study, the paper gives the human time at each end: writing the initial prompt took less than one hour of an expert clinician's time, and the final review and selection of candidates for testing took roughly three hours. Everything the system did in between followed from the goal that the clinician wrote. [^28]

The framing asks for a system that starts somewhere else.

> **3. What an LIA system might look like**
> Rather than asking an AI a question and receiving an answer, you build a persistent system that actively searches for questions worth asking.
>
> The important architectural distinction: the system is discovery-driven rather than query-driven. [Framing §3]

The passage makes two demands. The system is persistent, so it does not end when one answer has been delivered. And the thing it searches for is a question, where a query-driven system is handed the question and searches for the answer. The cycle in §7 of the founding text supplies the parts of such a system, and §7 attaches one requirement to it.

#### What exists

Co-Scientist is query-driven by design, and its authors say so. The 2025 preprint states that the work does not aim to automate the scientific process completely and that the system is purpose-built for a scientist who stays in the loop. [^37] The 2026 paper lists the points at which the scientist acts: defining the goal, refining the goal in light of the hypotheses returned, supplying reviews, contributing hypotheses of their own to the tournament, and making the final selection for experiment. [^28] The goal reaches into every agent's work: the Generation agent produces hypotheses that address it, and the Ranking agent's ordered list is a list of proposals aligned with it. [^28]

Inside a run, the system has some of the persistence the framing asks for. The paper reports that it operates continuously and asynchronously, that its state is written periodically to a context memory and used as feedback in later rounds, and that this memory allows a restart after a component fails. [^28] That persistence serves one goal and stops at the terminal state for that goal. The query is large and the run is long, and the arrangement is still a question followed by an answer.

A much older tool took a step toward the other arrangement. Chapter III-02 introduced Arrowsmith, the search tool that Don Swanson and Neil Smalheiser built on Swanson's procedure, and its two kinds of search. In the two-node search the user names both literatures and the tool lists the title words they share.

In the one-node search the user names one literature, for example the articles on a disease, and the search looks for a second literature, unknown to the user, that holds complementary knowledge. The 2006 paper on the tool says of it: "In a sense, the one node search is a way of generating new hypotheses." [^24] The 2019 review of literature-based discovery gives the general form under the name open discovery, in which "only the concept A is known in advance and concepts B and C are identified by the LBD process." [^23]

The one-node search proposes something the user did not ask for by name, which is the first half of what the framing wants. The 2006 paper reports field testers arriving at problems that called for it. One wanted a list of genes known to be expressed in the amygdala that had not been studied in the context of fear conditioning and could plausibly play a role in it. [^24] That request is a search for a gap, and a person still chose the amygdala and fear conditioning. The same paper reports which search the investigators preferred: the two-node search proved more generally applicable and "better aligned to the information practices of most biomedical investigators," and the interface built for the field testers focused on two-node searches exclusively. [^24]

The third piece concerns direction. Chapter I-04 and Chapter III-03 reported the 2023 study by Jamshid Sourati and James Evans, held in the research record as an abstract only. Their models take in the distribution of human expertise, and the abstract says that such a model can be tuned to avoid the crowd and generate "scientifically promising ‘alien’ hypotheses unlikely to be imagined or pursued without intervention until the distant future." [^16] A system that has to choose its own questions needs a rule for where to look, and this is one: look where the experts are not positioned to look. The abstract supports no statement here about how well those hypotheses held up.

Each of the three supplies one piece: a literature the user did not name, hypotheses generated and criticized without a person in each step, and a direction away from the places already being searched. In each, as its source reports it, a person supplies the starting point, and the work ends when that person's request has been met. [^24] [^28] [^16]

#### The parts, taken from the cycle

The founding text gives the method as a cycle, which Chapter IV-03 reproduced and walked through step by step. A discovery-driven system is that cycle kept running, and its parts can be read off the steps.

The first part is a corpus with dates. The cycle begins with "Define the excavation boundary" and "Acquire and preserve artifacts," and the paragraph under the diagram tells the investigator to define the corpus and temporal boundary. [LIA §7]

A system that chooses its own questions cannot take the boundary from the question, so the boundary has to be a standing property of the system. The dates matter because of how such a system can be checked. The literature-based discovery review reports that time-sliced evaluation requires a cut-off date that divides the data into a segment before it and a segment after it, with the method run on the first and judged against the second. [^23] Artifacts without dates cannot be divided that way.

The second part is a map, the step the cycle calls "Map entities, claims, and relationships." [LIA §7] Arrowsmith's list of shared title terms is a small map of where two literatures touch. [^24] Co-Scientist's Proximity agent builds a graph of how close its hypotheses are to one another. [^28] The first maps the corpus and the second maps the system's own output. A discovery-driven system needs the first kind, because the map is where it looks for something to ask.

The third part is a generator, covering "Detect gaps, anomalies, and possible connections" and "Construct candidate discoveries." [LIA §7] In a discovery-driven system the detection step replaces the user's question. The amygdala request above shows what its output looks like: a named region of the map where two things that plausibly belong together have not been studied together. [^24]

The fourth part is a challenger, for "Challenge evidence, novelty, and inference." [LIA §7] Co-Scientist's Reflection agent does part of this work. It searches for previous work, weighs the existing experimental evidence for and against a hypothesis, and filters out inaccurate hypotheses and, when the goal stipulates novelty, non-novel ones. [^28]

The fifth part is an evaluator that is separate from the generator, for "Validate or classify the remaining uncertainty." [LIA §7] The separation matters because of a fact the Co-Scientist paper states about its own scoring. The Elo rating that ranks its hypotheses is auto-evaluated and is not based on independent ground truth. [^28] The independent evaluation it reports came from outside the system: experts who selected candidates, and laboratory experiments on those candidates. [^28]

The sixth part is the record, for "Publish findings and rejected hypotheses," followed by the arrow that makes the cycle a cycle: "Repeat with the enriched record." [LIA §7] The record is what makes persistence useful. The eighth principle gives the reason for keeping rejected hypotheses in it: "so future investigators do not repeat the same excavation." [LIA §6] A system that runs indefinitely is its own future investigator.

One implementation assigns these roles by name. The project file `../../RESCOPE.md` sets out a research method in which source scouts map a diverse corpus, domain readers extract relations without seeing a desired target conclusion, bridge agents propose mappings, prior-art challengers search for earlier formulations and synonyms in both domains, a critic tests disanalogies and easier explanations, a separate evaluator scores evidence and testability, and the owner integrates accepted results and retains the failures. The companion file `../../discovery/README.md` documents an offline instrument that holds the record.

In its data contract every source carries a capture timestamp and a content digest. Every candidate must state its strongest alternative explanation, a disanalogy, a falsifiable test, a log of prior-art queries, the agent and model that generated it, and a review. A recorded disposition of accept, reject, or abstain requires a reviewer whose identifier differs from the generating agent's. The same file states the limit of that check: it catches obvious self-review and cannot verify genuine independence or quality.

Two facts about this implementation bound what it shows. `RESCOPE.md` states that a user may start with a question, a source, or a broad domain pair, so a person still supplies a starting point. And `discovery/README.md` states that the instrument itself makes no network requests and no model calls, and that the reference predictions shipped with it come from synthetic development labels, so their perfect score verifies the instrument and is no evidence of model performance, real discovery, or novelty. The files show the roles written down and a record format that enforces some of them. They do not show a discovery.

#### Rejecting more than it accepts

The founding text adds one requirement to a system built this way.

> A persistent LIA system could autonomously propose new lines of inquiry, but it must be designed to reject more than it accepts when the evidence warrants it. The objective is not maximal hypothesis production. It is the efficient identification of consequential, defensible findings. [LIA §7]

The requirement follows from the change in architecture. A query-driven system is limited by the number of questions people bring to it. A discovery-driven system has no such limit, and generation is the cheap part. Arrowsmith showed the proportions on a single pair of literatures: a search on retinal detachment against aortic aneurysm returned a raw list of 741 shared title terms, and the user was led to one syndrome that involves both conditions. [^24] Reporting all 741 as findings would have produced more and identified nothing.

The existing systems already reject most of what they generate when they are used well. In the leukemia work that Chapter III-04 reported, 30 top-ranked candidates went to oncologists, who chose five for testing, and three of the five inhibited the viability of leukemia cells in culture. In a second run experts chose three candidates, and one of the three did. [^28] Those are in vitro results on cell lines, and the rejections along the way were made by experts and by experiments. The founding text asks that a persistent system carry that behavior in its design.

The clause "when the evidence warrants it" keeps the requirement from becoming a quota. The project instrument makes the point with three deliberately weak controls, one that accepts every candidate, one that rejects every candidate, and one that abstains on every candidate. `discovery/README.md` states that all three must fail the configured gates.

On its twelve synthetic fixtures, which fall into three categories whose correct decisions are accept, reject, and abstain, each control has a macro accuracy of 1/3. The accept-all control has an acceptance precision of 1/3 and an unsupported acceptance rate of 1. The file states that abstaining or rejecting everything cannot pass. Two of the three categories call for a refusal to accept, and a control that only refuses still fails.

The last two sentences of the passage name what is being optimized. A count of hypotheses is the wrong measure, and the literature-based discovery review explains one reason evaluations drift toward it. When a method is tested by replicating a known discovery, only the associations in that discovery are checked, and "the remaining associations in the LBD output are not assessed." [^23] The founding text's objective puts the cost on the other side of the ledger. The word "efficient" refers to what each finding costs, including the hours of the people who review it, and the Co-Scientist paper's figures of less than one hour to set the goal and roughly three hours to review the output are the kind of number the word calls for. [^28]

The framing ends its own argument on the system this chapter has laid out.

> The most interesting version of LIA wouldn't simply be a better research assistant. It would be an autonomous discovery engine that continually searches the space of things humanity could already know, but doesn't. [Framing §6]

The parts of a discovery-driven system exist separately in the lineage, a person still supplies the starting point in each of them, and the founding text's condition on joining them is that the result reject more than it accepts when the evidence warrants it. The next chapter takes up the research agenda of §10, which says how such a system, and the discipline, would be judged.

<a id="chapter-iv-06"></a>

### IV-06. A research agenda

In 2019 Vahe Tshitoyan and colleagues ran one test eighteen times. They had about 3.3 million abstracts of materials science papers, published between 1922 and 2018, and a method that ranked materials by how closely the text associated each one with the word "thermoelectric." To learn whether the ranking would have been of use in the past, they built eighteen historical collections, one for each cutoff year from 2001 to 2018.

Each held only the abstracts published before its cutoff. They trained separate word embeddings on each collection from the start, and from each they took the 50 highest-ranked materials that had not yet appeared in an abstract beside a thermoelectric keyword. Then, for every year after each cutoff, they counted the share of those 50 that had since been reported in the literature beside such a keyword. [^15]

Averaged over the runs, a material from a top-50 list was eight times more likely to be studied as a thermoelectric within the next five years than an unstudied material drawn at random from the collection as it stood at that date, and three times more likely than a random material with a nonzero computed bandgap. [^15] Chapter II-03 went through the experiment step by step and Chapter III-03 gave the paper in full. It returns here because of its design. The corpora were frozen at stated dates, the method was fixed before the outcomes were read, and the outcomes were checked against what was published afterward. Among the cases in this piece it is the nearest thing to a finished example of the first item on a list that the founding text sets for itself.

That list is section 10 of the founding text, and it opens with the standard by which the rest is to be read.

> The discipline should be judged by what it can demonstrate, not by the ambition of its premise. [LIA §10]

Seven priorities follow, which the founding text calls early ones, and then a set of metrics. This chapter takes them in order and reports, for each, what the cases in this piece have done and what they have left undone.

#### Replay and its three controls

> **Historical replay.** Freeze a corpus at an earlier date and measure whether a method recovers discoveries documented afterward. Control for training-data leakage, hindsight in selecting tasks, and selection bias. [LIA §10]

The first sentence is the eighteen-cutoff experiment. The second names three ways in which a replay can report more than the method earned.

Training-data leakage is information from after the cutoff reaching the method. The Berkeley group excluded it by construction, since each set of embeddings was trained only on abstracts dated before its cutoff. [^15]

A large language model cannot be handled that way. A 2024 paper in the Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics states that the training data of such models, given their vast size and wide-ranging sources, could explicitly or implicitly include test data, and that the opacity of the data and black-box access to the models make contamination hard to detect and mitigate. It proposes a detection method that works from a model's sampled outputs. [^27] A historical replay with a model whose training text cannot be inspected has not been shown in the sources of this piece.

Hindsight in selecting tasks is choosing a test because its answer is known to be recoverable. Thilakaratne and colleagues, in their 2019 review of literature-based discovery, report that the commonest evaluation in that field is to replicate a famous discovery from the literature that preceded it. The intermediate and target terms are then known in advance, and a system's parameters can be tuned to produce them, so that it performs well on that discovery and not on others. [^23] The materials experiment was less exposed. It scored fifty candidates at each of eighteen cutoffs and did not aim at one celebrated result, and the authors repeated the historical test with three other target words, "photovoltaics," "topological insulator," and "ferroelectric." [^15]

Selection bias enters through the answer key. In a time-sliced evaluation the gold standard is the set of associations present after the cut-off date and absent before it. [^23] It contains only what someone went on to publish. Of the five top-ranked predictions from abstracts published before 2009, two, HgZnTe and SmInO3, contain expensive or toxic elements and had not been studied. [^15]

The key scores both as misses, and it would do so whether or not either compound performs. The historical results and property calculations support a retrospective ranking. Experimental performance remains untested for some suggested compounds. The published work gives no basis for measuring a discovery lag. [^21]

#### Candidates published before the outcome

> **Prospective excavation.** Publish timestamped candidate discoveries before their outcomes are known, including failed predictions and null results. [LIA §10]

A prospective candidate is issued before the later record exists. Chapter II-04 told the one case in this piece with that form. In February 2020 a group at BenevolentAI published a letter in the Lancet naming baricitinib, an approved drug found through a knowledge graph built in part from the literature. "We identified baricitinib, which is predicted to reduce the ability of the virus to infect lung cells." [^25]

In December 2020 the ACTT-2 trial, run by other investigators, was published. "Patients receiving baricitinib had a median time to recovery of 7 days," against 8 days in the control group, where all patients received remdesivir. [^55]

The candidate carried a date and its outcome was not known when it appeared. The two publications appeared about ten months apart in 2020. The trial tested one regimen in one population. It did not test every mechanism the letter proposed. [^25]

The second half of the priority is the part one case cannot meet. A single candidate that fared well in a trial says nothing about the rate at which candidates from the same method fare well. That rate needs every candidate on the record with its date, and the failures and null results kept beside the successes.

#### Comparison and cost

> **Comparative performance.** Compare LIA methods with conventional search, literature-based discovery systems, domain experts, and simple statistical baselines under comparable resource constraints. [LIA §10]

The materials paper compared its ranking with two baselines: a material chosen at random from those not yet studied, and a random material with a nonzero bandgap. [^15] The literature-based discovery review explains why a shared, dated task matters for this priority. When two systems each claim to have replicated a famous discovery, nothing says which is better, and expert judgments are hard to quantify. Time-slicing gives numbers by which one system can be set against another. [^23]

Mario Krenn and colleagues made such a comparison in 2023. Their benchmark, Science4Cast, asks which pairs of concepts in the literature of artificial intelligence will come to be studied together, and ten methods were run on the same task. The authors report, "the most powerful methods use a carefully curated set of network features, rather than an end-to-end AI approach." Pure network features with no machine learning were competitive. [^36]

A simple baseline can come near a costly method, and only a test that includes both shows it. Jamshid Sourati and James Evans, in a 2023 paper held here at the level of its abstract, report a comparison between two kinds of model: one trained on research content alone, and one that also takes account of the distribution of human expertise. The second predicted future discoveries better, especially where the relevant literature was sparse. [^16]

Each of these sets one computational method against another or against a statistical baseline. A matched comparison with domain experts, under comparable limits of time and resources, is not supplied by the sources of this piece.

> **Excavation economics.** Measure cost per defensible finding, reviewer time, reproducibility, and the marginal benefit of additional computation or data. [LIA §10]

Chapter I-04 used Chihmao Hsieh's 2011 study of patented inventions for the cost of a connection. His finding was that the cost has two parts that move in opposite directions: "difficulty shifts from the cost of connecting unrelated features to the cost of synthesizing overly related ones." [^13] That is a mechanism. The ledger states that it is not a universal estimate of years saved by LIA, and it sets no price on any finding. [^13]

The materials paper offers one observation on marginal benefit: larger corpora that included more recent years raised the rate of successful predictions. [^15] It reports no cost for checking a candidate in a laboratory, and the review rates expert assessment of a system's output as expensive, time-consuming, and subjective. [^23] Cost per defensible finding has not been measured in any case told here.

#### Transfer, negative knowledge, and infrastructure

> **Cross-domain transfer.** Test whether useful principles, technologies, and explanations can be recovered across distinct vocabularies and institutional boundaries. [LIA §10]

Two findings from earlier chapters say where such tests should look. Qing Ke and colleagues, studying citation histories in 2015, found that papers recognized late often reached their importance in disciplines different from the ones in which they were published. [^5] Hsieh found that connecting unrelated features is the costly kind of search. [^13]

The computational tests in this chapter, however, each stayed inside one literature: materials abstracts in one, papers on artificial intelligence in the other. [^15] [^36] The literature-based discovery reviewers note that most earlier reviews of their field confined themselves to medical studies. [^23] A replay whose corpus spans two vocabularies, with the outcome checked in the second, is still to be run.

> **Negative knowledge.** Develop representations for disproven claims, failed experiments, unresolved contradictions, and previously discarded approaches whose constraints may have changed. [LIA §10]

The methods in this chapter register that two terms occur together. The materials authors say so about their own: the effects of negation were somewhat reduced in their study because abstracts tend to emphasize positive relationships, and full texts would contain more negative ones and more complex sentences. [^15] The time-sliced answer key has the same blind side. It lists associations that appeared after the cut-off, and it has no entry for an association that was tried and found false. [^23] A method scored against it earns nothing for knowing that a connection fails.

> **Epistemic infrastructure.** Develop interoperable excavation records, provenance standards, public benchmarks, and mechanisms for correction and replication. [LIA §10]

The reviewers report that literature-based discovery has no comprehensive gold standard datasets and no consistent formal approach to evaluation. [^23] Science4Cast is one public benchmark for one field, and its authors report numerous tests to exclude data leakage, overfitting, and duplication in it. [^36] Chapter IV-04 presented the excavation record of section 8, which is the founding text's proposal for the first item in this priority.

#### The metrics

> Useful metrics include verified novelty relative to a defined record, precision of validated claims, recall against bounded benchmarks, calibration of uncertainty, provenance completeness, reproducibility, and cost per validated result. No single score can adequately represent all of these dimensions. [LIA §10]

The cases show how far apart these quantities lie. The materials experiment defined its record with a list of keywords and counted a material as new only if it had appeared beside none of them, which is novelty relative to a defined record. Its eightfold figure is a ratio against a baseline. It is not a precision of validated claims, because the endpoint was later study and not a measurement.

The ranking also gives an order and no numerical estimate of a material's properties, so it carries no stated uncertainty to calibrate. [^15] The baricitinib trial supplies a validated claim, about recovery time for one regimen, and no rate. [^25]

Recall needs a bounded list of things that were there to be found. Ke and colleagues scored 22 million papers with a coefficient that needs no parameters and found a continuous range of delayed recognition. [^5] A set of papers drawn from the high end of that range, each with the date its attention arrived, is a candidate source for such a list: freeze the record before the rise and ask whether a method points to the paper.

The limit is the one Chapter I-02 stated. Citations are a proxy for attention, not proof of when usefulness or truth became known, and the ledger infers no discovery lag from the study. [^5] A benchmark built this way measures recall of later attention, the same endpoint the materials replay used.

A method can rank well against later attention and yield few validated claims. It can be precise and too costly to check. It can be cheap and leave no trail a second investigator could follow. That is the content of the founding text's last sentence on metrics.

Section 9 of the founding text, after naming the traditions LIA draws on, applies the same standard to the proposal as a whole.

> Whether this synthesis merits recognition as a distinct discipline is a question for the community and for the results it produces. [LIA §9]

The first priority has a worked example with stated limits, the second has a single case, and the others have partial results or none. The Coda takes up section 12 of the founding text, which says who is invited to run those tests and on what terms.

### C. An open invitation

When the Vesuvius Challenge opened its Herculaneum scans in 2023, outside entrants could take up different parts of the reading. The three grand prize winners brought together work that had already earned each of them a prize. They worked from a crackle pattern spotted by another contestant, ink detectors adapted from detached fragments, and software for tracing papyrus through a scan so its surface could be flattened. The tracing software had already earned three prizes. [^4]

The review was arranged the same way. In 2024 the organizers reported that papyrologists had worked to "review 15 columns of text in anonymized submissions," and that a technical team had audited the submitted code. The grand prize was announced that year. About one year separates the challenge data release from the prize announcement, and the age of the ancient writing is not a discovery lag. The winning reading covered passages from a single scroll. [^4]

Chapter I-01 opened on this case and Chapter III-10 returned to it. The coda needs one fact from it. The people who read the record were not the people who released it, and the papyrologists who checked the reading saw the submissions without names attached. [^4]

The founding text closes by asking for the same arrangement around itself.

> LIA is proposed as a shared intellectual project. No particular institution, model provider, or implementation should define its boundaries by default. [LIA §12]

The three things named are the three that could most easily come to own a method like this one: an organization that funds the work, a company whose model does the reading, and a piece of software that happens to be built first. The founding text gives none of them the authority to settle what counts as LIA.

> Researchers, engineers, archivists, historians, scientists, artists, and independent investigators are invited to adopt the term, challenge its assumptions, build tools, publish excavations, establish benchmarks, and extend the canon. [LIA §12]

The sentence names seven kinds of people and six things to do. Challenging the assumptions comes second, directly after adopting the term, and it is part of the invitation. Publishing excavations and establishing benchmarks are how an archaeological candidate comes to be tested by someone other than the person who proposed it. Extending the canon comes last, and the next paragraph of the founding text gives the terms on which that is done.

> The vocabulary is meant to be useful, not proprietary. The founding text is meant to be amended when better ideas or better evidence emerge. Where possible, preserve attribution and a visible history of revisions so that the development of the discipline remains itself an intelligible record. [LIA §12]

The condition for amendment is better ideas or better evidence. The request that goes with it is the one the method makes of every excavation: keep the provenance. A founding text revised with attribution and a visible history of revisions is an informational artifact that a later reader can examine in the way this piece has examined its cases.

> The ambition is not to establish a single system that knows everything. It is to establish a practice through which more of what is already available to humanity becomes intelligible, testable, and useful. [LIA §12]

**The frontier of discovery is not only ahead of us. Some of it is behind us, buried in the record we have already made.**

# Latent Information Archaeology (LIA)

*The systematic excavation of what the world could already know.*

**Founding text · Version 0.1 · November 25, 2026**

## Preamble

Humanity has become extraordinarily good at producing information and comparatively poor at exhausting its implications.

We publish papers, file patents, record observations, document failures, accumulate archives, and generate increasingly vast quantities of data. Each artifact enters a particular context, serves a particular purpose, and is usually interpreted within the boundaries of the discipline, institution, language, or moment that produced it.

The relationships between those artifacts are not necessarily exhausted when their original purpose is fulfilled. An observation made in one field may resolve a problem in another. A failed experiment may contain the evidence needed for a future invention. Two independently established facts may imply a third that nobody has articulated.

There is an asymmetry between what humanity has recorded and what humanity has recognized.

**Latent Information Archaeology (LIA) is the proposed discipline of investigating that asymmetry.**

Its premise is not that all future discoveries have already been made. It is that some discoveries can be made from information already in existence, and that the systematic exploration of this possibility deserves to be a discipline in its own right.

The name is an invitation to develop that discipline collectively.

## 1. Canonical definition

**Latent Information Archaeology is the systematic identification, reconstruction, and validation of previously unrecognized information, relationships, implications, and opportunities latent within existing informational artifacts.**

An informational artifact is any preserved representation of an observation, claim, process, decision, or event: a scientific paper, dataset, patent, photograph, recording, software repository, experimental notebook, model, or other record.

An archaeological discovery is not necessarily new information about the physical world. It may instead be a new understanding of the information already available about that world.

LIA is concerned with what can be recovered, inferred, or reconstructed from the existing record, including what can be identified as a promising candidate for subsequent empirical investigation.

It is a discipline, not a specific algorithm, architecture, product, or model. Artificial intelligence is an important enabling technology, but it is not a prerequisite for doing LIA.

## 2. The central thesis

The informational state of civilization exceeds its articulated understanding of that information.

The gap arises because knowledge production is distributed, specialization is necessary, archives are incomplete, vocabulary changes, incentives favor certain questions, and the possible relationships between artifacts grow faster than our ability to investigate them.

These conditions create an **epistemic backlog**: a collection of recoverable observations, potentially meaningful relationships, unresolved contradictions, and unexamined implications.

This backlog is not a fixed inventory of truths waiting to be retrieved. Its boundaries depend on the accessible corpus, the available methods of inference, the questions being investigated, and the standards of evidence applied. Some candidates will be false, others trivial, and others impossible to resolve without new observations.

LIA exists to distinguish among them.

The economic thesis is that improvements in search, machine reasoning, representation learning, and computational capacity can reduce the cost of investigating this backlog. If true, activities that were previously impractical may become routine. This is a research proposition, not an established universal law.

## 3. What is latent?

Latency is relational. A claim can be explicit in one archive, invisible to another community, and genuinely unarticulated when considered across an entire body of evidence.

LIA recognizes several kinds of latency:

| Class | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Archival** | Information explicitly recorded but subsequently forgotten, inaccessible, or overlooked. |
| **Relational** | Meaningful connections among artifacts that have not been recognized or documented together. |
| **Inferential** | Conclusions or hypotheses obtainable by combining observations, claims, or constraints. |
| **Contextual** | Previously known information whose relevance becomes apparent under different conditions or in a different domain. |
| **Representational** | Structure or associations encoded in a computational representation, such as an embedding or trained model, that require elicitation and independent verification. |

These classes are not exclusive. An excavation may recover an obscure observation, connect it to a separate field, and reveal its usefulness in a new technological context.

A model's ability to articulate a claim does not establish that the claim was encoded as truthful knowledge, nor that the claim is supported by its training data. Representational latency requires particular care because the original evidentiary trail may be inaccessible.

## 4. The epistemic boundary

Let `C(t)` be a defined corpus containing artifacts available at or before time `t`. Let `E(t)` denote the claims explicitly documented within a specified reference record by that time. Let `M` denote a specified set of retrieval, reasoning, and analytical methods.

Define `H(C(t), M)` as the candidate claims recoverable or constructible from the corpus using those methods.

A working discovery space is:

```text
L(C, t, M) = H(C(t), M) \ E(t)
```

The subtraction is semantic rather than literal: paraphrasing an existing claim does not make it novel. This is a conceptual model, not an assertion that the sets are fully enumerable or that membership can always be established.

Three important consequences follow:

- Latency is relative to a corpus, a historical boundary, and a method of investigation.
- A candidate in the discovery space is not necessarily true. The space includes unsupported conjectures and erroneous inferences.
- Novelty is relative to a documented reference record. The absence of an earlier publication cannot prove that no person previously knew a claim.

A separate validation process determines what epistemic status each candidate warrants.

## 5. The distinction between discovery and invention

LIA does not require that discoveries be logically entailed by the historical record. Some are deductive consequences; others are statistical patterns, explanatory hypotheses, analogies, or proposed transfers between domains.

The distinction is the origin of the evidentiary substrate, not whether the investigator employed creativity.

A useful boundary is between an **archaeological candidate**, generated from existing information, and a **validated discovery**, which has survived the appropriate tests. A candidate may require new experiments before it becomes a discovery about the physical world.

In that case, LIA identified the research opportunity; experimental science established its empirical validity.

Neither activity replaces the other.

## 6. Principles

The following principles define the proposed discipline.

1. **Evidence precedes assertion.** A plausible narrative is not a substitute for a traceable evidentiary chain.
2. **Provenance is part of the discovery.** A finding should preserve its source artifacts, dates, transformations, and inferential steps.
3. **Novelty is investigated, not presumed.** Rediscovery is valuable, but must not be misrepresented as original discovery.
4. **The question may itself be the discovery.** Recognizing that a problem can be asked differently is a legitimate outcome.
5. **Contradictions are excavation sites.** Inconsistencies can indicate missing variables, incompatible assumptions, errors, or genuine opportunities for explanation.
6. **Similarity is a lead, not proof.** Semantic proximity, analogy, and correlation may initiate inquiry but cannot independently establish causation or truth.
7. **Time is an evidentiary constraint.** Claims about what could have been discovered earlier must exclude information unavailable at the historical cutoff, including information introduced through model training or retrieval.
8. **Negative results belong in the record.** Failed connections, rejected hypotheses, and uncertainty should be retained when possible so future investigators do not repeat the same excavation.
9. **Methods are replaceable; standards are not.** Human scholarship, statistical analysis, graph reasoning, language models, and future computational systems can all participate.
10. **Knowledge should circulate.** Publish methods, evidence, and findings as openly as possible, consistent with privacy, source rights, safety, and the legitimate interests of affected people.

## 7. The archaeological method

LIA can be practiced as a recurring cycle rather than a one-time search.

```text
Define the excavation boundary
             |
Acquire and preserve artifacts
             |
Map entities, claims, and relationships
             |
Detect gaps, anomalies, and possible connections
             |
Construct candidate discoveries
             |
Challenge evidence, novelty, and inference
             |
Validate or classify the remaining uncertainty
             |
Publish findings and rejected hypotheses
             |
             +----> Repeat with the enriched record
```

The investigator should define the corpus and temporal boundary, retain primary sources, normalize entities without erasing meaningful context, and explore both expected and unexpected relationships.

Candidate discoveries should be subjected to competing explanations, counterexamples, source independence checks, and appropriate empirical or logical tests. Wherever possible, independent investigators should be able to reconstruct the reasoning.

A persistent LIA system could autonomously propose new lines of inquiry, but it must be designed to reject more than it accepts when the evidence warrants it. The objective is not maximal hypothesis production. It is the efficient identification of consequential, defensible findings.

## 8. The excavation record

A finding should be packaged in a form that other investigators can audit, challenge, reproduce, and build upon. The following is a proposed minimal record format, not a finalized technical standard.

```yaml
lia_version: "0.1"
id: "<stable identifier>"
claim: "<precise candidate finding>"
corpus: "<defined source collection>"
cutoff: "<historical cutoff, if applicable>"
latency_class: "<one or more classes>"
sources:
  - reference: "<persistent source identifier>"
    evidence: "<relevant observation or claim>"
    available_at: "<date or documented uncertainty>"
inference: "<reasoning connecting the evidence>"
alternatives: []
contradictory_evidence: []
novelty_check: "<scope, methods, and results>"
status: "candidate"
validation: "<tests and their results>"
limitations: []
next_test: "<what could refute or strengthen the claim>"
```

Suggested statuses are `candidate`, `corroborated`, `validated`, `rediscovered`, `rejected`, and `unresolved`. Novelty and validity are separate axes: a rediscovery can be true, and a novel candidate can be false. A mature standard should record those dimensions independently.

## 9. Intellectual lineage

LIA is a proposed synthesis and extension of existing traditions, not a claim to have invented their constituent methods.

- **Undiscovered public knowledge and literature-based discovery.** Don R. Swanson demonstrated how separately developed bodies of biomedical literature could support a previously unrecognized hypothesis. His 1986 work on fish oil and Raynaud's syndrome is a foundational precedent. [1, 2]
- **Computational extraction of latent scientific knowledge.** Tshitoyan and colleagues demonstrated in 2019 that representations learned from materials science literature could recover domain relationships and retrospectively recommend materials before their later documented investigation. [3]
- **AI-assisted scientific discovery.** Co-Scientist illustrates how multi-agent hypothesis generation and criticism can be integrated into scientific investigation. [4]
- **Archival scholarship, knowledge graphs, abductive reasoning, information retrieval, and exploratory data analysis.** These traditions provide additional methods for the recovery, organization, and interpretation of existing evidence.

The proposed contribution of LIA is to place the informational backlog itself at the center of a general, cross-domain discipline: to investigate its structure, develop methods for its excavation, and establish shared standards for the resulting findings.

Whether this synthesis merits recognition as a distinct discipline is a question for the community and for the results it produces.

## 10. A research agenda

The discipline should be judged by what it can demonstrate, not by the ambition of its premise.

Early priorities include:

- **Historical replay.** Freeze a corpus at an earlier date and measure whether a method recovers discoveries documented afterward. Control for training-data leakage, hindsight in selecting tasks, and selection bias.
- **Prospective excavation.** Publish timestamped candidate discoveries before their outcomes are known, including failed predictions and null results.
- **Comparative performance.** Compare LIA methods with conventional search, literature-based discovery systems, domain experts, and simple statistical baselines under comparable resource constraints.
- **Excavation economics.** Measure cost per defensible finding, reviewer time, reproducibility, and the marginal benefit of additional computation or data.
- **Cross-domain transfer.** Test whether useful principles, technologies, and explanations can be recovered across distinct vocabularies and institutional boundaries.
- **Negative knowledge.** Develop representations for disproven claims, failed experiments, unresolved contradictions, and previously discarded approaches whose constraints may have changed.
- **Epistemic infrastructure.** Develop interoperable excavation records, provenance standards, public benchmarks, and mechanisms for correction and replication.

Useful metrics include verified novelty relative to a defined record, precision of validated claims, recall against bounded benchmarks, calibration of uncertainty, provenance completeness, reproducibility, and cost per validated result. No single score can adequately represent all of these dimensions.

## 11. Why archaeology?

Archaeology is an appropriate metaphor because the investigator encounters a record shaped by time, loss, preservation, selection, and context.

An artifact rarely explains itself. Its position relative to other artifacts matters. An absence may be meaningful, or it may simply reflect what was never recorded or what failed to survive.

The archaeologist cannot legitimately manufacture missing evidence and present it as recovered material. Neither can the information archaeologist.

The work is excavation, reconstruction, interpretation, and verification. It is as much about understanding the limits of the record as it is about discovering what the record contains.

## 12. An open invitation

LIA is proposed as a shared intellectual project. No particular institution, model provider, or implementation should define its boundaries by default.

Researchers, engineers, archivists, historians, scientists, artists, and independent investigators are invited to adopt the term, challenge its assumptions, build tools, publish excavations, establish benchmarks, and extend the canon.

The vocabulary is meant to be useful, not proprietary. The founding text is meant to be amended when better ideas or better evidence emerge. Where possible, preserve attribution and a visible history of revisions so that the development of the discipline remains itself an intelligible record.

The ambition is not to establish a single system that knows everything. It is to establish a practice through which more of what is already available to humanity becomes intelligible, testable, and useful.

**The frontier of discovery is not only ahead of us. Some of it is behind us, buried in the record we have already made.**
